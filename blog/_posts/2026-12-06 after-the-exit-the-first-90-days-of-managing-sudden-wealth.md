# After the Exit: The First 90 Days of Managing Sudden Wealth

**Sudden wealth management** sounds like a problem everyone would love to have, right up until it is yours. The wire lands. The number in your checking account has a comma in a place it has never had one. And the strange truth that every exited founder reports: the dominant feeling is not celebration. It is vertigo. For years, your financial life had one strategy — build the company. Now the company is money, the strategy is gone, and everyone from private bankers to college roommates has an opinion about what you should do next.

This guide is a 90-day operating plan for that specific window. Not an investment strategy — what to buy is a decision for later, and for you. This is about sequence: what to do in the first two weeks, the first month, and the first quarter, in which order, and what to explicitly not do. The stakes are asymmetric. Money deployed slowly and boringly in the first 90 days costs you a little upside. Money deployed quickly and badly costs you the exit.

## Key Takeaways

* The first rule of a liquidity event is that there is no urgency: almost every serious post-exit mistake comes from acting fast, and almost none from parking cash safely for a quarter.
* Days 1–14 are defense: secure the cash across insured and near-cash instruments, tell almost no one, commit to no investments, and start a decision journal.
* Days 15–45 are visibility: build the complete picture of your new balance sheet — every account, entity, asset, and liability — before any deployment conversation.
* Days 45–90 are structure: assemble episodic professional help (CPA first), define what the money is for, and deploy slowly against a written plan.
* The skill that carries you past day 90 is capital efficiency — knowing at all times how much is idle, how it is allocated, and what job each dollar has.

## Day Zero: What Just Happened to Your Balance Sheet

Before the plan, it helps to name the shift precisely. An exit does not just make you richer; it inverts the structure of your net worth overnight.

Pre-exit, a founder's balance sheet is typically 60–90% one illiquid, concentrated position — the company — with a thin liquid layer around it. Every risk conversation was really about one asset. Post-exit, the concentration is gone and so is the excuse: you now hold the most fungible, most easily misallocated asset there is. Cash has no strategy embedded in it. It will become a strategy — deliberately or by drift.

The second shift is psychological. Founders are wired for action; the company rewarded decisiveness for years. Cash sitting "unproductive" feels like a problem demanding immediate solving. It is not. A large cash position after an exit is not idle capital in the pejorative sense — it is capital awaiting a plan, and the plan is worth more than the yield you are forgoing while you make it. The distinction between those two states — parked deliberately versus drifting — is exactly what a capital-efficiency lens is for, and it will matter for the rest of your financial life. ## Days 1–14: Defense

The first two weeks of sudden wealth management have one objective: make sure nothing bad can happen while you are not yet thinking clearly. Everything here is reversible, boring, and fast.

**Park the cash safely and spread it.** Bank deposit insurance has per-institution limits; a wire that lands in one checking account almost certainly blows through them. Spreading across insured accounts and near-cash instruments — money-market funds, Treasury bills — is standard practice, and the mechanics are worth an hour with your banker or CPA. The goal is not return. It is that no single institution failure, fraud incident, or mistake can touch a meaningful share of the proceeds.

**Tell almost no one.** The number of people who know is the single best predictor of inbound pressure — pitches, asks, "opportunities," and awkwardness. Your spouse, your CPA, your attorney. Beyond that circle, silence is not secrecy; it is buying yourself a quiet quarter to think.

**Commit to nothing.** No investments, no angel checks, no real estate, no "small" allocations to a friend's fund. Say this sentence out loud to yourself and then to anyone who pitches you: "I don't make investment decisions until [a date 90 days out]." A blanket rule is easier to hold than case-by-case judgment while your judgment is still recalibrating.

**Start a decision journal.** A plain document: every financial idea, every pitch, every impulse, dated, with a one-line rationale. Two purposes: it gives the action-impulse somewhere to discharge that isn't a wire transfer, and in 90 days it becomes data about your own state of mind — you will be surprised how different week-two ideas look from week-ten.

**Handle the genuinely time-sensitive.** A small list of things that may actually have deadlines, all of them CPA-and-attorney territory: tax withholding and estimated payments on the proceeds, any election windows connected to the transaction, insurance coverage appropriate to your new asset level. Note the pattern — the urgent items are professional-advice items, not investment items. Nothing in the first two weeks requires you to pick an asset.

## Days 15–45: Visibility

With the money safe and the noise fenced out, the first real project of sudden wealth management is not deploying capital. It is seeing it — building a complete, current picture of what you now own. This sounds trivial after the headline event, but post-exit balance sheets are messier than they look: proceeds possibly split across tranches, escrows, or earnouts; old 401(k)s and brokerage accounts that were ignored for a decade while the company consumed everything; real estate, crypto on an exchange and in a wallet or two, an entity or three left over from the journey; liabilities that suddenly deserve a strategy review.

The exercise is the same one behind any serious [net worth calculation](/blog/what-is-my-net-worth-the-founders-guide-to-total-wealth-intelligence): everything counted, every liability included, holdings classified by what they are rather than where they sit, entities kept separate from personal. What changes post-exit is the stakes — every deployment decision for the next decade will be made against this picture, so it needs to be complete and it needs to stay current, which a quarterly spreadsheet will not.

## Days 45–90: Structure and the First Slow Moves

The final phase adds two layers: the right professional help, and a written plan the first deployments can follow.

**Assemble episodic help, CPA first.** The year of an exit is the one year tax planning is unambiguously worth professional attention — proceeds structure, estimated payments, state and residency questions, charitable timing. All of it is CPA territory, none of it is something a platform or an article should decide for you. Beyond the CPA: an attorney for estate documents that probably now have teeth, and possibly an advisor for specific decisions. On the advisor question, the honest framework is the one laid out in [software vs. advisor](/blog/wealth-management-software-vs-advisor-the-2026-founders-framework): buy judgment episodically for irreversible decisions; own the visibility and monitoring layer yourself at flat cost, rather than converting your exit into a permanent percentage-of-assets fee for reporting.

**Write down what the money is for.** One page, before any allocation conversation: how many years of family runway this must protect no matter what; whether it funds the next company, and with how much; what lifestyle change, if any, it pays for; what share you are genuinely willing to risk on illiquid or concentrated bets. Every allocation decision downstream is easier against this page, and every pitch can be tested against it in one sentence.

**Deploy slowly, and simulate before you commit.** There is no prize for finishing your allocation by day 90. Moving in stages over months is a widely used, defensible approach — and each stage is a decision you can model before making. ## The Mistakes That Actually Hurt

Every sudden wealth management cautionary tale traces to a handful of patterns, all avoidable by sequence rather than brilliance.

**Deploying at week-two speed.** The classic: a founder's action bias, applied to a nine-figure decision set, in the exact window when judgment is most distorted. The 90-day commitment device exists because "I'll evaluate each opportunity on its merits" reliably fails in this window.

**Skipping the visibility phase.** Allocating from a mental model of your wealth instead of a complete picture — then discovering the escrow, the old entity, the forgotten account, and the fact that your "diversified" deployment doubled up on exposures you already had.

**Replacing one concentration with another.** Rolling a huge share of proceeds into a single new venture, a single property, or a friend's single fund. Sometimes concentration is a deliberate, eyes-open choice; post-exit, it is more often the action-impulse wearing a strategy costume. The milestone shifts that come with a step-change in net worth all assume you actually de-risked.

**Buying a permanent fee with a temporary feeling.** Signing a full-service, percentage-of-assets arrangement in month one because the vertigo wanted an adult in the room. The vertigo passes; the fee compounds. Hire the episode, not the percentage — and keep the system of record yours.

**Letting the cash drift instead of park.** The opposite failure from deploying too fast: a year later, everything is still in two bank accounts, not by decision but by avoidance. Parked-with-a-plan and drifting look identical from the outside; the one-page purpose document and a capital-efficiency lens are what tell them apart.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What should I do first after selling my company?

Defense before anything else: get the proceeds spread across insured accounts and near-cash instruments so no single institution holds a dangerous share, keep the circle of people who know very small, and commit — explicitly, with a date — to making no investment decisions for roughly 90 days. The only genuinely urgent items in the first weeks are tax-related, and those belong with your CPA, not with anyone selling an investment.

### How long should I wait before investing after an exit?

A commonly used rule of thumb is a deliberate pause of about 90 days, followed by staged deployment over months rather than a single reallocation — though the right pace is personal and worth discussing with your professionals. The reasoning is asymmetry: parking cash safely for a quarter costs a little forgone return, while a large misallocation made during the post-exit adjustment period can cost a meaningful share of the exit itself.

### Where do people typically park proceeds in the short term?

The standard toolbox is boring on purpose: insured deposit accounts across multiple institutions (insurance limits are per institution), money-market funds, and short-term Treasury instruments. The objective in this phase is safety and liquidity, not yield. The mechanics — how to structure it, and the tax treatment of the interim income — are an hour well spent with your banker and CPA.

### Do I need a wealth manager after a liquidity event?

Not automatically. Separate what you would be buying: judgment on specific, irreversible decisions (exit-year tax coordination, estate structure) is worth hiring episodically — fee-only, per engagement. Ongoing reporting, monitoring, allocation tracking, and scenario modeling is work that flat-subscription software now does continuously; on a large exit, a percentage-of-assets fee for that layer compounds into serious money. Many founders run the hybrid: own the visibility layer, hire the episodes.

### How do I avoid lifestyle inflation after sudden wealth?

The mechanism that works is the same one that works for allocation: write it down before the pressure arrives. A one-page document stating what the money is for — years of protected runway, next-venture funding, any deliberate lifestyle upgrades — turns each spending decision from a willpower contest into a check against a plan you wrote when calm. A live view of your burn against that plan closes the loop.

### What does capital efficiency mean for post-exit money?

It is the discipline of knowing that every dollar has an assigned job — runway protection, deliberate parking, deployed allocation — and seeing when reality drifts from the assignment. IOMI formalizes this as the Capital Efficiency Score, a 0–100 read on deployment across five factors: liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration. Post-exit, it is the difference between a cash position you chose and one that chose itself.

### How do I handle the flood of pitches after an exit?

With a rule rather than a case-by-case defense: "I don't make investment decisions until [date]" ends most conversations politely and instantly. Log every pitch in a decision journal instead of evaluating it live — anything genuinely good will still be good in three months, and the journal itself becomes a filter: by day 90 you will see which ideas were opportunities and which were just proximity to your wire transfer.
