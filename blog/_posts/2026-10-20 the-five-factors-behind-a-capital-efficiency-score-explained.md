# The Five Factors Behind a Capital Efficiency Score, Explained

A net worth number tells you how much you have. It says nothing about how well that money is positioned. Two founders can each hold $3 million and be in completely different financial shape: one with a funded cash buffer, diversified holdings, and every dollar assigned a job; the other with 70% of their wealth in a single stock and six figures sleeping in a checking account. Understanding the **capital efficiency score factors** — liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration — is how you tell the difference.

The Capital Efficiency Score (CES) is a 0–100 health check of how well your capital is deployed. It is not a credit score, and it has nothing to do with borrowing. It measures one thing: whether your money is working. This article walks through each of the five factors, what a weak reading looks like in real life, and what typically moves the number.

## Key Takeaways

* The Capital Efficiency Score is a 0–100 measure of how well capital is deployed, scored on five factors: liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration.
* Liquidity asks whether you can cover near-term needs without selling long-term assets at a bad time.
* Idle capital and drag are the two silent factors — cash doing nothing, and holdings quietly underperforming or costing you.
* Risk balance and concentration measure exposure: how your portfolio is spread across asset classes, and how much depends on a single position.
* A CES is only as good as the data behind it — it should be computed from real, connected accounts, not manual estimates.

## What a Capital Efficiency Score Actually Measures

Before breaking down the factors, it helps to be precise about what the score is and is not.

A CES is a diagnostic. It takes your full financial picture — bank accounts, brokerage, retirement, crypto, real estate, private equity, physical assets, liabilities — and grades how that capital is arranged on a 0–100 scale. A higher score means your money is well-positioned: enough liquidity, little waste, sensible spread. A lower score means something specific is off, and the factor breakdown tells you exactly what.

It is not a credit score. Credit scores measure your reliability as a borrower, for the benefit of lenders. A capital efficiency score measures the arrangement of your own assets, for your benefit. Nobody else sees it, and it has no bearing on whether you can get a loan.

It is also not a performance number. Returns tell you what happened. Efficiency tells you whether your current setup makes sense going forward. You can have a great year in the market and still have a mediocre CES because your winnings are now dangerously concentrated.

## Factor 1: Liquidity — Can You Move When You Need To?

Liquidity measures how much of your wealth you can access on short notice without selling something at a bad time.

For a salaried employee, the classic advice is three to six months of expenses in cash. For founders, the question is harder. Your income is lumpy. Your biggest asset is probably illiquid company equity. A capital call, a bridge round you want to join, a tax bill, or three slow months of revenue can all land in the same quarter.

The liquidity factor looks at your accessible assets — cash, near-cash, and holdings you could sell within days without material penalty — against your obligations and realistic near-term needs. Weak liquidity shows up in two mirror-image ways:

### Too little liquidity

Everything is deployed. Your net worth looks strong, but a $60,000 surprise would force you to sell equities in a drawdown or borrow at whatever rate the moment offers. Founders with most of their wealth in private company stock live here by default, often without realizing it.

### Too much liquidity

The opposite failure is over-insurance: keeping two years of expenses in a checking account "just in case". That excess belongs to the next factor.

A good score on liquidity means your buffer matches your actual exposure — not a generic rule of thumb, but your real burn, your real obligations, and the real lumpiness of your income.

## Factor 2: Idle Capital — Money With No Job

Idle capital is cash (or cash-like holdings) sitting beyond any reasonable buffer, earning little or nothing, with no assigned purpose.

This is the most common weakness among entrepreneurs, and the most understandable. When you sell a company, take a distribution, or close a strong quarter, cash lands in an operating or personal checking account. You are busy. It stays there. Months pass. The money is "safe", but it is doing nothing — and in any inflationary environment, doing nothing means slowly losing purchasing power.

The idle capital factor separates cash with a job from cash without one:

* **Cash with a job**: your emergency buffer, a tax reserve, a planned purchase within the next few months, payroll float in a business account. This cash is supposed to be liquid and boring.
* **Cash without a job**: everything beyond that. The distribution that landed eight months ago. The savings account that grew past any purpose you can name.

The distinction matters because the fix is not "invest everything". The fix is naming a job for every dollar. Some jobs are defensive (buffer, reserve). Some are offensive (deploy into the portfolio, fund the next venture). A dollar with no job is the only true waste.

The factor is measured across every connected account — which is why a business checking account with a forgotten surplus counts just as much as a personal one. ## Factor 3: Drag — The Quiet Cost of Suboptimal Holdings

Drag is the ongoing cost of holdings that underperform their purpose. Where idle capital is money doing nothing, drag is money doing something badly.

Common sources of drag:

### Low-yield placements

Cash that does have a job — a buffer, a reserve — but sits in an account earning a fraction of what an equivalent-safety alternative pays. The buffer is right; the placement is wrong.

### Fee-heavy holdings

Funds or products whose expense ratios materially exceed those of comparable alternatives. A high-fee fund tracking the same index as a cheap one is pure drag: identical exposure, worse outcome, forever.

### Debt that outruns your assets

Carrying high-interest debt while holding low-yield assets is negative arbitrage against yourself. Paying 21% on a card balance while cash earns 4% is an 17-point spread you are funding out of pocket.

### Redundant or forgotten positions

Overlapping funds, dust positions from old experiments, subscriptions and account fees on accounts you no longer use.

Drag is insidious because each individual item looks small. Half a percent here, a forgotten fee there. Compounded over a decade across a seven-figure portfolio, drag is often the difference between a good outcome and a great one. The drag factor exists to surface these leaks in one place, because nobody finds them by scanning twelve account statements.

## Factor 4: Risk Balance — Is the Spread Sensible?

Risk balance measures how your wealth is distributed across asset classes relative to a sensible spread — not a prescribed model portfolio, but a check against obvious lopsidedness.

The factor looks at your allocation across the major classes: cash, public equities, retirement accounts, crypto, real estate, private equity, physical assets, and liabilities against all of it. It then asks structural questions:

* Is nearly everything in one asset class?
* Is the volatile portion of the portfolio (crypto, single stocks, early-stage equity) large relative to the stable portion?
* Are liabilities concentrated against a single asset, so one price drop impairs your whole balance sheet?

One detail matters more than it looks: holdings are classified by what they are, not where they sit. Bitcoin held at a brokerage is crypto, not "brokerage assets". A REIT is real estate exposure. Without this discipline, a portfolio can look diversified on paper — four institutions! — while actually being one big bet wearing four logos.

Risk balance is deliberately separate from the next factor. A portfolio can be reasonably spread across classes and still be dangerously exposed to one position inside a class. That is concentration's job to catch.

## Factor 5: Concentration — How Much Rides on One Thing?

Concentration measures dependence on a single position, entity, or exposure. It is the factor most relevant to founders, because founders are structurally concentrated: the company usually is the balance sheet.

Typical concentration profiles:

* **The founder**: 60–90% of net worth in one private company's equity. Illiquid, unhedgeable, and correlated with their income — the same event that hits the equity hits the salary.
* **The post-exit operator**: sold the company, took stock in the acquirer, never diversified. Same concentration, new ticker.
* **The employee-turned-wealthy**: RSUs vested for a decade and never sold. A tech portfolio that is actually one company in a trench coat.
* **The crypto-native**: high conviction in one or two tokens representing most of liquid net worth.

The concentration factor does not say concentration is wrong. Concentration built the wealth — nobody diversifies their way to a founder-sized outcome. But there is a difference between chosen concentration and unexamined concentration. The factor exists to keep the number in front of you, so that staying concentrated is a decision you make on purpose, with current data, rather than a default you inherited from your own success.

This is also where a score beats a dashboard. A pie chart shows you the slice. A scored factor tells you the slice grew from 55% to 68% since spring because the position ran up, and that your overall efficiency reading moved because of it.

## How the Five Factors Combine into One Score

The five capital efficiency score factors are scored together into the single 0–100 CES. The value of combining them is not the arithmetic — it is the conversation between the factors.

The factors check and balance each other:

* Fixing idle capital by dumping all spare cash into your largest position improves one factor and worsens concentration.
* Maximizing liquidity by holding everything in cash sends idle capital and drag the wrong way.
* Chasing yield on your buffer can fix drag while quietly breaking liquidity.

A single combined score forces trade-offs into the open. You cannot game one factor without the others registering the cost. That is what makes the CES a deployment measure rather than a vanity metric: it goes up when the whole arrangement improves, not when one number gets cosmetically better.

## Reading Your Score: What the Factors Tell You to Do Next

A score is only useful if it changes behavior. Here is how to read a factor breakdown in practice.

**Start with the weakest factor, not the overall number.** A 68 with balanced factors and a 68 dragged down by one terrible factor are different situations. The weak factor is the to-do list.

**Weak liquidity** → build the buffer before anything else. Everything else is optimization; this is survival.

**Weak idle capital** → name a job for every unassigned dollar. Buffer, reserve, deploy, or spend — but decide.

**Weak drag** → hunt the leaks: placement of reserves, fee-heavy funds, expensive debt against cheap assets.

**Weak risk balance** → look at the class-level spread and ask whether the lopsidedness is intentional.

**Weak concentration** → you don't have to sell anything. You have to decide, consciously and with current numbers, that the exposure is one you want.

Then re-check after you act. Because the score is computed from live account data, a reallocation shows up in the factors on the next sync — which closes the loop between deciding and verifying in a way no annual review ever will.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the five capital efficiency score factors?

The five factors are liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration. Liquidity measures access to funds without forced selling; idle capital measures cash with no assigned purpose; drag measures the ongoing cost of underperforming or fee-heavy holdings; risk balance measures the spread across asset classes; concentration measures dependence on a single position or entity. Together they produce a single 0–100 score.

### Is a capital efficiency score the same as a credit score?

No. A credit score measures your reliability as a borrower and is used by lenders. A capital efficiency score measures how well your own capital is deployed, for your own use. It has no effect on borrowing, is not shared with any institution, and is scored on completely different inputs — your actual holdings rather than your repayment history.

### What is a good capital efficiency score?

Higher is better on the 0–100 scale, but the factor breakdown matters more than the headline number. A mid-range score with one clearly weak factor gives you a specific action; a high score with a slowly deteriorating concentration factor is a warning worth more than the total. Treat the score as a trend to improve, not a grade to pass.

### Which factor do founders usually score worst on?

Concentration, almost by definition — a founder's largest asset is usually their own company's equity. Idle capital is the most common second weakness, because irregular income means cash arrives in lumps and often sits unassigned. Neither is a moral failing; the point of scoring them is to make the trade-offs visible and deliberate.

### Does fixing one factor hurt the others?

It can, and that is by design. Deploying all idle cash into your biggest position fixes idle capital but worsens concentration; holding everything in cash fixes liquidity but creates idle capital and drag. Because all five factors feed one score, the CES only improves when a change genuinely improves the overall arrangement.

### How often should I check my capital efficiency score?

The score updates as your connected accounts sync, so there is no manual refresh to schedule. In practice, a glance when something material changes — a distribution lands, a position runs up, a liability is paid down — and a deliberate factor-by-factor review once a quarter is enough for most people.

### Can a capital efficiency score work with manually tracked assets?

Connected accounts give the most reliable inputs, since balances refresh on every sync. Assets that cannot be connected — private equity, some physical assets — are included at the values you maintain for them. The more of your balance sheet flows in automatically, the more trustworthy the factor readings become.
