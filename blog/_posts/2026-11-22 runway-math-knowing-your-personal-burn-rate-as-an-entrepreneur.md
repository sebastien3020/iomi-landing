# Runway Math: Knowing Your Personal Burn Rate as an Entrepreneur

Ask a founder their company's burn rate and you get an answer to the dollar, unprompted, with a trend line. Ask the same founder their **personal burn rate** and you usually get a pause, then a guess, then "probably around..." followed by a number that is wrong by 30–50%. It is one of the stranger asymmetries in entrepreneurship: the discipline founders apply obsessively to company cash almost never crosses over to their own accounts.

The asymmetry matters because personal runway is a strategic variable, not a lifestyle detail. It determines how long you can pay yourself nothing, how aggressive you can afford to be in a negotiation, whether a downturn forces a bad decision, and how much risk your family is actually carrying while you build. This guide applies runway math you already know to the balance sheet you've been ignoring: how to calculate your personal burn rate from real data, how to compute runway honestly, what the number should trigger, and how to keep it current without turning your life into a budgeting exercise.

## Key Takeaways

* Personal burn rate is your all-in monthly personal spending — computed from actual transactions over a trailing window, not estimated from memory, because memory-based estimates run 30–50% low.
* Personal runway = liquid personal assets ÷ personal burn. Only genuinely liquid assets count: cash, brokerage. Not retirement accounts, not home equity, not your company stake.
* For founders, personal runway is negotiating power: it is the number that decides whether you can say no to bad terms, survive a pay pause, or outlast a slow quarter.
* Irregular founder income makes burn *feel* untrackable, but burn is about outflows — spending is usually far more stable than income, which is exactly why it is measurable.
* This is monitoring, not budgeting: you need the number and its trend, not 40 spending categories and a monthly guilt ritual.

## The Number You Track for the Company but Not for Yourself

Company burn discipline exists because investors demand it and because the consequence of ignorance — running out of money — is vivid. Personal burn has the same failure mode with a longer fuse and no board asking about it.

Here is what running personal-burn-blind actually costs founders in practice:

**It corrupts company decisions.** A founder who doesn't know their personal runway negotiates like someone with two months of cash even when they have twenty — or worse, the reverse. Salary decisions, fundraise timing, whether to take that acqui-hire call seriously: all of these are quietly parameterized by "how long can I personally last," and most founders are running that calculation on a felt sense rather than a number.

**It hides the real risk transfer.** Every month you underpay yourself to extend company runway, you are transferring runway from your family to your company. That can be exactly the right move — but only if you can see both numbers. The company's runway is on a dashboard; if yours isn't, the transfer is invisible and unbounded.

**It compounds quietly.** Personal burn creeps the same way cloud spend creeps: no single decision, just accumulation. Without measurement, the creep is discovered late — usually at the moment liquidity is tightest.

The fix is not discipline. It is the same fix that works for company cash: measurement from source data, visible continuously, with a trend.

## Calculating Your Personal Burn Rate Honestly

The definition is simple: total personal cash outflow per month. The honesty is in the details.

**Use real transactions, not recall.** Pull actual outflows from every personal account — checking, credit cards, anything money leaves from. Memory-based estimates systematically miss annual charges, subscriptions, and irregular spending. This is precisely what read-only account aggregation is for: IOMI connects to 12,000+ institutions through Plaid, sees balances and transactions (and can never move money), so the outflow number comes from data rather than optimism.

**Use a trailing window, not last month.** Any single month is unrepresentative — insurance renewals, travel, holidays. A 3-month trailing average smooths noise; a 12-month view catches annual charges. Compute both: the 3-month number is your current burn, the 12-month number is your honest burn. If they differ a lot, the gap itself is information about seasonality or creep.

**Amortize the lumpy items.** Annual insurance, property taxes, tuition — divide by twelve and include them. The single most common cause of "my burn is $9k but somehow cash keeps dropping" is unamortized annual spending.

**Exclude transfers, include everything else.** Moving money to your brokerage is not burn; it is allocation. But taxes, debt service, and supporting family members are absolutely burn. The test: would this outflow continue if you stopped investing? If yes, it's burn.

**Keep the wall between personal and business.** Business expenses belong to the entity; your salary or draws are the *income* side of your personal picture, not part of the burn calculation. If your accounts blend the two, fixing that comes first — the compartment discipline in [tracking personal vs business assets](/blog/tracking-personal-vs-business-assets-the-founders-guide-to-total-oversight) is a prerequisite for either number being true.

Run this once and most founders find a number meaningfully higher than their guess. That gap between assumed and actual burn is the first payoff of the exercise.

## Runway Math: What Actually Counts as Liquid

Runway = liquid assets ÷ monthly burn. The burn side is now handled; the discipline moves to the numerator, because founders systematically overcount what is liquid.

**Counts:** cash and cash equivalents across all personal accounts; taxable brokerage holdings you would actually be willing to sell (marked with the understanding that selling has tax consequences — the specifics being CPA territory); crypto on exchanges or in wallets to the extent you'd genuinely liquidate it.

**Does not count:** your company equity — it is probably your largest asset and it is not spendable, which is the entire structural problem of the founder balance sheet, covered in depth in the [liquid vs illiquid net worth breakdown](/blog/what-is-my-net-worth-the-founders-guide-to-total-wealth-intelligence). Retirement accounts — real wealth, wrong bucket; early access is penalized and taxed, and planning to raid them is not a plan. Home equity — you live there; extracting it is slow, costly, and usually unavailable exactly when you need it. Money inside the business — it belongs to the entity and its creditors, not to your kitchen table.

Divide honestly and you get a number in months. A useful founder heuristic for reading it:

* **Under 6 months:** you are negotiating everything from weakness, whether you feel it or not. Extending this number is worth real sacrifice.
* **6–18 months:** functional, but decisions like pay cuts and bridge timing need to be made against the number, not near it.
* **18+ months:** personal runway stops constraining company strategy — you can take the longer fundraise, refuse the bad terms, survive the pivot.

These bands are heuristics, not advice; where you draw your own lines depends on dependents, obligations, and appetite. The point is to *have* lines and know which side you're on.

## Why Founder Income Makes This Feel Impossible (and Why It Isn't)

The standard objection: "my income is too irregular to track this." Notice what the objection assumes — that burn tracking requires income tracking. It doesn't. Burn is about outflows, and spending is usually far more stable than founder income. The lumpy side of your finances is money coming in; the measurable side is money going out. Irregular income makes burn tracking *more* valuable, not less possible, because when income is unpredictable, runway is the only stability metric you have.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a personal burn rate?

It is your total monthly personal cash outflow — everything you spend to run your life, including amortized annual costs like insurance and taxes, excluding transfers to your own investment accounts. For founders it is the personal mirror of the company burn rate they already track, and it is the denominator of personal runway.

### How do I calculate my personal burn rate?

Pull actual outflows from every personal account over a trailing window — 3 months for your current burn, 12 months to capture annual charges — and average per month. Exclude transfers to savings and investments; include debt service and taxes. Use real transaction data rather than memory: estimated burn is typically 30–50% below actual because subscriptions, annual charges, and irregular spending don't surface in recall.

### What counts as liquid assets for personal runway?

Cash across personal accounts, taxable brokerage holdings you would genuinely sell, and crypto you would genuinely liquidate. Excluded: company equity (illiquid), retirement accounts (penalized and taxed before retirement age — and consult your CPA before treating them as anything else), home equity (slow and costly to access), and any cash inside your business (it belongs to the entity). Runway is liquid assets divided by monthly burn.

### How many months of personal runway should a founder have?

There is no universal number, but the bands most founders find useful: under 6 months means personal finances are constraining company decisions whether you notice or not; 6–18 months is workable with attention; 18+ months means personal runway stops dictating strategy. Dependents, fixed obligations, and how volatile your income is all shift where your own threshold belongs.

### How is personal burn rate different from a budget?

A budget is prescriptive: category limits you try to hit, reconciled monthly. Burn rate is descriptive: what you actually spend, measured continuously, watched as a trend. Founders mostly need the second — the number and its direction — not the category ritual. If detailed household budgeting is the actual goal, a dedicated budgeting app like Monarch is the better tool for that job; IOMI's focus is the wealth and runway layer.

### How do I track burn rate with irregular founder income?

Separate the two sides. Burn is outflows, which stay relatively stable regardless of income — so measure it normally from transaction data. Income is the irregular side, so model it as explicit planned events (a distribution in March, a salary change in June) rather than a fake monthly average. Forecasting from planned events is how irregular-income founders get a usable forward view of runway.

### Does my company's runway affect my personal runway?

They are separate numbers that interact through one channel: your compensation. Every month of reduced or skipped salary extends company runway by transferring from your personal runway. That trade can be right or wrong, but it should be a visible decision — which requires both numbers on screen at once, each computed from its own compartment.
