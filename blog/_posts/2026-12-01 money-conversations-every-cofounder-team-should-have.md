# Money Conversations Every Cofounder Team Should Have

Cofounder breakups rarely start with a disagreement about product. They start with money — and specifically with **cofounder finances** that were never discussed until the moment they became a conflict. One founder has eighteen months of personal runway and wants to keep salaries at zero; the other has four months and a mortgage. One would sell the company at $5M tomorrow; the other considers anything under $50M a failure. Neither said any of this out loud at the start, because talking about personal money feels invasive, and because in month one everyone assumes alignment that was never actually checked.

This article is the checklist of conversations to have deliberately — ideally early, but the second-best time is now. Seven topics, the specific questions inside each, and the principle that makes all of them easier: you cannot align on decisions that depend on personal financial positions while keeping those positions completely opaque to each other. You don't need to open your bank statements to your cofounder. You do need to share the handful of numbers that drive your behavior.

## Key Takeaways

* Most cofounder money conflicts are misalignments that existed from day one and surfaced late — the conversations below are how you surface them early, when they're cheap.
* Personal runway is the number that silently drives cofounder behavior: risk tolerance, salary needs, and exit appetite all trace back to how long each founder can personally last.
* Salary, equity, and vesting deserve explicit conversations even when the answers seem obvious — "we'll figure it out" is a decision to have the fight later, with higher stakes.
* Exit expectations differ more than teams assume: a life-changing number for one founder is a disappointing one for another, and the difference shapes every strategic choice.
* Each founder keeping their own finances genuinely in order — visible runway, separated personal and business, honest net worth — is a precondition for honest team conversations.

## Why Cofounder Teams Avoid These Conversations

The avoidance is understandable. Early-stage cofounder relationships run on enthusiasm and mutual belief, and money conversations feel like introducing suspicion into a partnership that runs on trust. Asking "how much personal runway do you have?" can sound like "are you going to bail?" Asking "what number would you sell at?" can sound like "are you not ambitious enough?"

But the questions don't create the differences — they reveal differences that already exist. Two people almost never arrive at a company with the same savings, the same obligations, the same risk tolerance, and the same definition of enough. Those differences will express themselves eventually, through behavior: one founder pushing for revenue now while the other wants to build; one voting to take a mediocre acquisition offer the other finds insulting. By then, each founder reads the other's behavior as a character flaw instead of what it usually is — a rational response to a financial position the other never knew about.

The teams that handle this well treat financial alignment like any other operating discipline: explicit, recurring, unemotional. Here are the seven conversations.

## Conversation One: Personal Runway

The single most predictive number in cofounder dynamics is how long each founder can personally survive without meaningful income. It determines who can afford patience, who needs salary soon, whose stress will spike first, and whose risk calculus will quietly shift when their buffer runs low.

The conversation doesn't require full disclosure of net worth. It requires each founder to know their own number honestly — months of true personal burn covered by liquid personal reserves — and to share it at least in broad strokes: "I'm good for about eighteen months at current burn," "I have six, and a family." Knowing your number honestly is its own discipline; the method — real, unsubsidized burn against genuinely personal reserves — is laid out in [Emergency Funds for Founders](/blog/emergency-funds-for-founders-how-many-months-is-enough).

Then revisit it. Runway isn't static, and the founder whose personal buffer just dropped below four months is operating under pressure the team should know about before it shows up as strange decisions.

## Conversation Two: Salaries — When, How Much, and Equal or Not

"We'll pay ourselves when we can" is not a plan; it's a deferred fight. The explicit version answers three questions. What triggers salaries — a revenue threshold, a funding event, a date? How much — market rate, survival rate, something in between? And are they equal — which feels obviously fair until one founder's survival number is three times the other's because of family obligations or location?

There's no universally right answer, and unequal salaries with equal equity is a legitimate configuration many teams land on. What's not legitimate is discovering mid-crisis that your cofounder assumed a different arrangement than you did. Put the trigger and the numbers in writing. And note that how founder compensation is structured — salary versus distributions, and the tax treatment of each — depends on your entity type and belongs in a conversation with your CPA; the team conversation is about the amounts and the fairness logic, and about the discipline of paying deliberately rather than through ad-hoc transfers, which corrodes both the company's books and each founder's [personal-business boundary](/blog/separating-personal-and-business-finances-the-founders-system).

## Conversation Three: Equity and Vesting — Including the Uncomfortable Scenarios

Most teams manage to discuss the initial split. Fewer discuss the scenarios that actually generate conflict: What happens if one of you leaves in year two — voluntarily, or asked? What if one founder's life circumstances force them to part-time? What does vesting look like, and is there acceleration on an acquisition? Who decides, and how, if you disagree about bringing in a third senior person with meaningful equity?

Vesting schedules exist precisely because these scenarios are common, and agreeing on them while you like each other is incomparably easier than negotiating them when one of you is halfway out the door. The mechanics — cliffs, schedules, repurchase rights — are your startup attorney's territory. The team's job is agreeing on the principles before asking counsel to encode them.

## Conversation Four: The Exit Number

Ask each founder to answer privately, then compare: *At what acquisition price would you vote to sell tomorrow? What's your walk-away-happy number, personally?*

The answers diverge more than almost any team expects, because the same exit produces wildly different personal outcomes. A $6M acquisition might hand one founder — with modest savings and a 40% stake — a transformed life, while barely moving a wealthier cofounder's position. Neither founder is wrong. But if they don't know this about each other, every strategic decision — raise or stay lean, grow or sell, take the meeting or decline it — is being made by people optimizing for different destinations while assuming they share one.

This conversation also surfaces the deeper split between founders who are building to sell and founders building to run something for decades. Both are valid. A team that contains both and doesn't know it is a slow-motion conflict.

## Conversation Five: Who Funds the Company, and On What Terms

Early companies eat cash, and it often comes from the founders unevenly. The conversation: if we put personal money in, is it a loan, a capital contribution, or additional equity? What happens if one founder can contribute and the other can't — does the split shift? Where's each founder's ceiling, the amount past which they will not go deeper regardless of how promising things look?

The ceiling question is the important one, because it's really a question about concentration. A founder pouring personal savings into a company they already spend all their time on is stacking their income, their assets, and their attention into a single point of failure — the structural problem examined in [Concentration Risk for Founders](/blog/concentration-risk-for-founders-when-your-company-is-80-of-your-net-worth). Teams should know each other's ceilings before anyone approaches one, and any founder-to-company money should be papered properly — terms in writing, with the entity treatment confirmed by your CPA.

## Conversation Six: Lifestyle, Obligations, and What the Company Must Provide

This is the conversation teams skip because it feels most personal: what does each founder's life actually require the company to eventually provide? A founder with no dependents and low burn can ride a zero-salary experiment for years. A founder with kids, a mortgage, and a partner counting on income cannot — and shouldn't pretend otherwise in a fit of startup machismo.

Surfacing obligations isn't weakness; it's operational data. It explains salary needs (conversation two), colors the exit number (conversation four), and predicts who will feel pressure first in a downturn. It also changes over time — new children, new houses, a partner's job change — which is why this belongs in the recurring review rather than a one-time onboarding chat.

## Conversation Seven: The Recurring Review

One conversation nobody has in month one stays aligned by itself. The durable version is a lightweight recurring review — quarterly is a common cadence — where the team re-checks the numbers that drive behavior: each founder's rough personal runway, any change in obligations, the salary plan against its trigger, and whether anyone's exit thinking has moved.

This works dramatically better when each founder actually knows their own numbers, which is less common than it sounds. A founder guessing at their runway brings noise to the review; a founder who can see it brings data. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should cofounders share their personal finances with each other?

Not fully — but each should share the numbers that drive behavior: rough personal runway, salary needs, funding ceiling, and exit expectations. Full bank-statement transparency is unnecessary and most teams would find it invasive. Opacity about the behavior-driving numbers, though, means every strategic disagreement gets misread as a values conflict when it's usually a financial-position conflict.

### When should cofounder money conversations happen?

Ideally before incorporation, alongside the equity split — that's when the stakes are lowest and honesty is cheapest. Realistically, the second-best time is now, framed as an operating review rather than an accusation. Then recurring: quarterly re-checks of runway, obligations, salary triggers, and exit thinking keep alignment current instead of historical.

### How should cofounders handle unequal financial situations?

First by making them visible — unequal situations are nearly universal and only dangerous when hidden. Then by designing around them explicitly: salary arrangements that reflect different survival needs (equal equity with unequal salary is a legitimate pattern), funding contributions papered as loans or contributions with clear terms, and mutual awareness of whose pressure will spike first in a lean stretch.

### What is the most important financial number for a cofounder to know?

Their own personal runway: months of true, unsubsidized household burn covered by genuinely personal liquid reserves. It predicts risk tolerance, salary urgency, and exit appetite better than any stated intention. Most founders who haven't measured it carefully overestimate it — business-subsidized costs and reserves sitting inside the company inflate the guess.

### Should cofounders use a shared financial dashboard?

The company's finances, yes — that's ordinary operating transparency. Personal finances, no: the workable pattern is each founder maintaining their own private consolidated view (personal accounts, their equity stake, their real runway) and bringing summary numbers to the team review. IOMI supports exactly that per-founder setup: read-only account connections, personal and business entities in separate compartments, and a consolidated view each founder owns privately.

### How do exit expectations cause cofounder conflict?

Because identical exits produce different personal outcomes. A founder with modest savings may find $5M life-changing while a wealthier cofounder sees it as premature — so one votes to sell and the other feels betrayed, each reading the other's position as unreasonable. Comparing walk-away numbers early converts this from a latent betrayal into a known design constraint the team can plan around.

### Can software resolve cofounder financial disagreements?

It can't resolve values differences — no tool decides whether to sell the company. What it does is remove the factual fog: each founder seeing their real runway, real concentration, and deterministically simulated outcomes ("what does this offer leave me, actually?") means the team argues about genuine preferences instead of misestimated numbers. In practice a meaningful share of cofounder money conflict is misestimation, not misalignment.
