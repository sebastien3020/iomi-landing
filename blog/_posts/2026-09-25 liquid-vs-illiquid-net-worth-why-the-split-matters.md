# Liquid vs Illiquid Net Worth: Why the Split Matters

Two founders can have identical net worth and completely different financial lives. One holds $3 million: $2.8 million in private company equity, $200,000 in everything else. The other holds $3 million: half in liquid investments and cash, half in the company. Same headline number. Radically different flexibility, risk exposure, and set of available moves. The difference between them is captured by one metric the headline hides: **liquid net worth** — the portion of your wealth that could become spendable cash quickly and at close to its stated value.

For founders especially, the liquid/illiquid split is more decision-relevant than total net worth itself. It determines how long you can survive a rough patch, whether you can seize an opportunity this quarter, and how much of your wealth is actually under your control today versus contingent on a future event. This article defines the split precisely, shows how to compute it honestly, and covers the practical ratios and mistakes that matter.

## Key Takeaways

- Liquid net worth is the value you could convert to cash quickly without steep discounts — cash, public securities, and most crypto; total net worth adds illiquid holdings like company equity and real estate.
- Liquidity is a spectrum, not a binary: assets range from same-day (cash) to years-long (private equity), and honest tracking respects the gradations.
- Founders routinely overestimate their flexibility because company equity dominates the headline number while contributing nothing spendable.
- Illiquid values deserve a discount lens: what a private stake or property nets after time, costs, and negotiation is less than its stated mark.
- The split should be visible continuously, not computed annually — it shifts with markets, valuations, and every liquidity event.

## Defining the Split: What Counts as Liquid

Liquid net worth is the portion of your wealth that can become spendable cash quickly — days, not quarters — without losing a large slice of its value in the conversion. The definition has two teeth: speed and value retention. An asset that sells fast at a 40% discount is not liquid in any useful sense; an asset that fetches full value in eighteen months is not either.

Applying the teeth to a founder's typical holdings:

Clearly liquid: cash and bank balances, money-market holdings, publicly traded stocks and bonds and funds in taxable brokerage accounts. Major crypto on exchanges or in wallets generally qualifies too — it can usually be sold quickly — though its volatility means the value realized depends heavily on when you sell.

Conditionally liquid: retirement accounts hold liquid assets but sit behind age-based access rules; converting early carries penalties and taxes that violate the value-retention test. Vested public-company RSUs are liquid once tradable windows allow. Count these in a middle bucket, or count them out of the strict number.

Clearly illiquid: private company equity — your own or angel positions — which cannot be sold quickly, at all, or without approvals. Real estate, which transacts in months with meaningful costs. Physical assets and collectibles: watches, cars, art, which need a buyer found and a price negotiated. Business assets locked inside operating entities.

Liabilities subtract from both sides appropriately: near-term obligations reduce liquid net worth; long-term debt against illiquid assets (a mortgage) nets against the illiquid side. What remains after honest sorting is two numbers — and for most founders, the gap between them is enormous.

## Why the Headline Number Misleads

Total net worth is the number people track, quote, and feel. It is also, for founders, the number most likely to mislead — because it aggregates dollars that behave nothing alike.

A dollar of cash and a dollar of private-company mark both add one to the headline. But the cash dollar can pay a bill tomorrow; the equity dollar is a claim on a future event that may be years away and may reprice before it arrives. Adding them produces a figure that measures neither spending power nor risk. It measures accounting.

The distortion has practical bite. Founders make lifestyle and commitment decisions calibrated to the headline — the house, the burn rate, the personal guarantees — while their actual shock-absorbing capacity is the liquid slice. When a downturn or a slow quarter arrives, the illiquid 85% cannot help; the liquid 15% must carry everything. Wealth that looks abundant in total can be brittle in structure, and this is the mechanism: obligations scale to total net worth while resilience scales to liquid net worth.

The inverse mistake exists too. Some founders treat the illiquid mark as fake and manage as if only cash were real — underinvesting, over-hoarding, and carrying [idle cash](/blog/idle-cash-how-much-is-too-much) far beyond any reserve logic. The mark is not fake; it is real value with a time lock and a variance band. The point of the split is not to dismiss either side but to see both accurately: what you can use now, and what you own that is still becoming usable.

This is also why concentration and liquidity are entangled. When one illiquid position dominates the balance sheet, the liquid share is small almost by definition — the two problems compound, and [concentration risk](/blog/concentration-risk-for-founders-when-your-company-is-80-of-your-net-worth) deserves its own analysis on top of the split.

Classification integrity matters here as much as coverage: holdings counted by what they are, not where they sit. Crypto at a broker is crypto — liquid-ish, volatile. Fiat on an exchange is cash — liquid. Misclassification quietly moves value across the liquid/illiquid line.

### Apply a Discount Lens to the Illiquid Side

Stated values on illiquid assets are optimistic for cash-conversion purposes. A property marked at $900,000 nets meaningfully less after transaction costs and time. A private stake marked at the last round faces approvals and negotiation if it can trade at all. You do not need false precision — a simple haircut convention (for instance, valuing real estate at mark minus expected selling costs, and treating private equity as zero for near-term liquidity purposes) gives you a conservative "accessible value" number alongside the official mark. The gap between the two is itself informative.

## What Ratio Should You Aim For?

There is no universal correct ratio — but there are useful reference frames, and the discipline (as with all reserve questions) is choosing yours explicitly.

Frame one: months of survival. Divide your liquid net worth by your true monthly burn — personal, plus any business support you would provide in a bad stretch. A founder with $250,000 liquid against $20,000 monthly burn has a twelve-and-a-half-month liquidity runway, regardless of what the company is worth on paper. Many founders, seeing this number for the first time, discover it is far shorter than their total net worth had led them to feel.

Frame two: liquid share of total. Early-stage founders often sit below 10% liquid — nearly everything is the company. That can be a rational season, but it should be a *known* season, with a plan for how the share grows over time: distributions, secondaries where available, diversifying new savings rather than reinvesting everything. A liquid share that never grows across years is a structural choice someone should have made on purpose.

Frame three: obligations coverage. Liquid assets against the next twelve months of fixed obligations — taxes, debt service, committed expenses across entities. Below 1x, you are betting on income timing; comfortably above, you have optionality.

## The Founder's Liquidity Events: Where the Split Changes

For salaried professionals, the liquid share drifts slowly. For founders, it moves in punctuated jumps — and each jump is a decision point worth taking deliberately.

A distribution or strong profit quarter moves value from entity-locked to personally liquid. The default failure is residue: the money lands in checking and stays there, technically liquid but unmanaged. The better pattern is running it through an explicit allocation decision — which is precisely the [next-dollar question](/blog/how-ai-answers-what-should-i-do-with-my-next-dollar) — so the new liquidity gets a job.

A secondary sale is the classic split-shifter: converting a slice of the dominant illiquid position into liquid diversification. Whether and when to pursue one is a judgment call far beyond any dashboard's remit — but the *case* for one is made visible by the split. A founder looking at 4% liquid share and a growing family has data for a conversation that "my net worth is fine" was hiding.

An exit is the split inverting entirely — and the founders who navigate post-exit wealth best are typically the ones who built the seeing-and-deciding habit years earlier, on a smaller balance sheet.

In each case the pattern is identical: the event moves value across the liquid/illiquid line, and the quality of what happens next depends on whether the line was visible. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is liquid net worth?

Liquid net worth is the portion of your total net worth that could be converted to spendable cash quickly — typically within days — without a significant loss of value. It generally includes cash, bank balances, money-market holdings, publicly traded securities in taxable accounts, and most major crypto. It excludes private company equity, real estate, collectibles, and other holdings that take months to sell or would sell at steep discounts under time pressure.

### How is liquid net worth different from total net worth?

Total net worth counts everything you own minus everything you owe, regardless of how accessible it is. Liquid net worth counts only the quickly convertible portion. For founders the gap is usually large — company equity and real estate often dominate the total — which is why two people with identical total net worth can have completely different financial flexibility. The split, not the headline, predicts what you can actually do this quarter.

### Do retirement accounts count as liquid?

They sit in a middle category. The assets inside are typically liquid securities, but access rules mean early conversion triggers penalties and taxes — failing the value-retention test. A strict liquid net worth calculation excludes them; a looser one counts them at a haircut. The practical approach is tracking them as their own bucket: real wealth, on a timeline, not part of this month's flexibility.

### Is crypto liquid?

Major crypto held on exchange accounts or in wallets is generally liquid in the speed sense — it can usually be sold quickly. The caveat is volatility: the value you realize depends heavily on timing, so while it belongs on the liquid side of the ledger, prudent planning does not treat it as equivalent to cash. Tracking it correctly requires classification by nature — crypto at a broker still counts as crypto, and fiat sitting on an exchange counts as cash.

### How should I value illiquid assets in the split?

Use two lenses. The official mark (last-round valuation, estimated property value) belongs in total net worth. For liquidity planning, apply a conservative haircut: real estate at mark minus expected selling costs and time, private equity often treated as zero for near-term purposes since it may not be sellable at all. The gap between the two lenses is useful information in itself — it measures how much of your wealth is promise rather than availability.

### What liquid share should a founder target?

There is no universal number, but three frames help: months of burn covered by liquid assets (many aim for 12+), liquid share of total net worth (early founders often sit under 10% — acceptable as a known season, concerning as a permanent state), and coverage of the next year's fixed obligations. The real discipline is choosing your target explicitly and tracking the actual number continuously rather than annually.
