# Kubera Alternatives for Founders in 2026

If you are searching for a **Kubera alternative**, you probably already agree with Kubera's core premise: your wealth is more than a brokerage account, and it deserves one consolidated view. Kubera helped popularize the idea of the modern net worth tracker, and it does several things genuinely well. But no tool fits everyone, and founders in particular tend to hit specific walls — around business entities, decision support, and what happens after the numbers are on the screen.

This guide compares the realistic alternatives in 2026. It starts with what Kubera gets right, because an honest comparison has to. Then it walks through the main options — IOMI, Monarch, Copilot, and the spreadsheet you already have — and matches each to the person it actually serves. By the end you should know which tool fits your situation, not just which one has the longest feature list.

## Key Takeaways

* Kubera is a genuinely good net worth tracker: broad asset coverage, global account support, and a clean interface built around one consolidated number.
* The most common reason founders outgrow it is that tracking is where it stops — there is no entity separation for businesses, no scoring of how well capital is deployed, and no simulation layer.
* Monarch is the better pick if your real problem is budgeting and household cash flow; it is not built for complex founder balance sheets.
* IOMI targets the founder use case directly: personal and business tracked side by side, a Capital Efficiency Score, deterministic simulations, and an AI assistant that answers from your real holdings.
* Spreadsheets remain a legitimate alternative for people who value total control — at the cost of manual updates and no live data.

## What Kubera Does Well

Credit first. Kubera earned its position by taking net worth tracking seriously when most personal finance apps were budgeting tools with a net worth tab bolted on.

Its asset coverage is broad. ## Why Founders Look for a Kubera Alternative

Talk to founders who moved on from a pure tracker and the same three gaps come up.

### Gap one: no real separation between personal and business

A founder's balance sheet is entity-shaped. There is your personal net worth, and there is the LLC's operating account, and there is the holding company that owns two of your assets. A flat list of accounts blurs these together or forces you to choose one lens. What founders actually need is compartments: each business entity tracked as its own unit, personal wealth tracked separately, and a consolidated view on top that never mixes the two. If you have ever tried to answer "how much of this is actually mine, personally?" from a flat account list, you know the problem. The case for structuring it properly is laid out in [tracking personal vs. business assets](/blog/tracking-personal-vs-business-assets-the-founders-guide-to-total-oversight).

### Gap two: the number doesn't tell you what to do

A net worth figure is a scoreboard, not a strategy. Two founders can have the same $3M net worth — one with $600k idle in a checking account earning nothing and 85% of wealth locked in one asset, the other with capital deployed deliberately across liquid and illiquid positions. A tracker shows both the same number. It has no opinion on whether your capital is working, and no way to flag the idle cash, the drag, or the concentration.

### Gap three: no way to test a move before making it

The moment a founder has visibility, the next question is always "what if?" What if I move $100k from the business to a brokerage account? What if I sell the rental? A tracker cannot answer. You end up exporting numbers to a spreadsheet to model the decision — which means the tool got you to the starting line and then handed the hard part back to you.

If those three gaps describe your frustration, you are not looking for a prettier tracker. You are looking for the layer above tracking.

## Alternative 4: The Spreadsheet You Already Have

The honest list includes the incumbent. A well-built spreadsheet offers total control, any structure you can imagine, and zero subscription cost. Plenty of sophisticated founders run their wealth on one.

The trade-offs are known: every number is stale the moment you type it, updates depend on your discipline, formulas silently break, and there is no live connection to anything. A spreadsheet is a snapshot factory; keeping it current is a recurring tax on your attention. The full argument — including when the spreadsheet genuinely wins — is in [net worth tracker spreadsheet vs. app](/blog/the-modern-net-worth-tracker-a-founders-guide-to-wealth-intelligence).

The pattern that works for many people: use software for live consolidation and keep a spreadsheet for the one-off modeling exercises software doesn't cover. The pattern that doesn't work: maintaining a full parallel tracker by hand and wondering why you stopped updating it in March.

## How to Choose: Match the Tool to the Job

Strip the decision down to four questions.

**1. Is your wealth entity-shaped?** If you own businesses, run LLCs, or hold assets through structures, entity separation is non-negotiable. That points to IOMI. If everything you own is in personal accounts, the field is wider.

**2. Do you want an opinion or just a number?** Trackers report; intelligence layers evaluate. If you want software to flag idle cash, concentration, and drag — and to score how well capital is deployed — you want the evaluation layer. If you only want the consolidated number, a tracker suffices.

**3. Will you model decisions?** If "what if I move $200k?" is a question you ask, a deterministic simulator saves you the export-to-spreadsheet loop every time. If you never model, you won't miss it.

**4. Is the real problem spending?** Then say so, pick Monarch, and be happy. Founders sometimes buy wealth tools to fix a budgeting problem, and it never works — the tools measure different things.

There is no universally correct answer. There is a correct answer for your situation, and it falls out of those four questions fairly quickly.
