# Kubera vs Monarch vs IOMI: Which Tracker Fits Founders

The **Kubera vs Monarch** question comes up constantly in founder circles, and it is usually asked the wrong way — as if the two were rivals. They barely compete. Monarch Money is a budgeting app that also shows net worth; Kubera is a net worth tracker that barely touches budgeting. They were built for different jobs, both do their job well, and the confusion exists only because both end up on the same "personal finance tools" lists. Add IOMI to the comparison and you get a third job entirely: not tracking spending, not just listing assets, but analyzing how capital is deployed and what to do next.

So this comparison is organized around jobs rather than feature grids. What is each tool actually for? Where does each one honestly win? And for the specific reader of this blog — a founder or operator whose wealth spans personal and business, liquid and illiquid — which combination fits which stage? By the end you should know not which tool is "best," but which question you need answered, which is the only version of the choice that matters.

## Key Takeaways

* Monarch, Kubera, and IOMI solve three different jobs: spending discipline, asset aggregation, and capital intelligence — most "which is better" confusion dissolves once the jobs are named.
* Monarch is the best pick of the three for budgeting, categorization, and shared household money management; that is its home turf and it is genuinely good there.
* Kubera is a clean, broad net worth ledger — strong at aggregating diverse assets simply, lighter on analysis, scoring, or simulation.
* IOMI is built for founders specifically: personal and business entities in separate compartments, holdings classified by what they are, plus a scoring, simulation, and AI layer on top of the tracking.
* The founder-stage heuristic: spending problem → Monarch; visibility problem → Kubera or IOMI; decision problem (idle cash, concentration, what-if) → IOMI.

## Three Tools, Three Jobs

Every tool in personal finance is an answer to a dominant question, and the fastest way to compare these three is to name each one's question.

**Monarch answers "where did the money go?"** It is transaction-first: connected accounts feed a categorization engine, categories feed budgets and reports, and the household sees its cash flow clearly, together. Net worth exists in Monarch, but as context around the spending picture.

The Kubera vs Monarch confusion dissolves right here, because the second question is different. **Kubera answers "what do I own, everywhere?"** It is holdings-first and deliberately minimal: a modern ledger where bank accounts, brokerage, crypto, real estate, domains, and collectibles sit in one list with a total at the top. Transactions and budgets are not the point; the balance sheet is.

**IOMI answers "what should the money do next?"** It is also holdings-first — full aggregation across asset classes is table stakes — but the aggregation feeds an intelligence layer: a Capital Efficiency Score grading deployment, deterministic simulation of moves before you make them, projections, goals, and an AI assistant that answers from your real numbers. Tracking is the floor, not the product.

Notice that none of these questions is wrong. They are sequential. Early financial life is dominated by the first question, accumulation by the second, and complexity — the founder condition — by the third.

## Monarch, Honestly

Monarch's reputation is earned. Its transaction categorization is among the most accurate available, its rules engine automates the grunt work, and its budgeting is flexible without being fussy — rollovers, goals, one-off targets. The shared-access model is quietly excellent: partners get real logins and one shared picture, which matters enormously for households and is still rare in the category. Design and mobile experience are consistently strong, and as a paid product without ads, its incentives are clean.

Its limits for founders are structural, not qualitative. A transaction-first architecture treats the balance sheet as an appendix: business entities have no native separation from personal, so an LLC's accounts either pollute the household picture or stay disconnected; illiquid assets — private equity, real estate beyond a linked mortgage, collectibles — live as manual placeholders; and there is no analysis of the asset side at all, because that was never the job. None of this is a flaw in Monarch. It is what "budgeting app" means when your life has outgrown budgeting — the full argument is in [Monarch alternatives when you outgrow budgeting](/blog/monarch-money-alternatives-when-you-outgrow-budgeting).

**Choose Monarch when** spending discipline is your active lever: variable income against real obligations, a household that needs shared category visibility, a paydown plan that depends on monthly behavior. The direct feature comparison is at [IOMI vs. Monarch](https://iomifinance.com/compare/monarch).

## Kubera, Honestly

Kubera saw earlier than most that a growing class of people needed a balance-sheet tool, not a better budget. Its strength is breadth with simplicity: connect the standard accounts, add crypto, real estate, domains, even collectibles, and get one clean list with a total — no categories to groom, no budget guilt, no noise. For people who want a trustworthy number and a low-maintenance ledger, it is a genuinely good product, and its asset breadth remains among the widest in the category.

Its limits are the flip side of its minimalism. Kubera tells you what you have; it largely stops there. There is no scoring of how capital is deployed, no idle-cash detection, no deterministic what-if simulation, no AI layer interrogating the holdings. Entity handling is organizational rather than structural — you can arrange assets into groups, but the founder-grade model of business entities as hard compartments with consolidated views computed on top is not the design center. A founder with two LLCs and a holdco can make Kubera work with discipline; the tool will not do that work for them.

**Choose Kubera when** your need is exactly what it does: a broad, clean, current list of everything you own, and you are content to do the interpretation yourself. The detailed comparison is at [IOMI vs. Kubera](https://iomifinance.com/compare/kubera).

## IOMI, in the Same Honest Light

IOMI starts from the same holdings-first premise as Kubera and then makes two bets that define it.

The first bet is that founders need structure, not just breadth. Personal and business are tracked side by side and never mixed: each business entity is its own compartment (up to three on the Business plan), with a consolidated view computed across them. Holdings are classified by what they are rather than where they sit — crypto held at a broker counts as crypto — across the full founder asset map: bank and wallet accounts, brokerage, retirement, crypto in exchange accounts and wallets, real estate with live valuations, private and company equity, physical assets and collectibles, and liabilities. Connections run through Plaid to 12,000+ institutions, read-only: IOMI sees balances and transactions, can never move money, and never sees credentials.

The second bet is that a list is not an answer. On top of the tracking sits the intelligence layer: the Capital Efficiency Score, a 0–100 health check of deployment across liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration; a Simulator that models reallocations and planned cash-flow events deterministically; Projection, showing the year-by-year path from today's holdings; Goals with milestones; a Daily Brief; and IOMI AI, which answers questions from your actual holdings and never estimates when data is missing.

And the honest limits, stated as plainly as the competitors': IOMI does not do budgeting — no spending categories, no envelopes; Monarch is better at that job and this blog says so regularly. It is not a tax engine — anything with tax consequences belongs with your CPA. And its structure has a cost in simplicity: if all you want is a minimal list, Kubera's restraint may fit you better than IOMI's instrumentation.

Pricing is flat by principle — [$29/month Personal, $79/month Business](https://iomifinance.com/pricing) ats, never a percentage of assets, with a one-month free trial and no credit card required.

## The Founder's Decision Grid

Put the three side by side on the dimensions that actually differ — the ones the Kubera vs Monarch framing usually skips — and the choice mostly makes itself.

**Budgeting and spending.** Monarch, clearly. Kubera barely plays; IOMI deliberately doesn't. If this is the job, stop here.

**Asset breadth.** Kubera and IOMI both cover the wide map — crypto, real estate, private equity, collectibles — where Monarch relies on placeholders. Between the two, the difference is less about what fits and more about what happens after it is in.

**Entity separation.** IOMI is the only one of the three built around it: business entities as hard compartments, personal alongside, consolidation computed on top. For a founder with even one LLC, this is usually the single most differentiating row in the grid — the reasoning is laid out in [tracking personal vs. business assets](/blog/tracking-personal-vs-business-assets-the-founders-guide-to-total-oversight).

**Analysis and simulation.** IOMI again, essentially unopposed in this trio: scoring, deterministic what-ifs, projections, and a grounded AI. Monarch's insights aim at spending; Kubera's aim is the accurate list.

**Simplicity.** Kubera wins. Fewest concepts, least maintenance, calmest surface. Monarch is more involved because budgets need tending; IOMI is more involved because analysis has more to say.

**The household test.** Monarch for shared budgets, unambiguously. For a couple that runs a business together, the entity question usually overrides: the business needs its compartment before the household needs its categories.

Three profiles, three answers. A salaried couple managing spending toward their first investments: Monarch, full stop. A self-directed investor with a broad, mostly-personal asset base who wants a clean number: Kubera, and it will age well. A founder or operator with entities, concentration, idle-cash questions, and decisions queued up: IOMI — because at that stage the expensive problem is not knowing what you spent or even what you own, but what to do next.

## Can You Combine Them?

Yes, and some setups genuinely warrant it. The combinations that make sense are the ones that pair different jobs rather than duplicating one.

**Monarch + IOMI** is the common founder pairing: Monarch owns household spending categories and the shared budget; IOMI owns the balance sheet — entities, asset classes, efficiency, simulation. The overlap (both read your checking account) is harmless because each reads it for a different purpose. Two flat subscriptions for two different jobs is not redundancy; it is specialization.

**Kubera + Monarch** pairs the same way for non-founders: ledger plus budget.

**Kubera + IOMI** is the pairing that rarely earns its keep — two holdings-first tools doing overlapping aggregation, differing mainly in what sits on top. Pick one based on whether you want the analysis layer.

The anti-pattern in every combination is forcing a tool across the job boundary: grooming Monarch's manual placeholders into a pseudo-balance-sheet, or reconstructing budget discipline from a wealth platform's transaction feed. Match the tool to the question, and the stack stays small.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Kubera or Monarch better for net worth tracking?

For net worth specifically, Kubera. It is holdings-first by design: broad asset support including crypto, real estate, domains, and collectibles, in a clean ledger with minimal maintenance. Monarch shows net worth, but as an adjunct to a transaction-first budgeting architecture — standard accounts track fine, while irregular assets live as manual placeholders. If net worth is the job and budgeting is not, Kubera is the better fit of those two.

### Is Monarch better than Kubera for budgeting?

Yes, by a wide margin — budgeting is Monarch's entire design center. Accurate categorization, a strong rules engine, flexible budgets, and shared household access make it the best budgeting tool in this comparison. Kubera has no meaningful budgeting; IOMI deliberately has none either. If spending discipline is your active problem, Monarch is the honest recommendation, and no wealth-platform feature list changes that.

### What does IOMI do that Kubera and Monarch don't?

Two structural things and one layer. Structure: business entities as hard compartments tracked side by side with personal (never mixed, consolidated on top), and classification of holdings by what they are rather than where they sit. The layer: analysis on top of tracking — a Capital Efficiency Score across liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration; deterministic simulation of reallocations and planned events; projections; and IOMI AI answering from your real holdings, never estimating when data is missing.

### Which tool is best for a founder with an LLC or holding company?

Entity separation is the deciding dimension, and IOMI is the only one of the three built around it — each entity its own compartment, up to three on the Business plan, personal alongside, consolidation computed without mixing. Monarch has no native entity model; Kubera can approximate one with groups and discipline. Once intercompany flows, per-entity idle cash, and consolidated concentration matter, the structural model stops being optional.

### Do all three connect to banks the same way?

All three use aggregation connections to major institutions, with Plaid as the common backbone — IOMI connects through Plaid to 12,000+ institutions in read-only mode: it can see balances and transactions, never move money, and never sees bank credentials. Differences between tools show up less in bank coverage and more in what happens beyond banks: how each handles real estate valuations, private equity, crypto across exchange accounts and wallets, and collectibles.

### Is it worth paying for two of these tools?

If the two cover different jobs, often yes. Monarch plus IOMI is the classic pairing — shared household budgeting plus founder-grade balance-sheet intelligence — and both are flat subscriptions, so the combined cost stays trivial next to what either replaces. Two holdings-first tools (Kubera plus IOMI) rarely justify themselves, since they duplicate aggregation and differ mainly in the analysis layer. Pick per job, not per feature list.

### How should I actually test this decision?

Run the question test before any trial: write down the three or four financial questions you most wish you could answer instantly. If they are spending questions, trial Monarch. If they are "what do I own" questions, trial Kubera. If they are "how is it deployed and what should I do" questions — idle cash, concentration, what happens if I move $100k — trial IOMI; the free month requires no credit card, and connecting your real accounts answers the fit question faster than any comparison article.
