# Rebalancing for Founders When Your Biggest Asset Isn't Tradeable

Every rebalancing guide assumes the same thing: that you can sell what you are overweight in. Trim the stocks that ran up, buy the bonds that lagged, return to 60/40, done. For a founder, that assumption fails at the first line of the balance sheet. Your largest position is a private company you cannot sell, would not sell, and in most cases should not sell. **Rebalancing concentrated positions** when the concentrated position is illiquid requires a different playbook entirely — one that most portfolio advice never addresses because it was written for people whose wealth trades on an exchange.

This is not a reason to skip rebalancing. It is a reason to redefine it. The founder's version does not aim for textbook allocations; it manages the shape of everything around the untradeable core — the liquid portfolio, the cash, the new dollars arriving each month. This article lays out that playbook: what rebalancing means when your biggest asset is frozen, which levers you actually control, and how to run the process on numbers instead of anxiety.

## Key Takeaways

* Classic rebalancing assumes every position is sellable. For founders, the dominant position usually is not — so the discipline shifts to managing everything around it.
* The levers you control are new contributions, the composition of your liquid portfolio, cash and liability structure, and occasionally partial secondaries — not the concentrated asset itself.
* The most important number is not your total allocation but your liquid allocation: what the portfolio looks like if the company's value went to zero tomorrow.
* Correlation matters more than percentages: liquid holdings that rise and fall with your company's fortunes double your real concentration.
* Rebalancing decisions should be simulated deterministically before execution — moving $100k is easy to model and expensive to regret.

## Why Textbook Rebalancing Breaks for Founders

The standard model treats a portfolio as a set of positions with target weights. Drift happens, you trade back to target. Three assumptions hide inside that model, and founders violate all three.

**Assumption one: positions are liquid.** Private company equity has no daily market. You cannot trim 5% of your company because your allocation drifted. Even when secondary sales are possible, they are episodic, negotiated, and often restricted.

**Assumption two: concentration is an error.** In the textbook, an 80% position is a failure of discipline. For a founder, it is the strategy — deliberate, high-conviction exposure to the thing you control most. The concentration is not the mistake; being blind to what it implies for everything else is.

**Assumption three: the portfolio is one pool.** Founder wealth spans entities: personal accounts, one or more operating companies, sometimes a holding company. Cash in the business is not the same as cash in your brokerage account, and treating them as one pool produces rebalancing decisions that ignore legal and practical boundaries. Keeping [personal and business assets tracked side by side](/blog/tracking-personal-vs-business-assets-the-founders-guide-to-total-oversight), separated but visible together, is a precondition for doing any of this well.

Once you accept that the core position is fixed, the real question emerges: given a frozen core, what should everything else look like?

## The Two-Portfolio Frame

The most useful mental model is to split your balance sheet in two.

**The frozen portfolio** is the company equity and anything else you cannot or will not sell: restricted shares, illiquid partnership stakes, maybe a property you are committed to. You do not rebalance this portfolio. You measure it, update its valuation honestly, and understand its risk character — what sector it is exposed to, what would make it rise or fall.

**The live portfolio** is everything you can actually move: brokerage accounts, cash across entities, crypto on exchanges and in wallets, bonds, new savings as they arrive. This is where all rebalancing happens.

The insight that changes behavior: the live portfolio's job is not to maximize its own return. Its job is to complement the frozen portfolio. A founder whose frozen portfolio is a high-growth software company already owns an enormous, volatile, tech-correlated growth asset. A live portfolio stuffed with tech equities and venture-adjacent crypto is not diversification; it is the same bet wearing different tickers. The correct live portfolio for that founder is boring on purpose — and its boringness is what makes the overall balance sheet sane.

This is why the first metric to compute is **liquid net worth allocation**: strip the frozen assets out and look at what remains. If the company vanished tomorrow, what would you be left with, and how is it structured? Founders who run this exercise for the first time are often startled — the "diversified" picture they carry in their head is 75% one private asset, 15% correlated tech exposure, and 10% cash earning nothing. Computing that split honestly starts with knowing [what your net worth actually is](/blog/what-is-my-net-worth-the-founders-guide-to-total-wealth-intelligence), every asset included and classified correctly.

## The Four Levers You Actually Control

With the frame set, rebalancing concentrated positions comes down to four levers, in rough order of how often you can pull them.

### Lever one: direct new dollars

Every month brings new capital — salary you pay yourself, distributions, savings. This is the cheapest rebalancing mechanism that exists: no sales, no transaction friction, no negotiation. If your balance sheet is overweight growth and underweight stability, new dollars go to stability until the shape improves. Over a year or two, contribution routing alone can meaningfully reshape a liquid portfolio. ### Lever two: recompose the liquid portfolio

Within the live portfolio, classic rebalancing still works — sell what is overweight, buy what is underweight. The difference is the target. Your targets should be set relative to the whole balance sheet including the frozen core, not relative to the liquid slice in isolation. A 70% equity liquid portfolio might be reasonable for a founder whose company is a stable cash-flowing service business, and reckless for one whose company is a pre-revenue startup. Same liquid portfolio, different frozen core, opposite conclusions.

### Lever three: manage cash and liabilities deliberately

Cash is a rebalancing instrument, not a residue. Deciding how much runway to hold personally and inside each entity — and where that cash sits, since idle cash beyond your runway target is pure drag — is an allocation decision. So is debt: paying down a liability is functionally a risk-free return equal to the interest rate, which sometimes beats anything available in the live portfolio.

### Lever four: episodic liquidity from the frozen core

Occasionally the frozen portfolio thaws slightly: a secondary sale in a funding round, a tender offer, a partial exit. These are rare, high-stakes events, and they are the one moment you can rebalance the core directly. The mistake founders make is deciding the amount by feel. The better process: model what different sale sizes do to your liquid allocation, your runway, and your long-term projection — then pick the number that fixes the balance sheet, not the number that sounds impressive. Note that the tax and legal dimensions of any secondary are CPA and attorney territory; the modeling below concerns portfolio shape only.

## Mistakes That Undo the Whole Exercise

**Rebalancing the liquid slice in isolation.** A perfectly balanced 60/40 liquid portfolio next to an 80% frozen tech position is not a balanced balance sheet. Targets must be set at the whole-picture level.

**Ignoring correlation.** The most common founder error: a SaaS founder holding SaaS stocks, tech index funds, and startup-adjacent crypto. Every line item is different; every line item is the same bet. Diversification means owning things that fail differently.

**Letting stale valuations flatter the picture.** Marking the company at the last round's price two years later distorts every downstream ratio. Honest, conservative marks make the rest of the math trustworthy.

**Confusing rebalancing with de-risking the company.** No liquid-portfolio arrangement removes the core risk of a concentrated private position. Rebalancing manages everything around that risk; it does not neutralize it. Pretending otherwise leads to false comfort.

**Doing it annually from a spreadsheet.** By the time you assemble the picture manually, it is stale. The whole point of continuous consolidation is that the shape of your wealth is always current, so decisions happen when they are needed rather than when the spreadsheet gets rebuilt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you rebalance when most of your net worth is a private company?

You do not rebalance the company position itself — you manage everything around it. The levers are: routing new savings toward underweighted assets, composing your liquid portfolio to complement (not echo) the company's risk profile, setting deliberate cash and debt levels, and using rare liquidity events like secondaries to adjust the core. Targets are set at the whole-balance-sheet level, including the illiquid position.

### What allocation should the liquid portfolio have?

It depends on the frozen core. A founder whose company is a volatile growth asset generally wants a more conservative, less correlated liquid portfolio; a founder whose company is a stable cash-flowing business can take more liquid risk. There is no universal number — which is why simulating specific allocations against your actual balance sheet beats copying a model portfolio.

### How often should founders rebalance?

Review the shape monthly and route new contributions accordingly; recompose the liquid portfolio quarterly if drift is material. The concentrated position's valuation updates only when something real happens — a funding round, a tender offer, audited financials. Continuous monitoring with episodic action beats a rigid calendar.

### Should I sell company shares in a secondary to rebalance?

That is a personal decision with tax and legal dimensions that belong with your CPA and attorney. What software can do is show the portfolio consequences: model several sale sizes and compare what each does to your liquid allocation, runway, and long-term projection. Founders who model first tend to choose the size that fixes their balance-sheet shape rather than a round number chosen by feel.

### What is correlation risk in a founder portfolio?

It is when your sellable assets rise and fall with the same forces as your company. A SaaS founder holding tech index funds and growth stocks has one bet expressed three ways — if the sector turns, everything drops together. Effective rebalancing for founders weighs correlation as heavily as percentages: the liquid portfolio should fail differently than the company.
