# Domains, IP, and Digital Assets in Your Net Worth

When people search for **digital assets net worth**, they usually mean crypto. But for founders and operators, crypto is only the most liquid corner of a much larger category: domains bought over a decade, trademarks and patents, a newsletter with ten thousand subscribers, a code library that licenses for real money, a YouTube channel that pays monthly. These things have value — sometimes serious value — and they appear on almost nobody's balance sheet. The result is a net worth number that quietly understates what you actually own, and a category of wealth that never gets managed because it never gets measured.

This article is about closing that gap honestly. Which digital assets belong in your net worth, which don't, how to value them without fooling yourself, and how to track them alongside your accounts, property, and equity so the whole picture lives in one place. The theme throughout is conservatism: a digital asset entered at a defensible value strengthens your balance sheet; one entered at a hopeful value corrupts it.

## Key Takeaways

* Digital assets split into two very different groups: crypto (exchange accounts and wallets, with market prices) and everything else — domains, IP, content properties, licenses — which has value but no ticker.
* The inclusion test is transferability: if an asset could realistically be sold or licensed to someone else, it belongs in your net worth; if its value evaporates without you, it's income, not an asset.
* Value the non-priced assets conservatively — cost basis, comparable sales, or a conservative multiple of the cash flow they generate — and write the method down.
* Keep personal and business ownership straight: IP owned by your LLC is the entity's asset (and part of your equity's value), not a separate personal line — double counting is the classic error.
* Consolidation is what makes the category manageable: digital assets tracked next to everything else reveal concentration and idle value you can't see when they live in a registrar dashboard and a filing cabinet.

## What Counts as a Digital Asset — and What Doesn't

The category is blurry at the edges, so start with a working definition: a digital asset is a non-physical asset you own or control that could plausibly be converted to money by sale, license, or transfer. That covers more than most people expect:

* **Domains** — from a portfolio of speculative registrations to the one premium domain your business happens to sit on.
* **Registered IP** — trademarks, patents, registered copyrights.
* **Content properties** — newsletters, YouTube channels, podcasts, niche sites with audiences and revenue.
* **Software and code** — licensed libraries, plugins, templates, apps that generate sales.
* **Accounts and inventory with market value** — where genuine, legal secondary markets exist.
* **Crypto** — held in exchange accounts and wallets. The one sub-category with live market prices, and the one covered separately in the [crypto portfolio consolidation guide](/blog/crypto-portfolio-consolidation-exchanges-and-wallets-in-one-place).

Now the exclusion test, which matters more than the inclusion list: **would the value survive a transfer to someone else?** A domain transfers cleanly — its value survives. A trademark assigns. A newsletter sells with its list. But a personal brand doesn't transfer. Your LinkedIn following, your reputation, your ability to command consulting rates — genuinely valuable, and not assets. They are income-generating capacity, and putting them on a balance sheet is how net worth numbers become fiction. Same for accounts that terms of service prohibit selling: control without transferability isn't ownership.

The gray zone — a newsletter written in your voice, a channel that is mostly your face — takes a discount for key-person dependence. Buyers of such properties apply exactly that discount; your balance sheet should too.

## The Valuation Problem: No Ticker, No Feed

Crypto aside, digital assets have no market price. Nobody quotes your trademark daily. So valuation needs a method, and the method needs to be conservative, because you are both the appraiser and the beneficiary of optimism.

Three approaches, in order of preference:

**Comparable sales, discounted.** Domains have visible comps from marketplace sales; content properties trade through brokers at observable revenue multiples. Find the comps, then discount meaningfully — your asset is less liquid than the comp, and sellers publicize wins, not the inventory that never sold.

**Capitalized cash flow, conservative multiple.** For assets that earn — a licensed library, a monetized channel, a newsletter with sponsors — value as a multiple of annual profit attributable to the asset. Small digital properties typically trade at low single-digit multiples of annual earnings; key-person dependence pushes the multiple down further. Resist using the multiples from venture-scale comparisons; a solo-maintained plugin is not a SaaS company.

**Cost basis or zero.** For everything without comps or cash flow — the speculative domain portfolio, the patent not yet licensed — carry cost basis, or zero. A hundred domains renewed at $12 each are worth their renewal decision, not the hypothetical exit of the best one.

Two rules that keep the whole exercise honest. First, *write the method down* next to each valuation — "12 subscribers-months of profit at 2.5x" — so future-you can re-derive or challenge it. Second, *revalue on events, not on moods*: a real offer, a comparable sale, a revenue change. If your digital asset line only ever moves upward between funding conversations, it isn't a valuation; it's a wish. This is the same discipline that applies to any hard-to-price holding, and it's covered more broadly in [consolidating alternative assets in your net worth](/blog/consolidating-alternative-assets-in-your-net-worth).

## Personal or Entity: Get the Ownership Straight

Here is the error that corrupts more founder balance sheets than bad valuation: counting the same digital asset twice.

Digital assets usually have a legal owner, and for founders it is often not them personally — it's the LLC or corporation. If your company owns the trademark, the domain, and the codebase, those assets are part of what makes your *equity* worth something. They are inside the company compartment. Listing them again as personal assets alongside your equity stake counts them twice, and inflates net worth by exactly the amount of the double-count.

The clean mental model: every asset lives in exactly one compartment. Company-owned IP sits in the entity, contributing to the entity's value. Personally-owned digital assets — the domain portfolio you hold in your own name, the newsletter that predates the company — sit in the personal compartment. When ownership is ambiguous (common with founder-created IP), that ambiguity is itself a problem worth resolving with your attorney, because it matters enormously in a sale or dispute.

## Managing the Category Once You Can See It

Measurement changes behavior. Once digital assets sit on the balance sheet at honest values, a few management questions become natural — and answerable.

**The renewal audit.** A domain portfolio visible at cost basis invites the annual question: which of these renewals is a decision, and which is a habit? Carrying costs on speculative digital assets are small individually and meaningful in aggregate — textbook drag.

**The monetization check.** IP carried at zero because it earns nothing is a prompt, not a verdict. A patent that could license, a library that could charge — assets earning nothing are the digital version of idle cash.

**The concentration reality.** For some operators, one digital property — the channel, the newsletter, the domain the business runs on — is a top-three asset. Seeing it sized against everything else clarifies how much depends on one platform's policies or one algorithm's mood. That's concentration risk in a new costume, and it deserves the same scrutiny as any concentrated position.

**The estate and continuity note.** Digital assets are uniquely easy to lose at transfer: registrar accounts, wallet access, channel credentials. An inventory with values — which is what your balance sheet now is — is the foundation of making sure the assets survive you administratively. The legal structure around that is your attorney's domain.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do digital assets count toward net worth?

Transferable ones do: domains, registered IP, content properties with audiences and revenue, licensed software, and crypto in exchange accounts and wallets. The test is whether the value would survive a sale or transfer to someone else. Personal brand, reputation, and non-transferable accounts are income-generating capacity, not assets — including them inflates net worth with value no one could ever buy.

### How do I value a domain name for my net worth?

Comparable marketplace sales, discounted for illiquidity — or cost basis when no real comps exist. For a portfolio of speculative registrations, cost basis or zero is the honest carry; the hypothetical value of the best domain isn't a portfolio valuation. A domain earning parking or redirect revenue can carry a conservative multiple of that income. Whatever you choose, write the method down and revalue on events (a real offer, a comparable sale), not on optimism.

### How should founders track IP owned by their company?

Inside the company's compartment, not as a personal line. Company-owned trademarks, patents, and code contribute to what your equity is worth — listing them personally alongside your equity stake double-counts them. Keep every asset in exactly one compartment: entity-owned IP in the entity, personally-held digital assets in the personal column. If ownership is ambiguous, resolve it with your attorney; it matters most exactly when the stakes are highest.

### Is crypto a digital asset in this sense?

It's the special case: the one digital asset class with live market prices. IOMI treats crypto as its own asset class — exchange accounts and wallets — and classifies holdings by what they are, so crypto held at a broker still counts as crypto exposure. The rest of the digital category (domains, IP, content) has no price feed and needs the conservative manual valuation this article describes.

### How does IOMI track domains and IP?

As manually valued holdings alongside everything else: you enter the asset in the correct compartment (personal or a business entity), set a value using a method you can defend, and update it when something real changes. ### What digital assets should I leave off my balance sheet?

Anything non-transferable (personal brand, followings, reputation), anything a platform's terms prohibit selling, and anything whose value you can only justify with a story. Also resist counting projected value — a patent that might license someday is worth zero until it does. A shorter, honest balance sheet beats a longer, hopeful one in every decision that follows from it.

### How often should I revalue digital assets?

On events, not on a calendar: a genuine offer, a comparable sale, a meaningful revenue change, a platform shift that changes an asset's earning power. Between events, values stay put. An annual review pass makes sense to catch stale entries and abandoned renewals — but the discipline that matters is refusing to nudge values upward without something real behind the change.
