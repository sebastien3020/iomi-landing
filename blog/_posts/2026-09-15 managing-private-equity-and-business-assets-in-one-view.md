# Managing Private Equity and Business Assets in One View

For most founders, the strangest fact about their finances is this: the largest asset they own appears nowhere. Bank apps show cash. Brokerage apps show public holdings. But the company stake worth more than everything else combined lives in a cap table PDF and a memory of the last round's valuation. **Private equity tracking software** exists to fix that gap — to hold your company equity, angel positions, and business entities in the same live view as your liquid assets, so your net worth stops being a guess.

This guide covers what proper private-asset tracking looks like in practice: how to value stakes defensibly, how to keep business entities separated from personal wealth without losing the consolidated picture, what the honest limits of any tracking tool are, and how the unified view changes the decisions you make with everything else you own.

## Key Takeaways

* Private company equity is typically a founder's largest asset and the one most tools can't hold — leaving net worth calculations missing their biggest line.
* Valuation discipline beats valuation precision: record a defensible number, note the method, and update on real events rather than mood.
* Business entities need compartments, not blending — each LLC or holding company tracked separately, with consolidation on demand.
* A unified view changes downstream decisions: liquidity buffers, diversification, and new investments all look different once concentration is visible.
* Honest tracking software models what you own; it doesn't invent liquidity, forecast exits, or pretend a paper valuation is spendable cash.

## The Invisible Asset Problem

Spend ten minutes with a typical founder's finances and you find the same paradox. Meticulously tracked: checking accounts, credit cards, a brokerage portfolio worth maybe 15% of their wealth. Untracked: the company stake worth 60%, the LLC that owns a rental property, two angel investments, and a SAFE from a friend's startup. The tracked portion has apps, alerts, and charts. The untracked portion — the actual wealth — has a folder of PDFs.

The consequences are practical, not cosmetic. Ask yourself three questions most founders can't answer without an evening of archaeology:

* What is my total net worth, including all entities and private positions, as of today?
* What percentage of my wealth is illiquid, and how long could I operate if the liquid part had to carry everything?
* How concentrated am I when company equity, company-correlated investments, and business-held assets are added up honestly?

These aren't trivia. Liquidity buffers, diversification decisions, and every "can I afford this?" question depend on the full picture. A net worth figure that omits your largest asset isn't approximately right — it's a different number about a different person.

The failure isn't yours; it's tooling. Consumer finance apps were built around linkable institutions, and private assets have no institution to link. The answer is a platform designed to hold both: connected accounts syncing automatically, private assets held as first-class entries with real valuation discipline. ## What Belongs in the Private Side of Your Balance Sheet

"Private equity" on a founder's balance sheet is broader than the term suggests. A complete inventory usually includes:

**Your own company equity.** Common stock, options, or units in the business you operate. Usually the single largest line, and the one whose valuation requires the most discipline (next section).

**Angel investments and SAFEs.** Small checks into other people's companies. Individually modest, collectively meaningful — and easy to forget entirely. A SAFE that converted two rounds ago at a markup is wealth you own and probably aren't counting.

**Fund positions.** LP stakes in venture or other private funds, with capital calls and distributions that need tracking against commitments.

**Business entities as containers.** An LLC or holding company isn't just an asset — it's a compartment that *holds* assets: operating cash, equipment, property, sometimes investment accounts. Tracking the entity means tracking its contents, not writing one blended number.

**Business-held real estate and physical assets.** Property in an entity's name, vehicles, equipment with real resale value.

IOMI treats these as first-class asset classes alongside bank and wallet accounts, brokerage, retirement, crypto, real estate with live valuations, collectibles, and liabilities. Classification follows what an asset *is*, not where it sits — an investment account inside your LLC counts toward your investment exposure, held by the entity. That rule sounds pedantic until you ask the concentration question, at which point it's the difference between a true answer and a soothing one.

## Valuation Discipline: The Part Everyone Gets Wrong

The most common objection to tracking private equity is "but I don't know what it's worth." Correct — and it doesn't matter as much as you think. The goal is not a precise number; it's a *defensible, consistent, dated* number. Public-market precision is unavailable for private assets, and pretending otherwise is how tracking turns into fiction. What works:

**Anchor to real events.** The cleanest marks come from transactions: your latest priced round, a secondary sale, a 409A valuation, a signed term sheet. When an event happens, update the number and move on.

**Pick a method and write it down.** Last-round price. A revenue multiple you can justify from comparable companies. A deliberate discount to the last round if conditions have deteriorated. Any of these is fine; what's not fine is switching methods based on how you feel. Record the method next to the number so future-you knows what past-you was thinking.

**Update on a calendar, not a mood.** Annually at minimum, plus on every real event. The founders who update valuations when optimistic and "haven't gotten around to it" when pessimistic are running a morale dashboard, not a balance sheet.

**Consider a haircut for decision-making.** Some founders deliberately carry their stake below the last round's implied value — recognizing illiquidity, dilution to come, and the gap between paper and proceeds. Whatever you choose, the discipline is the same: one number, one method, one date.

**Never let the tool fill the silence.** This is a design principle worth insisting on: when data is missing, the system should say so rather than estimate. IOMI AI answers from your real holdings and never invents a number when one isn't there — because a confident guess about your largest asset is worse than an honest gap.

## Entities Deserve Compartments, Not a Blender

The second structural requirement is entity separation. If you operate through an LLC, a holding company, or several of each, "your" wealth is legally and practically compartmentalized — and your tracking should be too.

Blending fails in both directions. Mix business operating cash into your personal total and your net worth inflates by money that isn't practically yours to spend — it's working capital with obligations. Ignore the entities entirely and you undercount by whatever they hold. Either way, every downstream number — liquidity, concentration, runway — inherits the error.

The right structure is compartments with consolidation: each entity tracked as its own unit with its own accounts, assets, and liabilities; personal wealth as its own ledger; and a consolidated view you can switch on when you want the whole picture. How does entity-held property change personal diversification needs? If the business needs a cash injection, which personal position funds it least painfully? ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is private equity tracking software?

It's software that holds illiquid positions — your company equity, angel investments, SAFEs, fund stakes, and business-entity assets — as first-class entries on your balance sheet, alongside automatically synced bank, brokerage, and crypto accounts. The point is one complete, current view of net worth instead of a liquid-only picture that omits your largest holdings. IOMI does this with entity compartments and an intelligence layer on top.

### How do I value my private company equity for tracking?

Anchor to real events: your latest priced round, a 409A valuation, or a secondary transaction. Record the number, the method, and the date, then update on new events or annually — whichever comes first. Consistency matters more than precision; the failure mode is switching methods with your mood. Some founders deliberately carry a discount to the last round to reflect illiquidity and future dilution.

### Can I track LLCs and holding companies separately from personal assets?

Yes, and you should. Each entity gets its own compartment containing its accounts, assets, and liabilities; personal wealth stays its own ledger; and a consolidated view combines them on demand. IOMI supports up to 3 business entities on the Business plan, tracked side by side with personal and never blended — which keeps numbers like personal runway and entity cash honest.

### Should paper valuations count in my net worth?

Count them, but label them. A private stake at its last-round mark is real wealth and belongs on the balance sheet — but it isn't spendable, and your liquidity planning should treat it accordingly. The practical approach is tracking both facts at once: total net worth including private marks, and a clear view of what percentage is illiquid. Some founders also carry a deliberate haircut on paper valuations for decision-making purposes.

### How often should private asset values be updated?

On every real event — a funding round, a secondary sale, a new 409A, a signed term sheet — and at least annually otherwise. The discipline that matters is updating on a calendar rather than on sentiment, so the number stays a measurement instead of a mood ring.
