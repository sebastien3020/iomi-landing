# The Real Cost of Cash Drag in a High-Rate World

**Cash drag** is the return you give up by holding money in accounts that pay less than what that money could safely earn. It sounds like a rounding error. For founders, it is usually the single largest silent cost on the balance sheet — bigger than any subscription, bigger than most advisory fees, and completely invisible on a bank statement, because a bank statement never shows you what you didn't earn.

The strange thing about the current environment is that higher rates made the problem worse, not better. When short-term yields were near zero, parking cash cost you almost nothing relative to the alternatives. Now the gap between a 0.01% business checking account and a plain money market fund is measured in full percentage points. Every idle dollar has a visible, quotable opportunity cost — and most founders still have six figures sitting in accounts that were opened for convenience, not yield. This article walks through what cash drag actually costs, why entrepreneur balance sheets accumulate it faster than anyone else's, how to measure yours across every entity you own, and what a sensible fix looks like.

## Key Takeaways

* Cash drag is the gap between what your idle cash earns and what it could safely earn — in a high-rate world, that gap is often several percentage points on six-figure balances.
* Founders accumulate drag structurally: operating buffers in multiple entities, sweep accounts that never got set up, and post-liquidity-event cash that waits too long for a decision.
* The cost compounds. A persistent $200k of idle cash at a 4-point yield gap is roughly $8,000 a year, every year, before compounding.
* You cannot fix drag you cannot see. Measuring it requires a consolidated view of every account across personal and business — which is exactly where most founders' tracking breaks down.
* The goal is not zero cash. It is deliberate cash: a defined operating buffer and personal runway, with everything above that line deployed or parked at a competitive yield.

## What Cash Drag Actually Is

The definition is simple: cash drag is the return differential between where your cash sits and where it could sit at equivalent safety and access. If $150,000 sits in a checking account paying effectively nothing, and a government money market fund or Treasury bill yields several percent, the drag is the difference, multiplied by the balance, for as long as the situation persists.

Note what this definition does not say. It does not say "cash is bad." Cash is the most useful asset you own: it pays bills, survives drawdowns, funds opportunities, and lets you sleep. Drag is not about holding cash. It is about holding cash *badly* — in the wrong account, at the wrong yield, in amounts nobody actually decided on.

That last part matters most. Almost no founder decides to hold $340,000 across seven low-yield accounts. It accretes. A distribution lands in the LLC's checking account and stays there. A tax reserve gets overfunded and never trued up. An old sole-proprietor account keeps a balance because closing it feels like admin. Each individual balance looks reasonable. The total is only visible if something adds it up — which is why drag is fundamentally a visibility problem before it is a yield problem.

## Why High Rates Made Drag More Expensive, Not Less

There is a common intuition that high rates are good for savers, so cash holders should be happier now. That is only true for cash that is actually positioned to earn the rate.

When the risk-free rate is near zero, the spread between the worst cash account and the best one is small. Idle money in checking costs you maybe half a point versus the alternative. Annoying, not material. When short-term rates are meaningfully positive, that spread widens dramatically, because bank deposit rates on standard checking and many savings products lag far behind market rates. The money market fund reprices with the market. Your business checking account does not.

The result: the same idle balance that cost you $700 a year in a zero-rate world can cost $8,000–$12,000 a year now. High rates did not solve cash drag. They repriced it — sharply upward — while leaving the account structures that create it untouched.

There is a second-order effect too. High rates make holding *some* cash genuinely attractive, which gives founders a comfortable-sounding reason to hold *lots* of cash. "Cash is paying 4–5%" becomes a justification for balances that are actually earning 0.01%, because the founder is thinking about the asset class and not the specific accounts. The asset class pays well. Your accounts might not. Drag lives in that gap.

## Where Founder Balance Sheets Accumulate Drag

Employee finances have one or two places for cash to hide. Founder finances have a dozen, because every entity multiplies the account count.

### The operating buffer that grew

Every business needs an operating buffer — some months of expenses in reliably accessible cash. The buffer that was right at $30k of monthly burn quietly becomes triple what's needed when burn drops or revenue stabilizes. Nobody revisits it, because the number going up feels like safety. The excess above the actual buffer requirement is pure drag.

### One buffer per entity

If you run multiple LLCs or a holding company structure, each entity tends to keep its own conservative buffer. Individually defensible; collectively, you may be holding four overlapping safety nets when the structure only needs one and a half. Founders [tracking assets across multiple entities](/blog/tracking-personal-vs-business-assets-the-founders-guide-to-total-oversight) usually discover this the first time all accounts appear on one screen: the consolidated cash number is far larger than any mental estimate.

### Post-event cash waiting for a decision

After a liquidity event — a secondary sale, a property sale, a big distribution — cash lands and waits. The decision about deploying it is genuinely hard, so it gets deferred. Six months of deferral on a large balance in a low-yield account is one of the most expensive forms of drag, precisely because the balance is large and the account was chosen by the wire instructions, not by you.

### Tax reserves set by fear

Reserving for taxes is correct. Reserving 40% when your effective liability is 28%, in an account paying nothing, is drag with a virtuous-sounding name. The reserve should be sized by your CPA's estimate, not by anxiety, and it should sit somewhere that earns while it waits.

## Putting a Number on It

The arithmetic is deliberately simple, and worth doing with real numbers rather than reading past.

Take every account across every entity. For each, note the balance and the effective yield. Compare against a realistic safe alternative — a Treasury money market fund, T-bills, or a genuinely high-yield business savings product. The drag is:

**(alternative yield − current yield) × balance × time held**

A founder with $80k excess in business checking (0.01%), $120k of stale tax reserve in a savings account (0.5%), and $150k of undeployed distribution in personal checking (0.01%) against a 4.5% alternative is running roughly $15,000 a year of drag. That is a real cost, paid in full, every year the configuration persists — it simply never appears as a line item anywhere.

## Why You Can't Fix What You Can't See

Everything above assumes you can list every account with its balance and yield. In practice, that assumption fails for exactly the people with the most drag.

A founder with two operating companies, a holding entity, personal accounts at two banks, a brokerage, and an exchange account has 15–25 places cash can sit. No single institution shows the whole picture. Spreadsheets go stale the week after they're built. ## Fixing Drag Without Breaking Your Buffer

The fix is not "hold less cash." It is a three-step structure that most founders can implement in an afternoon once the full picture exists.

**Step one: define the buffers deliberately.** Decide, per entity, how many months of operating expenses belong in instantly accessible cash, and decide your personal runway target the same way. Write the numbers down. These are policy decisions, not market decisions, and they should change only when burn or circumstances change.

**Step two: reposition the buffer itself.** Even the buffer does not need to earn zero. Business money market funds, Treasury-backed sweep products, and high-yield business savings accounts keep same-week access while closing most of the yield gap. The buffer stays a buffer; it just stops being free money for your bank.

**Step three: give everything above the line a job.** Cash above the defined buffers either gets deployed according to your plan or gets an explicit parking decision at a competitive yield while the plan forms. The key word is explicit. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is cash drag in simple terms?

Cash drag is the return you lose by holding money in accounts that pay less than what the same money could safely earn elsewhere. If $100,000 sits in a checking account paying 0.01% while a money market fund yields 4.5%, the drag is roughly $4,500 per year. It never appears as a fee or a charge — it is income that simply never arrives.

### Isn't holding cash smart when rates are high?

Holding cash can be smart; holding it in the wrong account is not. High rates make the asset class attractive, but standard checking accounts and many savings products do not pay anywhere near market rates. The question is never "should I hold cash?" — it is "is every dollar of cash I hold actually earning what cash currently earns?" For most founders the honest answer is no.

### How much cash should a founder actually hold?

There is no universal number, but the useful structure is: a deliberate operating buffer per business (commonly several months of expenses), a personal runway target, and a tax reserve sized by your CPA's estimate rather than by anxiety. Everything above those defined lines should either be deployed according to your plan or parked at a competitive yield while you decide. The failure mode is not the buffer — it is the undecided cash above it.

### How do I measure my own cash drag?

List every account across every entity with its balance and effective yield, compare each against a realistic safe alternative (Treasury money market funds or T-bills are a fair benchmark), and multiply the yield gap by the balance. The hard part is the first step — getting a complete, current list across personal accounts, business entities, and forgotten accounts. That is a consolidation problem, and it is the reason drag persists.

### Is cash drag the same as idle cash?

They are closely related but not identical. Idle cash is the balance itself — money sitting without a defined job.
