# Diversifying Out of Your Own Company Without Losing Conviction

Every founder eventually meets the advice to **diversify concentrated stock**, and most founders quietly resent it. The concentration is not an accident — it is the whole thesis. You built the company. You know it better than any outside asset you could buy. Selling a piece of it to purchase index funds feels like trading the thing you understand for things you don't, and doing it on the recommendation of people who would never have taken your original risk in the first place.

This article takes the resentment seriously, because it contains a real insight: conviction is an asset, and diversification frameworks written for lottery winners and lucky employees do not map onto owner-operators. But it also takes the arithmetic seriously, because the arithmetic does not care about conviction. The goal is a middle path with actual mechanics — how to decide what concentration is deliberate versus accumulated, how to sell without feeling like a deserter, and how to structure the process so that both your conviction and your family's security survive whatever happens next.

## Key Takeaways

* Concentration and conviction are not the same thing. Conviction is a belief about the company's future; concentration is a fact about your family's exposure. You can keep all of the first while reducing the second.
* The ruin asymmetry is the core argument: past a certain point, additional upside barely changes your life while the downside changes everything. Diversification is not doubt — it is capping the one outcome you cannot recover from.
* Rules decided in advance beat decisions made in the moment. A written policy — what triggers a sale, how much, where proceeds go — removes the emotional tax from every individual transaction.
* Secure founders operate better. A personal balance sheet that survives the company's failure makes you a braver CEO, not a hedged one: you can take the right long-term risks precisely because your family is not the collateral.
* Measure the process, not the mood: track your concentration percentage over time, simulate moves before making them, and let the trend — not headlines or funding-round euphoria — drive the pace.

## Concentration Is a Fact; Conviction Is a Belief

Start by separating two things that feel identical from inside a founder's head.

Conviction is your assessment that the company will win. It is informed, earned, and probably better calibrated than any outsider's view. Nothing in this article asks you to revise it downward.

Concentration is a number: the share of your total net worth represented by one illiquid, unhedgeable asset whose value is correlated with your income, your reputation, and your daily output. That number is a fact about your household's risk structure, and it is true regardless of whether your conviction is right.

Here is the uncomfortable part the resentment skips: even if your conviction is correct in expectation, concentration determines what happens to you in the tail. Great companies with correct-in-hindsight theses still hit funding winters, lawsuits, platform shifts, co-founder implosions, and plain bad timing. The graveyard contains plenty of founders who were right about the market and still lost everything, because being right eventually is not the same as surviving until eventually.

So reframe the question. Not "do I believe in my company?" — irrelevant, the answer is yes. But: "given that I believe, what is the maximum share of my family's wealth that should ride on any single outcome, including the one I control?" That is a portfolio question, and it has honest answers that leave your conviction fully intact.

Getting the number right requires seeing it. Most founders have never computed true concentration — company equity as a share of everything, counted across [all asset classes and entities](/blog/what-is-my-net-worth-the-founders-guide-to-total-wealth-intelligence). It is routinely higher than the founder's guess, because the guess anchors on the brokerage account and forgets that the house has a mortgage and the angel checks are the same bet wearing different logos.

## The Ruin Asymmetry: Why Some Chips Come Off the Table

The mathematical case for partial diversification fits in a paragraph, and it does not require pessimism.

Consider a founder whose company equity is 85% of net worth. If the company 10x's, their life improves — but honestly, a founder at that level already lives well, and the marginal life change from "very wealthy" to "extremely wealthy" is modest. If the company zeroes, their life is restructured: housing, timeline, options, family stress — everything. The payoff curve is brutally asymmetric: bounded gains in lived experience on the upside, unbounded damage on the downside.

Now take the same founder after moving, over a few years, enough off the table that diversified assets cover their family's needs indefinitely. The company is still 60% of net worth. The upside case is nearly identical — the equity still dominates the outcome if things go right. But the ruin case is gone. The company can fail completely and the family's floor holds.

That is the trade: give up a sliver of the extreme upside to delete the catastrophic downside. No view about the company's prospects is embedded in it — it is pure payoff-shaping, the same logic as insurance, practiced by people whose houses have never burned down.

There is also a performance argument, and experienced founders will recognize it. A CEO whose family is fully exposed makes subtly worse decisions: too conservative where boldness is right (can't risk the downside), too desperate where patience is right (need the outcome too much). A founder with a secured floor plays the actual game instead of managing personal terror. Diversification, done right, is not hedging your conviction — it is removing the conflict of interest between your family and your strategy.

## Mechanics: How Founders Actually Take Chips Off

The available moves depend on your company's stage, and each has real constraints. This is the educational map; the execution always runs through your counsel, your board, and your CPA — the tax dimension of any equity sale is significant and entirely situation-specific.

**Secondary sales** — selling a slice of private shares to investors, often inside a funding round — are the primary tool for venture-backed founders. Boards and investors increasingly understand that a founder with a secured floor is a better bet, and negotiated founder secondaries at meaningful rounds are now common. Constraints: they happen when rounds happen, require approval, and carry signaling weight that makes size and framing matter.

**Distributions** are the equivalent for profitable owner-operated businesses: cash out of the company, on a schedule, against a buffer rule, redeployed into diversified assets. Slower, but fully under your control and repeatable every quarter.

**Structured selling programs** apply to founders with public or soon-public stock: pre-committed schedules that sell fixed amounts at fixed intervals, decided in advance precisely so no individual sale is a market call or a statement.

**What not to do** is improvise: episodic, mood-driven sales decided under either euphoria or fear. Every mechanism above works because it moves the decision from the moment to the policy — which is the next section.

## Write the Policy Before You Need It

The emotional tax on diversification comes from re-deciding every time. Each individual sale asks, "do you still believe?" — and answering that question quarterly is exhausting and distorting. The fix is one decision, made once, written down.

A founder diversification policy fits on a page and answers four questions:

**The target band.** What share of net worth do you intend the company to be — deliberately, as a chosen bet? Not a universal number; a written one. The point is that 85% should be a decision, not an accumulation.

**The triggers.** What events open a diversification window — a priced round with secondary appetite, distributions above the buffer, a valuation threshold, a date? Rules-based triggers convert "should I sell?" into "did the trigger fire?"

**The size formula.** When a window opens, how much moves? A fixed fraction of the opportunity, or whatever brings you back inside the band — decided in advance, so the number is arithmetic instead of negotiation with yourself.

**The destination.** Where do proceeds go, in what order? Typically: personal reserves to a runway target first, then boring diversified assets. Pre-deciding the destination prevents the classic failure where de-risked capital quietly re-risks itself into angel checks — which are, as your balance sheet sees it, [the same early-stage bet in different packaging](/blog/tracking-angel-investments-and-safes-in-your-portfolio).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much of my net worth should be in my own company?

There is no universal threshold, and IOMI does not give personalized allocation advice. The discipline that matters is making the number a decision: compute your true concentration across everything you own, choose a target band deliberately, and write the policy that moves you toward it. Founders differ legitimately on the band; what fails is concentration by accumulation, where the number was never chosen at all.

### Does selling founder shares signal a lack of confidence?

Less than it used to, and less than founders fear. Investors and boards increasingly view a founder who has secured a personal floor as more stable, not less committed — desperation is a worse look than diversification. Framing matters: a modest, policy-driven secondary inside a strong round, communicated as securing the family so the founder can go long, reads very differently from an improvised large sale.

### What is the difference between diversifying and giving up on my company?

Diversifying changes your family's exposure; giving up changes your effort and belief. A founder who moves 15% of equity into boring assets and keeps operating with full intensity has diversified. The confusion comes from treating every sale as a referendum on conviction — which is exactly what a written policy exists to prevent: the policy decided once; each sale is just execution.

### How do I diversify if my company is private and there's no liquidity?

The main mechanisms are negotiated secondary sales during funding rounds (for venture-backed companies) and scheduled distributions against a cash-buffer rule (for profitable owner-operated ones). Both are episodic and constrained, which is why the policy matters: define your triggers and sizes in advance so that when a window opens, you execute instead of deliberating from scratch.

### What should I do with the proceeds after selling concentrated stock?

Pre-decide the destination in your policy — that is the honest answer, and the ordering most founders use is reserves first until personal runway hits target, then diversified holdings. The classic failure is de-risked capital quietly re-concentrating into angel checks and speculative bets, which your balance sheet correctly reads as the same risk in new packaging. Tax consequences of any sale are significant and specific — plan them with your CPA before, not after.

### Should I diversify before or after a liquidity event?

Structurally, the years before a potential exit are when concentration risk peaks — everything rides on an outcome not yet secured — which argues for taking policy-driven opportunities (secondaries, distributions) as they arise rather than waiting. The counterargument is that pre-exit equity may compound fastest. The resolution is the band: hold your chosen concentration deliberately, harvest windows that fire your triggers, and let neither fear nor euphoria set the timing.
