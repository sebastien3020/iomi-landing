# Emergency Funds for Founders: How Many Months Is Enough?

The standard advice — three to six months of expenses in a savings account — was written for people with paychecks. An **emergency fund for founders** has to solve a harder problem: your income, your largest asset, and your daily attention are all concentrated in the same company. When something goes wrong with the business, it usually goes wrong with all three at once. The salaried professional's emergency is losing a job while their investments keep compounding. The founder's emergency is a down quarter that cuts their income at the exact moment their net worth drops and their stress peaks.

So the honest answer to "how many months is enough?" is not a single number. It is a method: understand why your risk is correlated, measure your real personal burn (not the subsidized version), size the buffer against your specific volatility, and then — this is the step most founders skip — cap it, because every dollar past the right size is idle capital dragging on your whole balance sheet. This article walks through each step.

## Key Takeaways

* The 3–6 month rule assumes your income and your assets fail independently. For founders they fail together, which argues for a larger personal buffer than standard advice suggests.
* Your real personal burn is often understated because the business quietly covers costs — measure what your household would spend if the company covered nothing.
* Business runway and personal runway are separate funds in separate compartments; personal reserves held inside the company are not personal reserves.
* Size by scenario, not by rule of thumb: months to replace your income or stabilize the business, times monthly burn, adjusted for your revenue volatility and obligations.
* An emergency fund has a right size — beyond it, extra months of cash become idle capital, one of the five factors that drag a Capital Efficiency Score down.

## Why the Standard Rule Breaks for Founders

The classic emergency fund logic rests on an assumption nobody states: that your income source and your asset base are uncorrelated. An accountant who loses her job still owns her index funds, and their value doesn't depend on her employment. Her emergency fund only needs to bridge the gap between jobs.

A founder's balance sheet violates the assumption completely. Consider what a serious business problem — a lost anchor client, a failed raise, a market turn — does simultaneously: your salary or distributions shrink or stop; the value of your largest asset, your equity, falls; your ability to sell that asset, already limited, gets worse precisely because the business is struggling; and your attention gets consumed by the fire, making side income unrealistic. Four hits, one cause.

This correlation is the single most important fact about founder emergency planning, and it cuts one way: the buffer needs to be bigger and more genuinely liquid than the salaried version. It is the same structural fact that drives [concentration risk for founders](/blog/concentration-risk-for-founders-when-your-company-is-80-of-your-net-worth) — the emergency fund is, in effect, the liquid counterweight to a concentrated illiquid position.

## Step One: Find Your Real Personal Burn

Every sizing formula multiplies months by monthly spend, so an error in monthly spend corrupts everything downstream. Founders systematically understate this number for one reason: the business quietly subsidizes their life. The phone, the car, the travel that is partly personal, the health insurance, the software subscriptions, the meals — costs that would land on the household budget if the company stopped covering them.

The number you want is *unsubsidized burn*: what your household spends per month if the business covers nothing. Go through three months of personal transactions, then add everything the business currently pays that you would still need. For founders who run clean compartments — separate accounts, deliberate pay, the system described in [Separating Personal and Business Finances](/blog/separating-personal-and-business-finances-the-founders-system) — this is a twenty-minute exercise, because the boundary already makes the subsidies visible. For founders with commingled finances, this exercise is usually the moment they discover their lifestyle costs 30–50% more than they thought. Whatever your number is, get it real before you size anything.

## Step Two: Separate the Two Runways

"Emergency fund" for a founder is actually two funds with different owners, different failure modes, and different homes.

**The business reserve** belongs to the company. It answers: how many months can this company make payroll and keep the lights on if revenue stops or a major payment is late? Its size depends on revenue volatility, fixed obligations, and how quickly costs could be cut. It lives in the company's accounts and exists to protect the company.

**The personal reserve** belongs to your household. It answers: how long does my family run if the business pays me nothing? It lives in your personal accounts, full stop.

The common failure is merging them — keeping "the buffer" inside the business because that's where cash accumulates, and mentally counting it as personal safety. It isn't. Cash inside the company is subject to the company's needs and creditors first, and pulling it out on short notice has consequences that depend on your entity structure and tax situation — a conversation for your CPA, and the middle of an emergency is a bad time to have it for the first time. If the money is your family's runway, it should sit on your family's side of the boundary before the emergency, not after.

## Step Three: Size by Scenario, Not by Rule

With real burn measured and the two funds separated, size the personal reserve against your actual situation. Three factors move the number.

**Recovery time.** If your business failed outright, how long until you replace the income — through a turnaround, a new venture, or employment? Founders with deep networks and hireable skills might honestly say six months. Founders in niche markets, or whose plan B is starting another company, should think twelve to twenty-four. The correlated-risk problem means you should assume the recovery happens in a bad environment, not a good one.

**Income volatility today.** A founder taking a stable salary from a company with 18 months of runway faces different odds than one living on irregular distributions from a services firm with two anchor clients. The lumpier and more fragile the income, the more months the buffer earns. (Founders with genuinely irregular income have a related planning problem beyond emergencies — the [cash-flow forecasting guide for irregular income](/blog/cash-flow-forecasting-for-founders-with-irregular-income) covers it.)

**Fixed obligations and dependents.** A mortgage, a family, tuition — obligations that can't flex compress your options and argue for more months. A single founder with a flexible lease can honestly run leaner.

A reasonable synthesis: **six months of unsubsidized burn is a floor for most founders, twelve is a common target, and eighteen to twenty-four fits founders with dependents, fragile revenue, or long expected recovery times.** These are wider ranges than consumer advice gives, and the correlation argument is why. The right number for you is a scenario calculation, not a percentile of what other people hold.

## Step Four: Cap It — The Idle Capital Problem

Here is the counterweight, and the reason this article carries a capital-efficiency tag: past the right size, every additional month of cash is a cost.

Cash is insurance, and insurance has a premium — the return that money isn't earning deployed elsewhere. A founder holding thirty months of burn "to be safe" isn't safer than one holding fourteen; they're paying an unnecessary premium every year, compounding. Excess buffer is one of the most common forms of idle capital on founder balance sheets, partly because it feels virtuous. Fear reads as prudence.

Two practical notes on the capped fund. Keep it genuinely liquid — the point of this money is availability in a bad week, so reaching for yield with lockups or market exposure defeats it. And where you park it and what it earns is a decision worth making once, deliberately, rather than defaulting to whatever account the cash accumulated in.

A useful annual ritual: re-run the scenario math and write the answer down. "Fourteen months at $X burn, because recovery estimate Y and obligations Z." When the number has a stated rationale, next year's review is a comparison against reasons rather than a fresh bout of anxiety — and cutting an oversized buffer stops feeling reckless, because you can see exactly which scenario the extra months were never protecting against.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many months should a founder's emergency fund cover?

Wider ranges than standard advice: six months of true personal burn as a floor, twelve as a common target, eighteen to twenty-four for founders with dependents, volatile revenue, or long expected recovery times. The reason founders need more than the salaried 3–6 is correlation — a business problem cuts your income and your largest asset's value simultaneously, and recovery likely happens in a bad environment.

### Should my emergency fund sit in my business or personal accounts?

Personal reserves belong in personal accounts, before the emergency. Cash inside the company answers to the company's needs and creditors first, and extracting it under pressure raises entity and tax questions best handled by your CPA in calm conditions, not urgent ones. Run two funds: a business reserve sized for the company's obligations, in the company; a personal reserve sized for household runway, in the household.

### How do I calculate my real monthly burn as a founder?

Measure unsubsidized burn: three months of personal transactions, plus everything the business currently pays that your household would still need — insurance, phone, car, travel, subscriptions. Founders with commingled finances typically discover their real burn is 30–50% higher than their estimate, which silently undersizes every buffer calculation built on it.

### Can an emergency fund be too big?

Yes, and for founders it often is. Beyond the months your worst realistic scenario requires, additional cash is idle capital — insurance premium paid for coverage you don't need, compounding as opportunity cost every year. IOMI's Capital Efficiency Score treats this as a measurable factor: right-sized liquidity supports the score, while idle capital beyond it drags the score down.

### Where should founders keep their emergency fund?

Somewhere genuinely liquid — the defining requirement is availability in a bad week, so lockups and market exposure defeat the purpose. Which specific accounts and yields make sense is a decision to make deliberately for your situation. The structural rule matters more than the venue: personal compartment, visible against your real burn, reviewed when your circumstances change.

### Does the emergency fund replace insurance?

No — they cover different failures. Insurance transfers specific large risks (health, disability, liability, property); the emergency fund covers income interruption and the deductible-sized gaps insurance leaves. Founders sometimes over-build the cash buffer while under-insuring correlated personal risks like disability. Review both together, with appropriate professionals, rather than letting cash stand in for coverage.
