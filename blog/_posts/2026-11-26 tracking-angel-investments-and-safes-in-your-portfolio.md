# Tracking Angel Investments and SAFEs in Your Portfolio

Ask a founder what their angel portfolio is worth and you will usually get one of two wrong answers. The optimist marks every SAFE at the last round's valuation and quotes a number that assumes every startup exits at its high-water mark. The nihilist writes the whole thing to zero and stops thinking about it. Honest **angel investment tracking** lives between those poles — and it matters more than the dollar amounts suggest, because angel checks interact with everything else on your balance sheet: your concentration, your liquidity picture, and your sense of how much risk you are actually carrying.

This guide covers the mechanics that make an angel portfolio legible: what a SAFE actually is on your balance sheet, the three defensible valuation policies and how to choose one, why the power law means your "portfolio value" is a fiction until it isn't, and how to fold twenty illiquid line items into a net worth picture you can trust. The goal is not precision — precision is unavailable in this asset class. The goal is a portfolio you can see, valued by rules you set in advance, so the number means something when you look at it.

## Key Takeaways

* A SAFE is not equity yet: it is a contract that converts into shares at a future priced round. Until conversion, you hold a right, not stock, and your tracking should reflect what you paid, not an imagined share count.
* Pick one valuation policy — hold at what you paid, mark at the last priced round, or mark with a discount — write it down, and apply it to every position identically. The policy matters less than the consistency.
* The power law means most checks go to zero and one or two drive the outcome. A portfolio "worth" $400k on paper is really a distribution of outcomes, and your balance sheet should carry it with humility.
* Angel positions are illiquid for years, often a decade. They belong in your net worth under private equity, and outside every calculation that depends on money you can reach.
* The real payoff of disciplined tracking is decision quality: knowing your total exposure, your pacing, and your actual results turns angel investing from a series of vibes into a strategy you can evaluate.

## What You Actually Own When You Sign a SAFE

Start with the instrument, because tracking something you misunderstand produces confident nonsense.

A SAFE — Simple Agreement for Future Equity — is not stock. It is a contract: you give the company money now, and in exchange you receive the right to shares when a triggering event happens, usually the next priced equity round. The valuation cap and discount define the conversion mechanics. Until that trigger, you own no shares, hold no shareholder rights in the traditional sense, and appear on no cap table as an equity holder.

This has direct tracking consequences. Before conversion, there is no share count to multiply by a price — the only hard number attached to the position is what you paid. When a priced round arrives, the SAFE converts, and you now own actual shares whose quantity depends on the cap, the discount, and the round price. At that point your position has a share count and a reference price, and marking becomes possible, though still speculative.

Convertible notes behave similarly with the addition of interest and a maturity date. Priced-round equity — when you invest directly in a Series A, for example — is the straightforward case: shares at a known price from day one.

So a real angel portfolio is a mix: unconverted SAFEs carried at what you paid, converted positions with share counts and a last-round reference, maybe a note or two, and a graveyard. Each type needs its own handling, which is exactly why a spreadsheet with one "value" column drifts into fiction.

## The Three Valuation Policies (Pick One, Write It Down)

There is no objectively correct way to value an illiquid minority position in a private startup. There are three defensible policies. Sophisticated funds spend enormous effort on this problem; you need a rule you can apply in five minutes per position per quarter.

### Policy 1: Carry at what you paid

Every position sits at the amount invested until there is a realization event — an exit, a secondary sale you actually complete, or a shutdown (then zero). Markups are ignored entirely.

This is maximum conservatism. Your portfolio number is the amount you have deployed and not yet lost, full stop. The cost: it hides genuine progress. A company that has raised three up rounds since your check is carried identically to one that has gone silent — informative about your risk, silent about your results.

### Policy 2: Mark to the last priced round

Converted positions get marked at the most recent round's share price; unconverted SAFEs at what you paid until conversion. This is roughly how venture funds report, and it uses the only external price signal that exists.

The trap is treating the mark as money. A last-round price is what one investor paid for preferred shares with rights you do not have, in a negotiated transaction that may be years stale. Startup valuations also fall — down rounds and shutdowns will hit a marked-up portfolio hard, and a number that only ratcheted upward was never honest.

### Policy 3: Mark to last round with a standing discount

The middle path: apply the last-round mark, then haircut it — commonly 25–50% — to acknowledge illiquidity, preference stacks, and staleness. Update marks only on new priced rounds; write positions down on visible distress, not just at shutdown.

Which to choose? If angel checks are a small satellite of your wealth, policy 1 keeps life simple. If the portfolio is large enough to influence decisions, policy 3 usually gives the best balance of information and honesty. The non-negotiable part is uniformity: one policy, all positions, written down, applied on a schedule. The moment you mark winners optimistically and losers charitably, your portfolio number becomes a mood.

## The Power Law Makes Your Total a Fiction (Carry It Anyway)

Angel outcomes follow a power law: across a typical portfolio, most checks return nothing, a few return modest multiples, and one big winner — if you get one — dominates the entire result. This is not pessimism; it is the observed shape of early-stage returns, and it changes what your portfolio total means.

A portfolio of twenty positions "worth" $500k under policy 3 is not like $500k of index funds. It is a probability distribution wearing a number: plausibly worth zero, plausibly worth several times the mark, and almost certainly not worth the mark itself. The single number is a bookkeeping convenience, not a valuation.

So why track a number at all? Because the alternatives are worse, and because the number does jobs that don't require it to be precise. It bounds your exposure: knowing you have $300k deployed across angel checks — versus vaguely "some angel stuff" — is what lets you decide whether the next check fits. It reveals concentration: your angel book plus your own company equity is a combined bet on early-stage tech that should be visible in one place, a cousin of the [concentration problem founders carry in their own company](/blog/diversifying-out-of-your-own-company-without-losing-conviction). And it enables honest scorekeeping over the decade the asset class actually takes: deployed, returned, written off, remaining — the four numbers that eventually tell you whether you are any good at this.

## A Quarterly Routine That Takes Twenty Minutes

Angel portfolios decay into illegibility through neglect, not complexity. A minimal quarterly routine prevents it.

**Update events, not vibes.** Scan for triggers since last quarter: priced rounds (convert your SAFEs on paper — compute the share count from cap and discount), new financings (update marks per your policy), shutdowns (write to zero, promptly — the graveyard is data), and any distributions or completed secondary sales (record proceeds against the position).

**Reconcile the four numbers.** Total deployed, total returned, current carrying value, positions written off. These four, tracked over years, are your actual track record.

**Check pacing and exposure.** Compare deployed capital against the allocation you intended. Angel investing has a way of growing check by check past any plan — the quarterly view is where you notice.

**File the paper.** SAFEs, side letters, conversion notices — one folder per company. Future-you, at conversion or exit or during diligence, will need documents that are miserable to reconstruct years later.

Twenty minutes, four times a year, and your angel portfolio stays a portfolio — visible in your [net worth system of record](/blog/the-modern-net-worth-tracker-a-founders-guide-to-wealth-intelligence) — instead of a shoebox of optimistic memories. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I value a SAFE in my portfolio?

Before conversion, carry it at what you paid — there is no share count and no market price, so the invested amount is the only honest number. After a priced round converts it into shares, you can mark it against that round's price, ideally with a standing discount for illiquidity and staleness. Whatever policy you choose, apply it identically to every position.

### Should I count angel investments in my net worth?

Yes, in a private equity category, at a conservative mark under a consistent policy — excluding them understates your wealth and hides real concentration. The critical discipline is separation: angel positions belong in total net worth but never in liquidity calculations, because converting them to cash typically takes years and is not under your control.

### What percentage of my wealth should be in angel investments?

There is no universal number, and IOMI does not give personalized allocation advice. The useful discipline is deciding your exposure deliberately — as a share of your liquid wealth, not your total — writing it down, and checking deployment against it quarterly. Remember that your own company equity is part of the same early-stage risk bucket; the combined concentration is what deserves attention.

### How do I track SAFEs before they convert?

Record the instrument facts — amount, date, valuation cap, discount, company — and carry the position at the invested amount. When a priced round triggers conversion, compute your share count from the cap and discount, and from then on track it as equity with a share count and reference price. Keep the signed documents filed per company; you will need them at conversion and exit.

### What happens to my tracking when a startup shuts down?

Write the position to zero promptly and keep it visible as a realized loss rather than deleting it. The graveyard is essential data: your true track record is deployed versus returned across everything, including failures. Portfolios that quietly drop losers report fictional performance to their own owner.
