# The Best Quicken Alternatives for Net Worth Tracking

The search for a **quicken alternative** usually starts with respect, not frustration. Quicken has been the default of personal finance software for decades, and for good reason: it is genuinely comprehensive, it has outlasted nearly every competitor, and people have run their financial lives on it since the nineties. But "comprehensive" and "decades" cut both ways. Its center of gravity remains desktop software with a register-and-reconcile workflow, and if what you actually need in 2026 is net worth tracking — a live picture of everything you own across banks, brokerages, crypto, real estate, and maybe a business — you may be maintaining a lot of software to get a number it was never really designed around.

This guide takes the honest path through the alternatives. Different tools won this category for different users, so instead of a ranked list pretending one winner fits everyone, we'll match each serious option — Monarch, Copilot, Kubera, Empower's dashboard, spreadsheets, and IOMI — to the person it is actually best for. Including the cases where that person is not an IOMI customer.

## Key Takeaways

* Quicken remains strong for people who want deep, register-level control of their finances in desktop software — leave it only if your actual job-to-be-done has changed.
* Most Quicken switchers discover their real need is one of three different jobs: budgeting and expense management, simple all-asset net worth tracking, or wealth intelligence across personal and business.
* For budgeting, Monarch is genuinely the best pick — better than Quicken and better than IOMI, which is not a budgeting app and doesn't pretend to be.
* For high-net-worth asset aggregation with a clean, minimal philosophy, Kubera is a credible specialist; for polished automatic spending tracking, Copilot is.
* IOMI is the pick when net worth tracking is the start rather than the end: entity separation for business owners, a Capital Efficiency Score, deterministic simulation, and an AI that answers only from your real data.

## Why People Leave Quicken (It's Not the Product's Fault)

Talk to long-time Quicken users who left and a pattern emerges: the product didn't get worse — the user's life changed shape.

Quicken's model is the electronic register: accounts, transactions, categories, reconciliation. It rewards diligence and gives back control. That model fits beautifully when your financial life is accounts and transactions. It strains when your net worth stops being account-shaped: a stake in your own company, rental property, crypto split across an exchange and two wallets, an LLC or three. You can represent some of this in Quicken with manual accounts and discipline, but you are working against the grain — the software wants to reconcile registers, and you want to see a balance sheet.

The second driver is maintenance appetite. Quicken's depth assumes you'll show up regularly to categorize and reconcile. A growing group of users — founders and operators especially — want the opposite trade: less input, more synthesis. Not "here are your transactions, sorted," but "here is your position, and here is what's drifting."

Neither driver makes Quicken bad. If you love the register model and your finances fit it, staying is a defensible choice. The rest of this guide is for everyone who answered otherwise — sorted by what you're actually replacing it *with*.

## First, Decide Which Job You're Hiring For

The search term "**quicken alternative**" hides three distinct jobs, and mismatching job to tool causes most switcher regret:

**Job 1: Budgeting and expense management.** Where does money go, are we within plan, which subscriptions crept in. Transaction-centric, household-oriented.

**Job 2: Net worth aggregation.** One number and one page for everything owned and owed, updated automatically, minimal fuss.

**Job 3: Wealth intelligence.** Aggregation plus analysis: allocation and concentration, business and personal separated, what-if modeling, an assistant that can answer questions about your actual position.

Quicken partially serves all three, which is exactly why leaving it feels confusing — no single alternative replaces all of it, and the right move is usually to pick the job that matters most and accept a narrower, better tool. Here is the honest map.

## For Budgeting: Monarch (Yes, Really)

If Job 1 is your job, the best quicken alternative is Monarch — buy it and stop reading, including stop reading about IOMI, which would be the wrong purchase.

Monarch took the spot Mint vacated and earned it: collaborative budgeting that two partners can actually share, clean transaction categorization, flexible budget structures, goals tied to spending behavior, and a design that makes a weekly money check-in something people actually do. As a household money-management system, it is better at that job than Quicken's budget features and meaningfully better than anything IOMI offers, because IOMI deliberately doesn't compete there — it is not a budgeting app, and expense categorization is not its center.

Monarch's limits appear exactly where its focus pays off elsewhere: it is transaction- and cash-flow-centric, so a balance sheet full of business entities, private equity, and mixed crypto custody is outside its core design. If your finances are household-shaped, that limit is irrelevant and Monarch is the best Quicken successor available. If your finances are founder-shaped, keep reading. The detailed line-by-line is at [IOMI vs Monarch](https://iomifinance.com/compare/monarch).

## For Automatic Spending Clarity: Copilot

Copilot approaches money management from the opposite end of Quicken: instead of rewarding manual diligence, it invests heavily in automation — machine-learned categorization that improves with light correction, a polished native app experience, and a design philosophy of "glance, understand, move on." For someone leaving Quicken because the maintenance became a chore, Copilot's near-zero-input spending picture is the direct antidote, and its execution is excellent.

The trade is depth on the balance-sheet side: Copilot is strongest as a personal spending-and-accounts app, not as a system for entities, private holdings, and simulation. Think of it as the best answer to "I want to *see* my money with zero effort," rather than "I want to *interrogate* my wealth." Full comparison: [IOMI vs Copilot](https://iomifinance.com/compare/copilot).

## For Minimalist All-Asset Aggregation: Kubera

Kubera deserves real credit for defining Job 2. It set out to be the balance sheet Quicken never quite was: every asset class in one list — bank accounts, brokerages, crypto, real estate, and more — with a philosophy of deliberate minimalism. No budgets, no transaction feeds to groom, no gamification. Add everything, see the total, done. For high-net-worth individuals who want a dignified, comprehensive net worth statement and nothing else, Kubera is a genuinely strong choice and the closest thing to a purpose-built Job 2 specialist.

The philosophical difference with IOMI is what happens *after* aggregation. Kubera's answer is: nothing — the list is the product, and its restraint is intentional. IOMI's answer is that the consolidated balance sheet is the input, not the output: it feeds a Capital Efficiency Score grading how well capital is deployed across liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration; a Simulator for modeling moves deterministically before making them; Projection and Goals for the forward path; and IOMI AI answering questions from your actual holdings. Whether that layer is essential or unnecessary is a fair fork in the road — the case for each side is at [IOMI vs Kubera](https://iomifinance.com/compare/kubera).

## For Free: Empower's Dashboard and Spreadsheets

Two honorable mentions, both free, both real options.

**Empower Personal Dashboard** (the former Personal Capital) offers genuinely useful free tools — net worth aggregation, portfolio views, retirement planning calculators. The economics to understand: the free dashboard is the top of a funnel for Empower's advisory business, so expect outreach about wealth management services. If you're comfortable with that trade and your needs fit its investor-centric frame, it is a legitimate free answer to Job 2.

**Spreadsheets** remain the most customized net worth tracker ever built — yours. Total flexibility, zero subscription, and for a static balance sheet reviewed quarterly, honestly sufficient. The failure mode is well documented: manual updates stop happening, holdings-level detail never gets entered, and the sheet quietly becomes a record of the past. Spreadsheets suit people whose finances are simple or whose maintenance discipline is genuinely exceptional; the moment you want live data across many accounts, you've outgrown the sheet.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best Quicken alternative for net worth tracking?

It depends on which job you're hiring for. For household budgeting and expense management, Monarch is the strongest pick. For effortless automatic spending clarity, Copilot. For minimalist all-asset aggregation, Kubera. For free, Empower's dashboard (with advisory outreach as the trade) or a spreadsheet (with maintenance as the trade). For wealth intelligence — entity separation, capital efficiency scoring, simulation, a data-grounded AI — IOMI. Matching tool to job matters more than any ranking.

### Is Quicken still worth using in 2026?

For its core user, yes. If you value register-level control, deep transaction history, and desktop software you own the workflow of, Quicken remains the most comprehensive tool of its kind, and leaving it for fashion is a mistake. The case for switching is a changed job-to-be-done: wealth that stopped being account-shaped (business equity, property, crypto, entities) or a maintenance appetite that stopped matching Quicken's assumptions.

### What's the difference between a budgeting app and a net worth tracker?

A budgeting app is transaction-centric: it categorizes spending, manages plans, and answers "where does the money go?" A net worth tracker is balance-sheet-centric: it aggregates everything owned and owed and answers "where do I stand?" The tools genuinely diverge — Monarch leads the first category; Kubera and IOMI live in the second, with IOMI adding the analysis layer (efficiency scoring, simulation, forecasting) on top of aggregation.

### Can IOMI replace Quicken for budgeting?

No, and it doesn't try to. IOMI is not a budgeting app — it has no category-limit budget workflow, and expense management is not its focus. If detailed budgeting is central to why you use Quicken, Monarch is the better successor for that job. IOMI replaces Quicken for people whose actual need is the balance sheet: consolidated net worth across personal and business, allocation and concentration, and forward modeling.

### How does IOMI handle assets Quicken struggles with?

Business entities each get their own compartment, kept separate from personal and consolidated on demand. Real estate carries live valuations. Crypto covers exchange accounts and wallets, classified as crypto wherever it's held. Private and company equity, collectibles, and liabilities are first-class entries. Everything connectable syncs read-only through Plaid; everything else is a managed manual entry rather than a workaround.

### Is my data safe when switching from desktop software to a cloud tool?

The architectural questions to ask of any cloud tool: Is access read-only? Where do credentials go? Is there custody? IOMI's answers: connections are read-only through Plaid — balances and transactions visible, moving money impossible; your bank credentials are entered in the institution's flow and never seen or stored by IOMI; and nothing is custodied. The trade against desktop software is local control for live aggregation — a real trade, but not a security downgrade when the architecture is right.

### Do any of these tools give financial advice?

No — and be wary of any that blur the line. Monarch, Copilot, and Kubera are tracking and management tools. Empower's free dashboard sits atop a paid advisory business, which is its funnel. IOMI computes — scores, simulations, projections — deterministically from your data and explains the results, but it does not provide personalized investment advice; decisions, and advisors if you want one, remain yours.

IOMI is not a bank, broker or financial advisor. It does not custody assets, execute trades or provide personalized investment advice. All outputs are educational.
