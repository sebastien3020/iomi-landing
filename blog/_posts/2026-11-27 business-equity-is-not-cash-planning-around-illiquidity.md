# Business Equity Is Not Cash: Planning Around Illiquidity

The **business equity liquidity** problem has a familiar shape: a founder who is worth several million on paper and hesitates before a five-figure expense. The net worth statement says wealthy. The checking account says careful. Both are telling the truth, and the distance between them is the most misunderstood number in a founder's financial life.

Business equity is real wealth. It is also wealth you cannot spend, cannot rebalance, and cannot access on your own schedule — a claim on a future transaction that has not happened yet, at a price nobody has agreed to. This article is about planning around that fact deliberately: how to think about the paper–cash gap, how to size personal runway when your biggest asset is frozen, which mistakes founders make when they treat equity as money, and how to keep both truths visible at once instead of oscillating between feeling rich and feeling broke.

## Key Takeaways

* Net worth and spendable wealth are different quantities; for most founders the majority of net worth cannot be converted to cash within a year, and some of it may never be.
* The liquid/illiquid split deserves as much attention as the headline number — it determines your real risk capacity, runway, and negotiating position.
* Personal runway must be funded from liquid assets only; equity-rich founders with thin cash buffers are fragile in exactly the moments the business is too.
* Illiquid wealth concentrated in one company means your public portfolio and cash should be built more conservatively, not less.
* Track both numbers continuously and model liquidity events deterministically before they arrive — decisions made inside a transaction are worse than decisions modeled ahead of it.

## The Two Numbers That Get Confused

Every founder carries two balance sheets, whether or not they have written either down.

The first is total net worth: everything you own minus everything you owe, with the business at a documented estimate. This number is real and worth tracking honestly — it drives long-horizon decisions, measures whether the years of work are compounding, and determines what is at stake in an exit.

The second is liquid net worth: what could become cash within roughly 30 days without a forced discount. Bank balances, public brokerage positions, most crypto on exchanges. Not the company, not the real estate, not the LP positions, not the collectibles.

For a salaried professional the two numbers sit close together. For a founder they can differ by 5x or 10x, and the confusion between them causes opposite errors. Founders who anchor on total net worth overspend, under-save, and take casual risks with cash because "I'm worth millions." Founders who anchor on the checking account under-invest in their own lives and businesses out of a scarcity feeling the balance sheet does not justify. The cure for both is the same: see the two numbers side by side, continuously, and let each drive the decisions it actually governs. The deeper treatment of this split is in [liquid vs illiquid net worth](/blog/liquid-vs-illiquid-net-worth-why-the-split-matters); this article is about planning around it.

## Why Equity Is So Much More Frozen Than It Feels

Founders systematically underestimate how illiquid their equity is, because the number on the balance sheet updates like an asset while behaving like a promise. It is worth being concrete about the mechanics.

**There is no market.** Public stock sells in seconds at a visible price. Private equity sells when a specific buyer materializes, negotiates, diligences, and closes — a process measured in quarters, with meaningful odds of dying at every stage.

**Selling has gates.** Shareholder agreements, transfer restrictions, investor consent rights, rights of first refusal — most private equity cannot legally change hands without permissions you do not unilaterally control. Partial sales (secondaries) exist for some venture-backed companies, but they are episodic, discounted, and typically capped.

**The price is a hypothesis.** Until a transaction prices it, your equity value is an estimate resting on a method — and buyers apply discounts for illiquidity and control that founders' own estimates usually omit. If you have not pressure-tested the number, the guide on [how to value a private business](/blog/how-to-value-a-private-business-for-your-personal-net-worth) covers doing it honestly.

**The correlation is total.** Here is the sharpest edge: the scenarios in which you most need to convert equity to cash — the business struggling, you burned out, a downturn — are precisely the scenarios in which the equity is worth the least and hardest to sell. Equity fails as a reserve asset because its value collapses exactly when reserves are needed. This is why liquidity planning cannot lean on the business at all.

## Sizing Personal Runway When the Big Asset Is Frozen

The first practical consequence: your personal cash buffer must be funded entirely from the liquid side, sized as if the equity did not exist.

Standard advice says three to six months of expenses. Founders need more, for stacked reasons: your income and your largest asset share one failure mode (the business), your income is lumpy rather than salaried, and personal thin-ness has a nasty feedback loop — a founder with no personal runway makes desperate business decisions, takes bad terms, and sells things at the wrong time. Twelve months of personal expenses in genuinely liquid form is a defensible founder baseline; more if the business is volatile or concentrated in a few customers.

Two boundaries keep the runway honest:

**The business's buffer is not yours.** Cash inside the company belongs to the company's risk profile — payroll, obligations, its own volatility. Counting the operating account in your personal runway double-books the same dollars against two different disasters that tend to arrive together.

**Credit is a bridge, not a buffer.** Lines of credit help with timing, but they are commitments that must be repaid from — again — liquidity, and they have a way of being reduced or called in the same bad weather that made you need them.

## Let Illiquidity Reshape the Liquid Portfolio

The second consequence is less intuitive: because most of your wealth is one illiquid, volatile, concentrated position, the liquid remainder should usually be built more conservatively than a diversification-minded professional's portfolio — not less.

Think of your total balance sheet as one portfolio. The equity slice is high-risk, high-concentration, zero-liquidity. If the liquid slice is also aggressive — concentrated stock bets, illiquid alternatives, long lockups — the whole is a barbell with no handle: nothing stable anywhere. The liquid side is carrying jobs the equity cannot do: emergency reserve, opportunity fund, diversifier, sleep aid.

Three planning rules follow:

* **Diversify away from your own sector.** Your equity is a leveraged bet on your industry. Liquid holdings that repeat the bet (your sector's stocks, your customers' stocks) compound the concentration invisibly.
* **Price liquidity into every new commitment.** Each angel check, LP position, or real estate deal moves dollars from the spendable column to the frozen one. The question is never only "is this a good investment" but "can my liquid share afford another freeze?"
* **Watch the ratio, not just the total.** A year in which net worth grows while the liquid share shrinks from 25% to 12% is a year your risk profile deteriorated while the headline improved.

## Planning the Thaw: Liquidity Events

Illiquidity is not forever — it ends at a transaction. Exits, partial secondaries, meaningful distributions: these are the moments the paper number becomes real money, and they reward planning years ahead.

**Model scenarios before they are offers.** The time to know what a $2M secondary does to your life is before anyone offers one. Deterministic simulation is the right tool: take today's actual holdings, apply the event — sale proceeds in, equity stake down, deployment plan out — and look at the year-by-year path. **Pre-decide the deployment.** Money that arrives without a plan gets allocated by mood. Decide the runway top-up, the diversification tranche, and the discretionary slice while the event is hypothetical.

**Scope the professionals early.** Transaction structuring, timing, and the tax dimensions of a sale are CPA and attorney territory, and the expensive mistakes happen when they are engaged after terms are set. **Don't count it until it closes.** Deals die in diligence routinely. A liquidity event is a probability until funds settle — plans that spend it early convert illiquidity risk into commitment risk.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why shouldn't I treat my business equity like cash?

Because it fails every test cash passes: it cannot be spent, cannot be converted quickly, has no agreed price until a transaction sets one, and — critically — tends to lose value in exactly the scenarios where you would need to draw on reserves. Equity is real wealth with a different job: long-horizon compounding, not liquidity. Plans that assign it cash's job break precisely when tested.

### How much liquid runway should a founder keep?

Around twelve months of personal expenses in genuinely liquid form is a defensible baseline — more than standard advice, because a founder's income and largest asset share the same failure mode. Fund it entirely from personal liquid assets: business cash belongs to the business's risks, and credit lines are bridges that can be withdrawn in bad weather, not buffers.

### What percentage of net worth should be liquid?

There is no universal number — early-stage founders are often unavoidably above 80% illiquid — but the ratio should be a tracked, deliberate quantity rather than an accident. Watch its direction: a growing net worth with a shrinking liquid share is a deteriorating risk position wearing a good headline. Each new illiquid commitment should be checked against what it does to the ratio.

### How do I put a number on my business equity in the first place?

Use a documented, conservative method — last priced round adjusted for preferences and staleness, or a revenue/earnings multiple with honest discounts for illiquidity and key-person dependence — and update it on a schedule with the same method each time. Consistency matters more than precision for tracking. Formal valuations for sales, buyouts, or estate purposes are professional work.

### Should my investment portfolio be more aggressive because I'm young, or more conservative because of my equity?

Viewed as one total portfolio, most founders already hold an extremely aggressive core position: one concentrated, illiquid private company. The liquid remainder is usually doing the stabilizing jobs — reserve, diversifier, optionality — which argues for building it more conservatively and away from your own sector, whatever your age. The right frame is the whole balance sheet, not the brokerage account in isolation.

### How do I plan for a liquidity event that may or may not happen?

Model it while it is hypothetical: apply the assumed proceeds and equity change to your actual current holdings and look at the year-by-year path — deterministic simulation, not probability theater.
