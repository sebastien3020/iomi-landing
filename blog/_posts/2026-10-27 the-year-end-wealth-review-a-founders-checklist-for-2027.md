# The Year-End Wealth Review: A Founder's Checklist for 2027

The last week of December is one of the few times a founder's calendar goes quiet — and it is the single best window of the year to look at your entire financial life in one sitting. Not to judge it. To *see* it: what you actually own and owe now, how the year's decisions played out, where the risks quietly shifted, and what should be true by next December. A structured **year-end financial review** turns that quiet week into the highest-leverage two hours of your financial year.

This is a working checklist, not a philosophy piece. It is organized into six passes — reconcile, exposure, flows, efficiency, goals, and setup for 2027 — each with concrete items to check off. One scope note before starting: this is an educational wealth review, not tax preparation. Tax questions belong with your CPA, and nothing here replaces that conversation.

## Key Takeaways

* A year-end review is six passes: reconcile the balance sheet, audit exposure, review the year's flows, read your efficiency factors, re-test goals, and set up next year.
* Reconciliation comes first — every later judgment inherits its quality from the numbers being true, current, and complete.
* Exposure questions (concentration, liquidity, leverage) matter more than performance questions, because exposure is what you control.
* Compare year-end against your status-quo trajectory, not just against last January — the honest question is whether your decisions beat doing nothing.
* The best output of a year-end review is a system that makes next year's review almost automatic.

## Before You Start: Assemble the Picture

A review is only as good as the picture it reviews. Founders who track their wealth on connected infrastructure start this step already done; everyone else starts with an evening of assembly.

The completeness bar: every bank and wallet account, every brokerage and retirement account, crypto across exchanges *and* wallets, real estate at current valuations, private and company equity at a defensible mark, physical assets and collectibles at honest estimates, and every liability — mortgages, loans, credit lines, margin. Personal and each business entity, kept in their own compartments but visible together.

If assembling this takes more than an evening, that is itself the first finding of your review: your visibility layer is broken, and fixing it belongs at the top of the 2027 list. With the picture in hand, the six passes.

## Pass One: Reconcile the Balance Sheet

The goal of this pass is a single true statement: *"This is what I own and owe on December 31, 2026."*

**□ Verify every connected account synced recently.** Stale connections are silent — re-link anything that dropped.

**□ Hunt for orphans.** The old 401(k), the dormant exchange account, the business account at a bank you stopped using. Year-end is the annual sweep for accounts that exist but appear nowhere.

**□ Re-mark every manual asset, with basis and date.** Private equity: still marked at a defensible basis (last priced round, 409A, or a conservative multiple), with any 2026 events — a round, a secondary, a material business change — reflected? Real estate: does the carried valuation survive contact with recent local comparables? Collectibles: mark to what the market pays, not what you paid.

**□ Confirm every liability is present and current.** Balances, rates, and terms — rate changes during the year alter more than the payment.

**□ Record the year-end number.** Total net worth, per-compartment, per asset class. This snapshot is what next December's review will be measured against.

The discipline here is honesty over flattery. An aging mark carried forward untouched is the balance-sheet equivalent of an untested backup: comforting until the day it matters.

## Pass Two: Audit Your Exposure

Performance gets all the attention; exposure deserves it. Returns are mostly the market's doing — exposure is yours. Four questions:

**□ Concentration: what fraction rides on one thing?** Compute your largest position — usually the company — as a percentage of total net worth, and compare against last year. Founders' concentration typically *grows* passively, because the biggest asset is often the fastest-growing. The point is not that concentration is wrong; it is that the number should be known and the exposure chosen, not discovered.

**□ Liquidity: how long could you move freely?** Accessible funds versus true monthly burn, remembering that cash inside an operating entity is not the same as cash in your pocket. Check the buffer against known 2027 obligations — capital calls, planned purchases, the lumpy months.

**□ Leverage: what is borrowed against what?** Total debt against total assets, and the specific pairings — the mortgage against the property, any margin against the portfolio. Ask the stress question: which single asset falling 30% would hurt the most, and would it force selling anything else?

**□ Classification: are the labels still true?** Exposure math only works if assets are classified by what they are, not where they sit — crypto at a broker is crypto, a REIT is real estate. One mislabeled account can misstate a whole class.

If your wealth includes significant real estate, private equity, or collectibles, this pass is where consolidation pays off — the walkthrough is in [Consolidating alternative assets in your net worth](/blog/consolidating-alternative-assets-in-your-net-worth).

## Pass Three: Review the Year's Flows

The balance sheet says where you ended; the flows say how you got there. This pass reads 2026 as a story.

**□ Decompose the year's net worth change.** How much came from asset performance versus new savings versus valuation re-marks? A year that "went well" purely on paper marks is a different year than one carried by real cash generation — you want to know which one you had.

**□ Total what each entity paid you.** Salary, distributions, dividends across compartments. Founders frequently misremember their own income by wide margins; the actual number recalibrates every plan built on it.

**□ Face your real burn.** The full-year household number, divided by twelve — including the lumpy items monthly estimates always miss. This annual figure is the honest input for every buffer and runway calculation in 2027.

**□ Revisit the year's big moves.** The reallocation, the property, the angel checks, the position you exited. Not to grade them on outcome — outcomes involve luck — but on process: did you decide with full information, on purpose? Anything decided by default goes on next year's watch list.

**□ Note what surprised you.** The expense category that doubled, the income that arrived late, the call you didn't expect. Surprises are the raw material for better buffers.

## Pass Four: Read Your Efficiency Factors

With truth established and the story understood, grade the arrangement. The Capital Efficiency Score frames this pass: a 0–100 health check of how well capital is deployed, across five factors — liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration. Scored automatically from connected holdings or worked as a manual checklist, the five questions are the same:

**□ Liquidity** — is the buffer sized to your real 2027 obligations, neither starved nor bloated?

**□ Idle capital** — how much cash is sitting with no assigned job? Year-end is when unassigned distributions from months ago get discovered. Every dollar gets a name: buffer, reserve, deploy, or spend.

**□ Drag** — the annual leak hunt: reserves parked below available safe yields, fee-heavy funds duplicating cheap ones, expensive debt carried against low-yield assets, forgotten dust positions and account fees.

**□ Risk balance** — does the allocation across classes match any decision you actually made this year, or has drift been deciding for you?

**□ Concentration** — from Pass Two: is the number known, and is staying at that number a choice you are making on purpose with current data?

Rank the five, then carry the weakest one forward as a named 2027 priority. One factor genuinely improved per year is a better record than five vaguely intended. ## Pass Five: Re-Test Goals Against Trajectory

Now the forward look. Two comparisons make this pass rigorous rather than aspirational.

**□ Score last January's intentions.** What did you mean to be true by now? Hit, missed, or — most often — quietly abandoned in March. Abandoned goals carry the most information: was the goal wrong, or the system for pursuing it?

**□ Compare against the status-quo trajectory.** The sharper question than "did I grow?" is "did my decisions beat doing nothing?" Project what your January 2026 holdings would have produced untouched, and compare. Sobering or encouraging, the answer calibrates how much activity your situation actually rewards.

**□ Set 2027 goals against the projected path.** Run your current holdings forward — a year-by-year status-quo projection — and set goals *relative to it*. A goal the trajectory already reaches is a plan to do nothing (fine, if chosen). A goal far above it demands a named mechanism: what specific change closes the gap?

**□ Simulate the big known decisions.** If 2027 holds a fork — a possible sale, a property, a sabbatical — model it deterministically now, from your reconciled year-end position, while the numbers are fresh. ## Pass Six: Set Up 2027 So Next Year's Review Is Easy

The quiet failure of year-end reviews is that they evaporate by February. The final pass converts findings into standing infrastructure.

**□ Fix the visibility gaps found in Pass One.** Connect the orphans, link what dropped, put update rhythms (with dates) on every manual mark.

**□ Give the year's cash a standing plan.** Where do 2027's distributions land, and what is the default assignment when they do? A pre-decided answer is the difference between deployment and drift.

**□ Name the one factor.** The weakest efficiency factor from Pass Four, written down as the year's single financial priority.

**□ Put the review on rails.** A daily ambient layer (a brief that surfaces material changes, a live cash-flow forecast) plus a one-hour deliberate check-in per quarter. On that cadence, next December's review is a confirmation, not an archaeology dig.

**□ Book the professional conversations.** Your CPA for anything tax-shaped that surfaced during the review, your attorney for anything structural. This checklist finds the questions; those conversations answer them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a year-end financial review?

A structured pass over your complete financial position at year close: reconciling every asset and liability to current, honest values; auditing exposure (concentration, liquidity, leverage); reviewing the year's income, spending, and major decisions; grading how efficiently capital is arranged; and re-testing goals against your actual trajectory before setting next year's.

### How long should a year-end review take?

With connected, current data: about two focused hours across the six passes. If assembly alone takes an evening — hunting statements, rebuilding a spreadsheet — treat that as the review's first finding and make standing visibility the top priority for next year. The review should be judgment time, not gathering time.

### Is this the same as year-end tax planning?

No. This is an educational wealth review — position, exposure, efficiency, goals. Tax planning is a separate exercise with its own deadlines and rules, and it belongs with your CPA. A thorough wealth review will surface questions worth bringing to that conversation, but it does not replace it.

### What's the most important part of the checklist for founders?

The exposure pass, and concentration specifically. A founder's largest position usually grows passively as a share of net worth, so the exposure that defined last year is rarely the exposure you hold now. Knowing the current number — and choosing it deliberately — is worth more than any performance statistic in the review.

### How should I judge whether my year went well financially?

Compare against your status-quo trajectory, not just last January's balance. Project what your start-of-year holdings would have produced if left untouched, and ask whether your decisions beat that path. It is the honest test of activity — and either answer is useful for calibrating how much intervention your situation rewards.

### What should I do with idle cash found during the review?

Assign every dollar a named job: buffer, reserve, deployment, or planned spending. The assignment matters more than speed — "held deliberately until a decision in March" is a plan, while an unassigned balance is quiet decay. Year-end is simply when the unassigned balances from the year's distributions tend to get discovered.
