# How to Improve Your Capital Efficiency Score

Net worth measures how much you have. Capital efficiency measures how hard it works. Two founders with the same $3M balance sheet can be in completely different shape: one holds balanced, deployed, liquid-enough capital; the other has $400k earning nothing in a business checking account and 75% of everything riding on a single asset. If you want to **improve capital efficiency**, the first step is making that difference visible — and the second is working it down, factor by factor.

This guide is the practical playbook. It assumes you know what the Capital Efficiency Score is (if not, start with [the CES explainer](/blog/what-is-a-capital-efficiency-score-the-founders-guide-to-wealth-intelligence)) and focuses on what actually moves it: concrete actions for each of the five factors, in the order most people should tackle them, with the simulation habit that keeps improvements from becoming new problems.

## Key Takeaways

* The Capital Efficiency Score is a 0–100 health check across five factors — liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration — and it improves factor by factor, not all at once.
* Idle capital is usually the fastest win: cash beyond your buffer earning near-zero is pure drag on compounding, and redeploying it is often a one-afternoon fix.
* Liquidity cuts both ways — too little is fragility, too much is idleness. Define your buffer deliberately instead of letting cash accumulate by default.
* Concentration is the founder's structural challenge: you rarely eliminate it, but you can stop compounding it and diversify everything around it.
* Simulate before you move. Deterministic modeling shows how a reallocation changes your score and trajectory before real money goes anywhere.

Once the picture is complete, resist the urge to react to the headline number. A 54 is not an insult; it's a map. Look at the factor breakdown and find the one or two factors doing the damage. One more framing note before the playbook: the score is a means, not the end. The goal is capital that compounds, survives shocks, and matches your risk tolerance. The score is how you check your work.

## Factor One: Fix Liquidity First

Liquidity is the factor to settle before touching anything else, because every other move depends on knowing how much cash must stay put.

Too little liquidity is fragility: a surprise expense or a slow quarter forces you to sell assets at the wrong time. Too much is idleness wearing a safety costume. The failure mode for founders is almost always the second one — cash accumulating in operating and personal accounts by default, because no one ever decided how much was enough.

So decide. Define your personal buffer as a number of months of personal spending — commonly six to twelve, adjusted for how volatile your income is. Define each business entity's buffer as months of operating expenses, adjusted for revenue predictability. Write both numbers down. Everything above them is, by your own definition, deployable — which converts a vague pile of cash into a specific redeployment decision.

Then check the shape of your liquidity, not just the amount. A balance sheet can be large and illiquid: if 85% of your wealth is a company stake and property, your buffer needs to be more conservative because your assets can't be tapped quickly without damage. This interaction between liquidity and concentration is exactly what a factor-based score surfaces and a single net worth number hides. It's also a component of your broader [financial health picture](/blog/financial-health-score-2026-guide-for-entrepreneurs) — runway and resilience first, optimization second.

## Factor Two: Put Idle Capital to Work

With buffers defined, idle capital becomes trivially easy to identify: it's every dollar above the buffer earning materially below what a low-risk deployed alternative would earn. This is usually the fastest score improvement available, and often the largest.

Idle capital hides in predictable places. The business checking account that grew because revenue came in and nobody swept it. The personal savings account at a legacy bank yielding a rounding error. The brokerage cash balance from a sale eight months ago that never got reinvested. The "temporary" parking spot that became permanent. None of these are decisions; they're the absence of decisions.

The fix is mechanical: inventory every cash position across every entity, subtract your defined buffers, and redeploy the remainder according to your allocation targets. What "deployed" means is your call — the point is that it should be a *chosen* position, not a default one. The cost of not choosing compounds quietly: idle cash doesn't show up as a loss on any statement, which is precisely why it persists for years.

Then make the check recurring, because idle capital regenerates — every distribution, exit, and strong quarter creates new cash that sits until someone notices. ## Factor Three: Cut the Drag

Drag is everything that quietly subtracts from returns without buying you anything: fees on products you don't use, expensive fund layers duplicating cheaper exposure, debt whose interest outruns what the borrowed capital earns, redundant accounts each holding their own cash cushion.

Hunting drag is unglamorous, but it's one of the most dependable ways to improve capital efficiency — the wins are small individually and pay like clockwork. Three places to look:

**Recurring costs on the balance sheet.** Account fees, platform fees, management fees stacked on top of fund fees. Each is small; the habit of never auditing them is expensive. A once-a-year sweep through every account's fee schedule usually finds something.

**Liability structure.** List every debt with its rate. High-interest debt against idle cash is the classic self-inflicted wound — you're paying more on one line than you earn on another, for no reason except that the two lines never got looked at together. That's a consolidated-view problem: it's invisible when accounts are checked one at a time and obvious when the whole balance sheet sits in one place.

**Structural redundancy.** Five accounts doing the job of two, each with minimum balances and its own fee schedule. Consolidating cuts both drag and the cognitive load of oversight.

Debt, to be clear, is not automatically drag — leverage that earns more than it costs is deployment. Drag is specifically the negative-carry positions and pure-cost items. The test for any line item: what is this costing per year, and what is it buying? No answer, no line item.

## Factors Four and Five: Rebalance Risk and Concentration

The last two factors are related but distinct. Risk balance asks whether your mix across asset classes matches a deliberate target. Concentration asks how exposed you are to any single position, asset, or entity.

For founders, concentration is the structural one. Your company stake is probably your largest asset, and that's not a mistake to fix — it's the bet you're making on purpose. What the score punishes is unmanaged concentration: not knowing the number, and letting every *other* decision compound it. Practical moves:

* **Measure it honestly.** Cross-account, cross-entity, classified by what assets are. Founders who feel diversified are often surprised: company equity plus company-correlated investments plus real estate in the same city where the company operates is one bet wearing three costumes.
* **Stop adding to the concentrated position by default.** New capital should generally flow toward the underweighted parts of your target mix. This is exactly what Next Dollar Allocation logic is for.
* **Diversify the perimeter.** You may not be able (or willing) to reduce the core position, but everything around it — liquid portfolio, cash deployment, new investments — can be built to offset rather than echo it.
* **Revisit after every valuation change.** A markup in your company's valuation silently increases concentration without a single transaction. Live tracking catches this; annual spreadsheets don't.

Risk balance then follows: set a target mix you can defend, compare actual against it, and correct with flows (directing new money) before resorting to reallocation (moving old money) — flows are cheaper and less disruptive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good Capital Efficiency Score?

There's no universal passing grade — the score is a 0–100 health check meant for comparison against your own past, not a leaderboard. What matters is the factor breakdown and the trend: a 60 with idle capital fixed and concentration falling is better news than a static 70. Most people find one or two factors doing most of the damage, and the score's job is to point at them.

### What's the fastest way to improve capital efficiency?

Usually idle capital. Define liquidity buffers for yourself and each business entity, inventory every cash position across all accounts, and redeploy what's above the buffers into chosen positions. It's often a one-afternoon fix that improves the score immediately and — unlike concentration or risk-balance work — requires no difficult trade-offs. Fee and negative-carry cleanup is the close second.

### How much cash should I keep as a buffer?

A common range is six to twelve months of personal spending, and a comparable operating buffer per business entity — more if income is volatile or your balance sheet is heavily illiquid, less if income is stable and your assets are easy to tap. The specific number matters less than deciding it deliberately: a defined buffer converts everything above it into a visible redeployment decision instead of an ever-growing pile.

### Is founder concentration always bad for the score?

No — concentration is a factor, not a verdict, and for founders a large company stake is usually the deliberate core bet. What hurts is unmanaged concentration: not measuring it across accounts and entities, letting correlated assets pile up around it, and never adjusting as valuations move. You improve the factor by directing new capital to underweighted areas and building the rest of the balance sheet to offset the core position, not necessarily by selling it.

### Does improving my score require selling assets?

Rarely as a first step. Most early improvements come from redeploying idle cash, cutting fees and negative-carry debt, and directing *new* capital toward underweighted areas — flows rather than reallocations. Selling becomes relevant when a factor like concentration can't be corrected at the perimeter, and that's exactly the kind of consequential move worth simulating before executing.
