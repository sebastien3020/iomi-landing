# The Daily Brief: What a 5-Minute Financial Review Looks Like

Most founders check their finances in one of two broken modes: obsessively — refreshing a brokerage app eight times a day — or never, until a quarterly panic forces a weekend of spreadsheet archaeology. A **daily financial review** is the boring, effective alternative: five minutes, once a day, over a complete picture of everything you own. Not to react to every wiggle, but so that nothing material ever surprises you by more than 24 hours.

The catch is the word "complete." Five minutes only works if the picture is already assembled. If your review requires logging into six institutions, converting a currency, and remembering what your company stake is worth, it is not a five-minute habit — it is a chore you will abandon by Thursday. This guide covers what belongs in a daily review, what emphatically does not, and how IOMI's Daily Brief compresses the whole thing into the time it takes to drink the first third of a coffee.

## Key Takeaways

- A daily financial review works only when the data assembles itself: read-only syncing across all accounts turns "log in everywhere" into "read one screen."
- The review is exception-based: you are scanning for what changed materially since yesterday, not re-auditing your entire balance sheet every morning.
- Five minutes splits into three passes — what changed, what needs a decision, what is coming — and most days the honest output is "nothing to do today."
- Daily is for awareness; weekly and monthly are for decisions. Mixing the cadences is how people end up either overtrading or asleep at the wheel.

## Why Daily — and Why Only Five Minutes

The case for daily is not that your net worth needs daily management. It does not. The case is about the cost of surprise. When you look at your finances monthly, every material event — a large payment that did not land, a balance that drifted, a position that quietly doubled its share of your wealth — has up to 30 days to compound before you notice. Problems age badly. A subscription price change is trivial on day one and a budget line by the time a quarterly review catches it.

The case for only five minutes is just as important. Financial attention has diminishing — then negative — returns. Past the point of awareness, more checking produces more twitchiness: reacting to market noise, second-guessing allocations that were fine, treating every red number as a summons to act. The research on investor behavior is consistent on this: the more frequently people check volatile positions, the more they trade, and the worse they generally do.

So the design constraint is precise: maximum awareness, minimum dwell time. That is a genuinely different product than a dashboard you wander around in. It is a brief — in the morning-briefing sense: here is what changed, here is what needs your attention, dismissed.

## The Five Minutes, Minute by Minute

Here is the structure of a **daily financial review** that actually fits in five minutes. Three passes, in order of urgency.

### Minutes 1–2: What changed?

Scan the deltas since yesterday. Net worth moved — by how much, and driven by what? A market move across your brokerage? A currency effect? A large transaction in a business account? The skill here is triage, not analysis: you are sorting changes into "expected" (payroll left the account, an invoice landed) and "unexpected" (a balance that should not have moved, a charge you do not recognize). Expected changes get a nod. Unexpected ones get flagged — not necessarily solved — right now.

This is the pass IOMI's Daily Brief is built for: it surfaces what changed across your entire consolidated picture, so the scan takes a look instead of a hunt.

### Minutes 3–4: What needs a decision?

Most days: nothing, and saying so out loud is the point. Some days: a real item. Cash accumulated past your working threshold and is now idle. A position drifted past the concentration ceiling you set. An invoice is a week overdue. A planned event — quarterly estimated payment, a big purchase — is inside the two-week window.

Notice what these have in common: they are threshold crossings, not fluctuations. A good daily review reacts to lines being crossed, never to wiggles. This is also where the Capital Efficiency Score earns its place in a morning glance — a 0–100 read across liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration. You are not studying it daily; you are noticing when it moves.

### Minute 5: What's coming?

A forward glance at the cash-flow forecast — built from profile income minus expenses plus planned events. Anything large landing or leaving in the next two weeks? Any date you need to move money before (moving money yourself, from your own accounts — IOMI never moves money)? This single minute is what converts the review from a rearview mirror into an early-warning system. ## What a Daily Review Is Not

Boundaries keep the habit healthy. Three things do not belong in the five minutes:

**It is not trading.** The review produces awareness and, occasionally, a flagged decision to make later with a clear head. If your daily review routinely ends in transactions, it has become the twitchiness it was designed to prevent.

**It is not transaction-level bookkeeping.** Categorizing every coffee is a different activity with a different tool — honestly, if line-item household budgeting is your goal, a budgeting app like [Monarch](https://iomifinance.com/compare/monarch) is the better pick for that job. IOMI's review operates at the balance-sheet level: positions, balances, flows, and score. What did my wealth do, not what did I spend on lunch.

**It is not the venue for big decisions.** Reallocations, goal changes, and what-if planning belong in the weekly or monthly sitting, where the Simulator and Projection get the attention they deserve. The daily review can queue those questions; it should not answer them.

## Daily, Weekly, Monthly: The Full Cadence

The five-minute review works because it sits inside a hierarchy. Each layer has a distinct job:

- **Daily (5 minutes):** awareness. What changed, what crossed a threshold, what is coming. Output: usually nothing; occasionally a flagged item.
- **Weekly (20–30 minutes):** decisions. Work the flagged items. Check the cash-flow forecast over a full horizon. Review anything the Daily Brief surfaced repeatedly.
- **Monthly (an hour):** direction. Read the Projection — the year-by-year path from today's holdings. Check Goals against their milestones and the status-quo trajectory. Run Simulator scenarios for any move you are contemplating. Consider the Next Dollar Allocation question if cash has built up.

The pattern to notice: information flows up the cadence, decisions flow down. The daily layer feeds the weekly layer questions; the monthly layer sets thresholds that make the daily layer's triage automatic. Founders who skip the daily layer end up doing painful monthly reconstructions; founders who skip the monthly layer do crisp daily reviews of a portfolio drifting nowhere in particular. - **Anchor it to something you already do.** First coffee, end of the gym session, the minute before the first meeting. The review has no natural trigger of its own; borrow one.
