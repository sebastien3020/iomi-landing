# Risk Balance: Diversification Beyond 60/40 for Founders

The 60/40 portfolio — 60% stocks, 40% bonds — is the default answer to a question founders are not asking. It assumes your wealth arrives as salary, sits in liquid markets, and needs balancing between two asset classes. **Founder diversification** starts from the opposite position: most of your net worth is one illiquid, unhedgeable position — your company — and everything else you own is a satellite orbiting it. Telling a founder to hold 60/40 in their brokerage account is rearranging deck chairs on 15% of their wealth.

This guide is about risk balance as founders actually experience it: concentration by default, income correlated with the same asset you hold, and diversification levers that look nothing like a model portfolio. The goal is not to eliminate concentration — concentration is how founders get wealthy — but to make it a number you chose instead of a number that happened to you.

## Key Takeaways

- 60/40 was designed for salaried investors with liquid portfolios; founders hold their largest position in illiquid company equity, and their income is correlated with it.
- The first step of founder diversification is measurement: classify every holding by what it is — equity, crypto, property, cash, collectibles — and compute what share sits in your single largest exposure.
- Risk balance and concentration are two of the five factors in IOMI's Capital Efficiency Score, alongside liquidity, idle capital, and drag — a 0–100 read on how well your capital is deployed.
- Founders diversify with different levers than employees: what you do with distributions and secondaries matters more than how you split a small brokerage account.
- Concentration is a phase, not a sin. The failure mode is not holding a big position — it is not knowing how big it is, or having no liquidity beside it.

## Why 60/40 Was Never Built for You

The classic balanced portfolio solves a specific problem: smoothing the ride for someone whose entire wealth is in liquid markets and whose income comes from elsewhere. Both assumptions fail for founders.

**Your wealth is not liquid.** Company equity cannot be rebalanced quarterly. There is no sell button. Whatever allocation framework you use has to work around a large position you mostly cannot adjust — which is a constraint 60/40 simply does not model.

**Your income is not independent.** An employee's paycheck survives a market crash. A founder's income comes from the same company as their equity. When the business struggles, the asset falls and the income falls with it — at exactly the moment you might need liquidity. This correlation is the single most under-priced risk in founder finances.

**Your asset mix is wider than two classes.** Founders hold company equity, crypto, real estate, angel positions, collectibles, and cash across personal and business entities. A framework with two sliders cannot describe this, let alone balance it.

None of this makes diversification irrelevant. It makes the textbook version irrelevant. You need a framework that starts from your actual balance sheet.

## Step One: Measure What You Actually Hold

You cannot balance what you cannot see, and most founders genuinely cannot see their allocation. It is scattered across a cap table, two brokerages, an exchange account, a property, and a guess about what the company is worth.

The fix is classification by substance. Crypto held at a broker counts as crypto. Company stock in three different accounts counts, together, as exposure to one company.

Once everything is classified, three numbers define your risk position:

1. **Largest single exposure as a share of net worth.** For most founders this is their company, and the honest number is often 60–90%.
2. **Liquid share.** What could become cash within a week without breaking anything? This is your capacity to absorb a bad year.
3. **Correlated share.** What falls together? Your equity plus your income plus any industry-adjacent angel bets form one cluster, not three positions.

## The Diversification Levers Founders Actually Have

**Founder diversification** does not happen inside the brokerage account. It happens at a handful of decision points, most of them rare, all of them high-stakes.

### The distribution decision

Every time cash leaves the business — a profit distribution, a dividend, a bonus you pay yourself — you choose between reinvesting in the concentrated position or building the diversified base. This is the highest-frequency lever most founders have. The default (leave it in the company, or park it in checking) is a decision too, usually the wrong one made passively. IOMI's Next Dollar Allocation exists for precisely this moment: given your current position, where does the next dollar do the most good?

### The secondary decision

Selling a slice of your company — in a secondary, at a round, or at exit — is the single largest diversification event of most founders' lives. The mistake is treating it as binary (all-in vs. sold out) rather than as a dial. Taking modest liquidity does not signal lost faith; it converts an unsurvivable failure mode into a survivable one.

### The satellite portfolio

What you do with already-liquid wealth: index funds, real estate, crypto, angel checks, collectibles. Here conventional allocation thinking applies — with one founder-specific correction. Your satellite portfolio should be balanced against your concentrated position, not internally. A SaaS founder who angel-invests only in SaaS has diversified nothing. The satellites should deliberately not correlate with the core.

### The liability side

Debt is negative diversification: a mortgage or a line of credit concentrated against the same asset base amplifies the downside of everything else. Founders often skip counting liabilities in their risk picture; a balance sheet with the liabilities included is the one that tells the truth.

## When Concentration Is the Right Call

An honest framework has to say this: sometimes the concentrated position deserves the capital. Early-stage founders holding 90% of their wealth in their company are not making an allocation error — they are making the bet that defines the whole enterprise. Diversification advice that ignores this is advice for someone else.

The discipline is not "never concentrate." It is:

- **Know the number.** Concentration you can quote is a strategy; concentration you would have to look up is an accident.
- **Hold liquidity beside the bet.** Runway is what lets you hold a concentrated position through a drawdown instead of being forced to act at the worst moment.
- **Set the tripwire in advance.** Decide now at what valuation, round, or life event you will take liquidity — before the emotions of the moment are in the room.
- **Re-measure on every event.** Rounds, markups, big crypto moves, property revaluations: each one changes your real allocation without you lifting a finger.

One more distinction keeps the thinking straight: diversification for founders is asymmetric in time. Early on, the concentrated bet is the strategy and the satellite portfolio is a safety margin. Later — after a secondary, a profitable stretch, or an exit — the ratio inverts, and the discipline shifts from protecting the bet to not letting the proceeds drift back into correlated positions out of habit. The framework does not change; the weights do. What stays constant is the requirement to know the numbers at every stage, because the transition points are exactly where accidental allocations happen.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is founder diversification?

The practice of managing risk across a founder's full balance sheet — company equity, liquid investments, crypto, property, collectibles, and liabilities — rather than just balancing a brokerage account. It centers on measuring concentration in the founder's largest position and using the levers founders actually control: distributions, secondaries, and the composition of the satellite portfolio.

### Is the 60/40 portfolio wrong for founders?

It is not wrong so much as beside the point. 60/40 balances liquid assets for someone whose income is independent of their portfolio. A founder's dominant position is illiquid company equity correlated with their income. Balancing the liquid 15% while ignoring the concentrated 85% is precision applied to the wrong problem.

### How much concentration is too much?

There is no universal threshold, and anyone quoting one is guessing. The workable questions are: do you know your number, could your liquid assets carry you through a bad two years, and have you decided in advance when you will take liquidity? A founder who answers yes to all three can responsibly run high concentration; one who answers no to all three is at risk even at moderate levels.
