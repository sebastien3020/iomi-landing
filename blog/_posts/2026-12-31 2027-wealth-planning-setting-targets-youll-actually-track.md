# 2027 Wealth Planning: Setting Targets You'll Actually Track

Somewhere in the first week of January, most founders will write down financial targets for the new year. By March, nobody will be able to say whether they are on track, because the targets were written in a document no system reads and measured against numbers nobody consolidated. **2027 financial planning** does not fail at the ambition stage; it fails at the tracking stage — and so the fix is not better goals but goals wired into a system that measures them without asking you to remember.

This guide is a planning session you can actually run in an afternoon: establish the real baseline, review what 2026 actually did (not what it felt like), set a small number of targets with the properties that make them trackable, wire them to milestones and a status-quo trajectory, and choose the cadence that keeps the plan alive past February. It is written for founders — people whose targets span personal wealth and business entities — but the method is general.

## Key Takeaways

* Annual targets fail at the tracking stage, not the ambition stage: goals written outside any measuring system are abandoned by spring, predictably.
* Planning starts with a consolidated baseline — you cannot set a trajectory from a starting point you cannot see.
* Three targets beat ten: one net-worth target, one deployment-quality target, one structural target is a complete founder plan.
* Every target needs a number, a date, milestones, and a status-quo trajectory to be judged against — otherwise "on track" is a feeling.
* The plan's survival depends on cadence: a thirty-second daily brief, a weekly ten-minute check, and a monthly review beat any quantity of January resolve.

## Why Last Year's Plan Died (It Wasn't Discipline)

Run the autopsy honestly. The 2026 targets you set — where are they written? When did you last check them against real numbers? For most founders the answers are "a note somewhere" and "not since winter," and the standard self-diagnosis is insufficient discipline.

The diagnosis is wrong. Founders are not undisciplined people; they run companies on OKRs and dashboards that get reviewed weekly without fail. The difference is infrastructure. Company goals live inside systems that measure them automatically and surface them on a rhythm. Personal wealth targets live in a January document connected to nothing — checking them requires manually assembling a net worth figure from a dozen accounts, which means checking doesn't happen, which means the goals silently detach from reality and then from memory.

So the design principle for 2027 financial planning: no target exists unless something measures it continuously. A goal you must remember to check is a goal you have already abandoned; you just haven't reached the date yet. Everything below follows from this one rule.

## Step Two: The 2026 Review — What Actually Happened

With the baseline live, run last year's numbers before setting next year's. Thirty minutes, four questions:

**Where did net worth move, and why?** Attribution matters more than the delta. Growth that came entirely from your company's paper valuation is a different fact than growth from deployed capital — one is concentration deepening, the other is a portfolio working.

**What did each entity actually deliver to you?** Trailing-twelve-month owner earnings per business, not topline. Portfolio entrepreneurs are routinely surprised by which stream carried the year — the measurement discipline is laid out in [tracking multiple revenue streams](/blog/tracking-multiple-revenue-streams-as-a-portfolio-entrepreneur).

**Where did capital sit idle?** Look for cash that spent the year earning nothing beyond any deliberate reserve. This number is usually the cheapest improvement available in the whole plan — the anatomy is in [idle cash: how much is too much](/blog/idle-cash-how-much-is-too-much).

**Which 2026 intention quietly died, and why?** Not to relitigate it — to extract the failure mode. Almost always the answer is the tracking gap this article opened with, which is exactly what 2027's design fixes.

## Step Three: Three Targets, Not Ten

Here is where planning usually inflates: a target for every account, every asset class, every virtue. Ten targets produce the same outcome as none, because attention is the scarce input. A complete founder plan for 2027 is three targets, one per layer.

**A net-worth target.** The headline: total (or liquid, if that's your real constraint) net worth of X by December 2027. Ground it in arithmetic, not aspiration — a target disconnected from your actual income, savings capacity, and plausible asset behavior is a wish with a date. The projection machinery in step four will keep it honest.

**A deployment target.** Quality, not quantity: raise the Capital Efficiency Score from its December reading by a chosen amount — which decomposes into concrete moves like deploying the idle cash, reducing drag, or trimming a concentration edge. This target is the one most plans miss entirely, and it is often worth more than the headline target because it improves how every future dollar behaves. The five factors are unpacked in [the Capital Efficiency Score guide](/blog/what-is-a-capital-efficiency-score-the-founders-guide-to-wealth-intelligence).

**A structural target.** One change to the shape of things rather than the size: fully separate a blurred entity boundary, complete the consolidation itself, build the reserve your [stress test](/blog/stress-testing-your-wealth-plan-before-life-does) said you need, or establish personal runway independent of business income. Structural targets don't compound numerically — they make the numerical compounding safer.

Anything else you're tempted to add belongs on next year's list or inside one of these three. (Retirement-account and tax-timing intentions have their place, but scope them with your CPA — that layer is professional territory, not a dashboard checkbox.)

## Step Four: Wire the Targets — Milestones and the Status-Quo Line

Now the part that separates 2027's plan from 2026's: each target gets wired into the measuring system with two attachments.

**Milestones.** A December target with no intermediate structure is unfalsifiable until it's too late — all year long, "on track" remains a feeling. Quarterly milestones convert it into four small verdicts: by March 31, net worth at X₁; by June 30, X₂. Each quarter delivers a yes or no, early enough for the no to be actionable. In IOMI, Goals carry milestones natively, checked against your live consolidated numbers rather than your memory of them.

**The status-quo trajectory.** The most underrated planning instrument: a deterministic projection of where you land by December 2027 if you change nothing — current holdings, current income profile, current behavior, computed year by year. This line does two jobs. Before the year, it tests the target's honesty: if the status-quo line already reaches your target, the target is a description, not a goal; if the gap is enormous, the target needs either bigger planned moves or a humbler number. During the year, it becomes the baseline your actual trajectory is compared against — progress means beating the line you would have ridden anyway. ## Step Five: The Cadence That Survives February

The last design decision is rhythm. A plan checked never dies in March; a plan checked obsessively dies of fatigue by February. The sustainable cadence has three speeds:

**Daily — thirty seconds.** A brief scan: what changed, does anything need you. Most days the answer is no, and a good daily brief says so instantly — IOMI's Daily Brief exists for precisely this. The point of the daily touch is not action; it is keeping the plan ambient so it never needs re-remembering.

**Weekly — ten minutes.** A short interrogation of the live picture: cash positions across entities, anything drifting, one look at the current trajectory against the milestone. A handful of good [questions to your wealth AI](/blog/20-questions-founders-should-ask-their-wealth-ai) covers it — answered from your real holdings, never from estimates.

**Monthly and quarterly — the review.** Monthly: a real look at all three targets against their lines. Quarterly: the milestone verdict, plus adjustments — re-running projections if reality diverged, resizing a target if the business changed structurally. Adjusting a target on evidence is planning; adjusting it on mood is the old failure with better tooling. The difference is that now there's a computed line to argue with.

### How many financial goals should I set for the year?

Three, one per layer: size (a net-worth target), quality (a deployment target like raising your Capital Efficiency Score), and shape (a structural target like entity separation or a properly sized reserve).
