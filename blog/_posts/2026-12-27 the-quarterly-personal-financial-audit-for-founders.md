# The Quarterly Personal Financial Audit for Founders

A **personal financial audit** is the discipline founders apply to their companies and almost never to themselves. Your business gets a monthly close, a board review, and an annual audit. Your personal balance sheet — often larger than the company's, spread across more institutions, carrying more concentrated risk — gets a nervous glance at a banking app and a vague intention to "sort it out after this quarter." The asymmetry would be funny if it weren't expensive.

The fix doesn't require an accountant or a weekend. Run a structured quarterly audit — roughly 90 minutes, four times a year — and the compounding effect is dramatic: idle cash gets caught within a quarter instead of festering for years, stale valuations get refreshed before they distort decisions, drift gets corrected while corrections are small, and the year-end review becomes a formality instead of an archaeology dig. This article is the complete protocol: what to check, in what order, what "done" looks like for each step, and how the right tooling collapses most of the work into reading a screen instead of building one.

## Key Takeaways

* Founders audit their companies quarterly and their personal finances never — inverted priorities, since the personal balance sheet often carries more value and more concentrated risk with less oversight.
* The audit has six blocks: reconcile completeness, verify valuations, hunt idle cash, re-check risk and concentration, review cash flow and goals, and close with decisions logged.
* The single most valuable habit is the completeness check: an audit over 80% of your assets silently audits the wrong balance sheet.
* With consolidated tooling doing the aggregation continuously, the quarterly audit shrinks from an afternoon of spreadsheet assembly to about 90 minutes of actual judgment.
* The output is not a report — it's a short list of decisions with owners and dates. An audit that ends without decisions is a viewing, not an audit.

## Why Quarterly — Not Monthly, Not Annually

The cadence question first, because it determines whether the habit survives.

Annual reviews fail on damage. Twelve months is long enough for $150k to sit idle the entire year, for concentration to drift ten points, for a subscription stack to compound quietly. Whatever you catch in an annual review has, by definition, been costing you for up to a year.

Monthly personal audits fail on adherence — and on signal. Founders don't reliably clear a monthly deep-dive slot, and most balance-sheet metrics barely move in a month, so monthly reviews degrade into glancing at noise until the habit dies. (Daily and weekly attention has its place, but it's *monitoring*, not auditing — more on that below.)

Quarterly is the working compromise: long enough for real signal to accumulate, short enough that anything you catch has only been wrong for weeks. It also matches the natural rhythm of founder finances — estimated tax payments, board cycles, distribution decisions — so the audit lands when adjacent decisions are being made anyway. Book all four sessions for the year now; the audit you schedule is the audit that happens.

## Block 1: The Completeness Check (15 minutes)

Every audit starts with the same question, and it's not "how am I doing?" It's **"am I looking at everything?"**

Walk the inventory: every bank account (each entity and personal), every brokerage and retirement account, crypto exchanges *and* wallets, each property, each private position — company equity, fund interests, notes you hold — collectibles worth counting, and every liability including personally guaranteed business debt. New accounts opened this quarter? Old ones forgotten? A note from a side deal that never got recorded?

This block exists because incompleteness is the silent killer of financial reviews: an audit over 80% of your assets isn't 80% as good — it can be actively misleading, because the missing 20% is usually the least-watched, worst-behaved part. Idle cash and impaired notes don't live in the accounts you look at weekly.

## Block 2: Valuation Honesty (15 minutes)

Connected accounts price themselves. This block is for everything that doesn't — and it's where audits earn their keep, because manual valuations rot silently.

For each manual position, two questions: *when was this last marked?* and *what's the evidence?* Company equity — still defensible, or anchored to a stale round? Property — checked against current comparables this year? The [classic cars marked with evidence or with love](/blog/classic-cars-in-your-net-worth-valuation-without-wishful-thinking)? Notes and fund positions — [marked to payment reality and latest NAV](/blog/private-credit-positions-in-a-founder-portfolio), or still at face because writing down hurts?

House rule worth adopting: any manual valuation older than two quarters gets refreshed or flagged this session, and every markdown the evidence demands gets taken *today*. The audit is precisely the venue for the unpleasant marks — that's what makes the rest of the year's numbers trustworthy.

**Done when:** every manual position has a mark under six months old with one line of evidence attached.

## Block 3: The Idle Cash Hunt (10 minutes)

Now the profitable part. Sum cash across every compartment and compare against your written buffer policy — you have one from last quarter's audit; if this is audit one, writing it is today's main outcome (per-entity operating buffer, personal runway, tax reserve sized by your CPA's number, not anxiety).

Everything above policy is idle by definition and needs a decision: deploy per your allocation plan, or park deliberately at competitive yield while a decision forms. The one forbidden outcome is the default one — leaving it unexamined where it sits. [Cash drag in a high-rate world](/blog/the-real-cost-of-cash-drag-in-a-high-rate-world) commonly runs to thousands per quarter on typical founder balances, which means this ten-minute block frequently pays for the entire year's audits many times over.

**Done when:** every dollar above written buffers has a decision attached — even if the decision is "parked at yield, revisit next quarter."

## Block 4: Risk, Concentration, and Liquidity (15 minutes)

Three structural questions, quarterly, forever.

**Concentration:** what share of net worth is the largest exposure — usually your company — and which direction did it move this quarter? Drift is fine *if chosen*: the audit's job is converting drift into decision. If [the company has crept from 70% to 78%](/blog/concentration-risk-for-founders-when-your-company-is-80-of-your-net-worth), the question is whether you're choosing that or merely receiving it. Group correlated exposures honestly — notes to your own industry count with the company, not against it.

**Liquidity:** re-run the [liquid versus total split](/blog/net-worth-vs-liquid-net-worth-vs-investable-assets). Liquid share shrinking three quarters straight is a trend demanding a plan, not a shrug. Check uncalled fund commitments against liquid reserves while you're here.

**Risk balance:** does the deployable portfolio still match the allocation you actually intend? A position that ran 40% has changed your allocation without asking. Rebalancing is a decision for you (and any advisor); *seeing* the drift is the audit's job.

Log the score each quarter; the quarter-over-quarter delta, and *which factor* moved it, is exactly the executive summary this block exists to produce.

**Done when:** you've logged the score, named the biggest mover, and decided whether it was chosen or drifted.

## Block 5: Cash Flow, Goals, and Trajectory (15 minutes)

Look backward one quarter, forward four.

Backward: how did actual income and spending compare to what you expected — personal burn, distributions taken, any slow leak in recurring costs? Trends invisible month-to-month are obvious quarter-over-quarter, which is the audit cadence's whole advantage.

Forward: update planned events — the tax payment, the tuition, the capital call, the planned distribution — and let the cash-flow forecast (profile income minus expenses plus planned events) show the next four quarters. Check each goal against its milestones and status-quo trajectory: on track, ahead, behind — and if behind, is the response more capital, more time, or a revised goal? A decision previewed is a decision made this quarter instead of deferred to the next.

**Done when:** planned events are current, every goal has a status ruling, and any big move has been simulated.

## Block 6: Close the Audit (15 minutes)

An audit that ends without decisions is a viewing. Close properly:

**Write the decision log.** Three to seven lines: *what, by when.* "Move $80k excess from OpCo checking to money market — Friday." "Refresh property valuation with two comps — this month." "Ask CPA to true up tax reserve — before estimate date." Vague intentions don't survive contact with the following Tuesday; dated actions mostly do.

**Update the policies.** Buffer sizes, allocation targets, valuation rules — if this quarter's facts changed a policy, change it in writing now, so next quarter's audit checks against current policy rather than folklore.

**Schedule the follow-through.** Put the decision items on your actual calendar. Then confirm next quarter's audit slot exists.

**File the human-only items.** Estate documents, insurance coverage, entity structure — the [blind spots no monitoring system catches](/blog/financial-blind-spots-ai-can-catch-and-the-ones-it-cant) — get reviewed annually with your attorney and CPA. The quarterly audit's job is merely to check those appointments exist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a personal financial audit?

A structured, recurring review of your complete personal financial picture: confirming every asset and liability is captured, refreshing valuations with evidence, hunting idle cash against written buffer policies, re-checking concentration and liquidity, reviewing cash flow and goal trajectories, and closing with dated decisions. It's the personal equivalent of the quarterly close founders already run for their companies.

### How long does a quarterly audit actually take?

With consolidated tooling doing aggregation continuously, about 90 minutes of actual judgment across six blocks. Without it, the honest answer is an afternoon — most of it spent logging into institutions and rebuilding a spreadsheet rather than deciding anything. The first audit takes longest because you're writing buffer policies and entering manual assets; subsequent ones get faster.

### What should I check first in a financial audit?

Completeness. Before asking "how am I doing?", confirm you're looking at everything: all accounts across all entities, crypto wallets, properties, private positions, notes, collectibles, and every liability. An audit over a partial balance sheet can be worse than none, because the unwatched 20% is usually where idle cash and impaired positions hide.

### Quarterly, monthly, or annually — what's the right cadence?

Quarterly for the audit itself: long enough for real signal, short enough that anything caught has only been wrong for weeks. Annual-only reviews let problems compound for up to a year; monthly deep-dives fail on adherence and mostly re-read noise. Daily and weekly attention should be lightweight monitoring — a brief, a score, a glance — not a full audit.

### What's the most valuable single step if I only do one thing?

The idle cash hunt. Sum cash across every account and entity, compare against deliberately written buffers, and give everything above the line a decision. It's ten minutes, it routinely finds five- and six-figure balances earning near zero, and the recovered yield frequently exceeds the cost of every tool and every hour the full audit consumes.

### What belongs in the audit's decision log?

Three to seven dated, owned actions — not observations. "Move $80k to money market by Friday," not "cash position is high." "Refresh property comps this month," not "valuation may be stale." Each item goes on your calendar before the session ends. The log from last quarter is also next audit's first read: unfinished items are the truest measure of whether the process is working.

### Do I still need my CPA and attorney if I audit quarterly?

Yes — the audit complements them and makes them cheaper to use well. Tax reserves, estate documents, insurance adequacy, and entity structure are professional territory the quarterly audit only checks is *scheduled*. What changes is that you arrive at those annual reviews with a complete, current, honest picture — so professional time is spent advising rather than reconstructing.
