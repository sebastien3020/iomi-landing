# Private Credit Positions in a Founder Portfolio

**Private credit tracking** is where a lot of otherwise disciplined founder balance sheets quietly fall apart. The brokerage account updates itself. The bank feeds sync. But the $150k note to a friend's company, the two fund commitments with capital calls outstanding, the seller financing from the business you exited — these live in a folder of PDFs and a memory of what was promised. They produce real income, carry real risk, and represent real money. And for many founders, they are tracked worse than the smallest checking account.

That mismatch has grown as private credit itself has grown. Founders encounter it from every direction: as investors in private credit funds chasing yield, as direct lenders to other founders' companies, as recipients of seller notes from their own exits, and sometimes as borrowers. This article covers how to hold private credit honestly inside a founder portfolio — what these positions actually are, how to value them without kidding yourself, what to monitor between statements, and how to consolidate them with everything else you own so the whole picture stays true.

## Key Takeaways

* Private credit positions — direct notes, fund commitments, seller financing — are real assets with contractual cash flows, and they deserve the same tracking rigor as any brokerage account.
* A note's value is not its face amount. Honest valuation reflects payment performance, borrower health, and seniority — and gets written down when the facts say so.
* The three things worth monitoring continuously: payment punctuality, uncalled commitments (money you still owe a fund), and maturity/repricing dates.
* Private credit is illiquid. It belongs on the illiquid side of your net worth and outside your emergency math, whatever the marketing said about redemption windows.
* Consolidation is the point: a note tracked in a PDF folder is a blind spot; the same note tracked alongside your accounts, entities, and property is a managed position.

## What Counts as Private Credit on a Founder's Balance Sheet

Private credit is lending that happens outside public bond markets and bank balance sheets. On a founder's personal balance sheet, it usually arrives in four shapes.

**Direct notes.** You lent money — to another founder's company, to a real-estate operator, to a family member's business — documented (hopefully) by a promissory note with a rate, a schedule, and a maturity. These are the most common and the most under-tracked.

**Fund positions.** Interests in private credit or direct-lending funds: you committed capital, some has been called, distributions arrive irregularly, and a quarterly statement reports NAV. The wrinkle founders forget: the *uncalled* commitment is a real future obligation that belongs in your liquidity planning.

**Seller financing.** You sold a business or property and took part of the price as a note. Economically, you are now your buyer's lender — often for years, often for a sum that rivals your liquid net worth. This position deserves professional-grade attention and usually gets nostalgia instead.

**Revenue-share and convertible instruments.** Revenue-based financing you provided, convertible notes awaiting a round, SAFEs with debt-like features. Hybrids — but until conversion, their economics are credit economics: you're owed money by a counterparty whose health determines whether you get it.

What unites all four: contractual cash flows, counterparty risk, no liquid market, and no automatic feed. Which is precisely why they end up untracked — and why they shouldn't be.

## Valuing Private Credit Without Kidding Yourself

The default founder method — carry every note at face value forever — is wrong in the same comfortable direction as every other private-asset fiction. The honest method has three parts.

**Start at face, then let the facts move you.** A performing note — payments on time, borrower healthy, terms at market — can reasonably sit near face value. But that's a starting point, not a birthright. The valuation question for any credit position is always: *what is the realistic probability I receive the remaining cash flows in full and on time?*

**Write down when the facts say so.** Payments late twice in a row? Borrower raising bridge financing? Covenant waived? The market for your note — if one existed — would already have repriced it. You should too. A serious approach marks impaired positions down in stages (say, to 70%, 50%, or recovery value) with a note recording *why*, the same evidence-based honesty applied to [collectibles valuation](/blog/classic-cars-in-your-net-worth-valuation-without-wishful-thinking): the write-down costs pride and buys accurate decisions.

**Use fund NAVs, with a lag warning.** For fund positions, the manager's reported NAV is your best available mark — but recognize it lags reality in both directions and smooths the ride by construction. Carry NAV, note the statement date, and don't treat a stale quarterly figure as today's truth.

Seniority deserves one honest sentence in every valuation note: are you secured or unsecured, senior or subordinated, and is there a personal guarantee? Two notes with identical rates and utterly different recovery prospects should not be marked identically when trouble starts.

Tax treatment of interest income, original-issue discount, and bad-debt write-offs is real and materially affects your net outcome — and it is firmly CPA territory. Track the positions; let your CPA handle the tax character.

## What to Monitor Between Statements

Private credit's danger is its silence. Public positions reprice daily and scream at you; a private note defaults quietly, months after the signs appeared. The monitoring list is short and non-negotiable.

**Payment performance, logged.** Every scheduled payment, marked received-on-time, late, or missed, the day it happens. Deterioration in payment punctuality is the single most reliable early signal in credit, and it's only visible if you're logging. A payment that just stops — and isn't noticed for two months because nothing was watching — is the canonical private credit blind spot, the exact species of unwatched drift that [AI-assisted monitoring catches well](/blog/financial-blind-spots-ai-can-catch-and-the-ones-it-cant) when the position is actually in the system.

**Uncalled commitments.** For fund positions: how much can still be called, and when plausibly? Uncalled capital is a contingent liability against your liquid reserves — a founder with $300k uncalled across two funds and $250k of liquid net worth has a planning problem hiding in plain sight. This is one more reason the [liquid net worth calculation](/blog/net-worth-vs-liquid-net-worth-vs-investable-assets) needs to see everything, including obligations that haven't landed yet.

**Dates.** Maturities, rate resets, balloon payments, conversion triggers. A note maturing in 90 days is a decision arriving — extend, renegotiate, or collect — and you want the decision on your calendar, not sprung on you.

**Concentration and correlation.** Private credit tends to concentrate invisibly: three notes to three companies in your own industry is one correlated bet wearing three costumes. And a founder whose net worth is dominated by their own company, who also lends into the same ecosystem, has stacked exposure on exposure. That's a portfolio property — visible only when [everything is consolidated in one view](/blog/asset-consolidation-software-the-strategic-cockpit-for-founders-in-2026).

## The Founder-Specific Failure Modes

Four patterns show up repeatedly in founder credit books; each is preventable with the structure above.

**The friendship discount — on tracking, not just rate.** Money lent inside a relationship gets documented casually and tracked never. The relationship is exactly why the tracking should be *impeccable*: ambiguity, not the loan, is what damages friendships. Note, schedule, log — every time.

**The exit note treated as won money.** Seller financing arrives at the emotional peak of an exit and gets mentally filed as "proceeds" rather than "new risk position." It's often the founder's single largest credit exposure, secured by a business now run by someone else. Track it like the material position it is, with payment logging and periodic borrower check-ins.

**Yield-chasing into opacity.** High headline rates on instruments whose risk you can't assess is how "private credit" becomes "expensive lesson." If you can't explain the seniority, the security, and the realistic downside in three sentences, the position deserves more diligence or less money.

**Forgetting you're sometimes the borrower.** Founders also *owe* private credit — investor notes, seller notes payable, personally guaranteed facilities. The same tracking rigor applies on the liability side, and the same consolidation logic: your true leverage is only visible when both sides are in one place.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How should I track private credit investments?

Treat each position like an account: record principal, rate, schedule, maturity, seniority, and documentation; log every payment as received, late, or missed; carry an honest valuation that moves with the facts; and hold it all in the same consolidated system as your other assets, in the correct personal or business compartment. The positions with no automatic feed are exactly the ones that need deliberate tracking most.

### How do I value a private note in my net worth?

Start at outstanding principal for a performing note, then adjust for reality: payment history, borrower health, seniority, and security. Write impaired positions down in stages with a recorded rationale. For fund positions, use the latest reported NAV and note its date. The test is always the same — the realistic probability of receiving remaining cash flows in full and on time.

### Are private credit funds liquid?

Treat them as illiquid. Even funds with stated redemption windows can gate or delay redemptions precisely when you most want out. Fund positions belong on the illiquid side of your net worth, outside emergency math — and the uncalled portion of your commitment is a future obligation that should be counted against your liquidity planning, not ignored.

### What are capital calls and why do they matter for tracking?

When you commit to a private fund, you typically don't send all the money upfront — the manager "calls" capital over time as deals are made. Uncalled commitments are real future obligations with uncertain timing. A founder with large uncalled commitments and thin liquid reserves has a hidden liquidity risk that only shows up when commitments are tracked alongside liquid assets in one view.

### How does private credit affect my concentration risk?

Often more than it appears, through correlation. Notes to companies in your own industry, or to your own professional network, tend to sour at the same time your main asset — your company — is under pressure. Honest concentration analysis groups exposures by what drives their risk, not by instrument type, and that requires seeing the credit book and the equity positions in one consolidated picture.
