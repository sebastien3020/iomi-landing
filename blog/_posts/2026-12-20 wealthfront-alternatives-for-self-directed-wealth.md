# Wealthfront Alternatives for Self-Directed Wealth

The search for a **wealthfront alternative** usually means one of two very different things. Some people want a different place to have their money managed — another robo-advisor, a different fee, a different fund lineup. Others have realized something more fundamental: the part of their financial life Wealthfront manages is no longer the part that needs help. The brokerage account is fine on autopilot. It is everything else — the company equity, the LLC, the crypto wallets, the rental property, the angel checks — that has no system at all.

This article is for the second group, though it will serve the first honestly along the way. We will give Wealthfront its genuine due, mark precisely where automated investing stops being the answer, and lay out what self-directed wealth actually requires: not a better manager, but a better view. If your financial life has outgrown a single managed account, the alternative you need may not be another Wealthfront at all.

## Key Takeaways

* Wealthfront is genuinely good at its job: low-cost automated investing, disciplined rebalancing, tax-loss harvesting inside its accounts, and a polished cash product. As a destination for a diversified liquid portfolio on autopilot, it earns its reputation.
* Its boundary is structural: Wealthfront manages the money you place with Wealthfront. It is not built to see — let alone analyze — the rest of a complex balance sheet: private equity, entities, real estate, crypto wallets, collectibles.
* For founders and self-directed investors, the unmanaged portion is usually the majority of net worth, which means the tool managing the minority cannot answer the questions that matter most: total concentration, idle cash across entities, what-if scenarios.
* The real alternative is a different category — a wealth intelligence layer that consolidates everything read-only, analyzes capital efficiency, and simulates decisions, while you keep managing money wherever you like (including at Wealthfront).
* The honest decision: robo-advisors and oversight platforms are complements, not competitors. Many IOMI users keep an automated brokerage account; what they replace is the spreadsheet pretending to be a system of record.

## What Wealthfront Does Genuinely Well

Any honest comparison starts here, because Wealthfront's core product is strong.

Automated investing is the flagship: you answer risk questions, and the platform builds and maintains a diversified, low-cost ETF portfolio — rebalancing mechanically, reinvesting dividends, and harvesting tax losses within the account without you lifting a finger. The management fee is a small fraction of what a traditional human advisor charges, and the discipline is arguably better: software does not panic-sell, forget to rebalance, or chase last year's winner. For the job of "take my liquid savings and invest them sensibly forever," this model is one of the genuine wins of the last fifteen years of fintech.

The cash account deserves its own credit: competitive yield, high FDIC coverage through partner banks, and clean mechanics for moving money — good enough that plenty of people use Wealthfront for cash alone.

And the product philosophy is coherent. Wealthfront has stayed focused on automation rather than pivoting to push human advice, the interface is clean, and planning tools give a reasonable long-horizon view of the accounts it holds. Within its walls, it is a polished, honest product. If your entire financial life fits inside those walls — salary in, diversified portfolio, cash buffer, retirement horizon — you may not need an alternative. Stop reading and go build something.

## Where the Walls Are

The limitation is not a flaw to be fixed in a future release. It is the shape of the product: Wealthfront is a money manager, and a manager's view is bounded by the money it manages.

Consider what a founder's balance sheet looks like and how much of it can live inside a robo-advisor. Company equity — usually the largest line — cannot. Business entities and their accounts: no. Real estate: no. Crypto on exchanges and in wallets: no. Angel checks and SAFEs: no. Collectibles: no. What fits is the liquid brokerage slice and some cash — for a typical founder, a minority of net worth, often a small one.

This creates a quiet absurdity: the most sophisticated tooling in your financial life is pointed at its simplest problem. The diversified ETF portfolio — the one asset that least needs your attention — gets automated rebalancing and tax-loss harvesting. Meanwhile the concentrated, illiquid, entity-tangled majority of your wealth is tracked, if at all, in a spreadsheet updated quarterly under duress.

And the questions that actually determine your financial outcomes live in that unmanaged majority. How concentrated am I across everything, including the company? How much cash is idle across all entities? What is my real liquid runway? What happens to my five-year picture if I take a distribution, or a secondary, or buy the building? Wealthfront is not wrong about these questions — it simply cannot see them. No robo-advisor can, because the answers require a consolidated view of assets no custodian holds.

The search for a **wealthfront alternative**, for self-directed people, is really the search for that view.

## Choosing Your Setup: Three Profiles

Self-directed wealth is not one situation. Here is how the decision falls for the three profiles that most often land on this page.

**The salaried professional with simple finances.** Income, index portfolio, cash buffer, retirement horizon. Wealthfront (or a competitor robo) genuinely covers most of this life, and an oversight platform is optional until complexity arrives. The honest advice is that you are not yet the customer for the category shift — bookmark it for the day you start a company or buy your first rental.

**The founder or owner-operator.** Company equity dominating net worth, one or more entities, lumpy income, a growing tangle of asset types. The managed account is a side detail of your financial life; the absence of a consolidated view is the actual problem. Start with the oversight layer — the [modern net worth tracker guide](/blog/the-modern-net-worth-tracker-a-founders-guide-to-wealth-intelligence) maps what first-class treatment of your asset mix looks like — and keep or add automated investing for the liquid slice as you like.

**The post-exit or multi-asset investor.** Liquidity has arrived, and with it a portfolio spanning brokerages, real estate, private positions, and crypto. Your questions are allocation-wide: concentration, idle capital, efficiency of the whole. A robo-advisor can absorb part of the liquid capital; only a consolidated intelligence layer can referee the whole — and at this scale, percentage-of-assets fees on oversight (as opposed to management) stop making sense entirely.

Wealthfront's planning tools can link external accounts to inform its projections, and that linking is genuinely useful within its frame. Aggregation-as-input differs from oversight-as-product.

Most founders get more immediate value from consolidating everything in an oversight layer, then routing whatever liquid capital deserves automation into a managed or self-directed brokerage account.
