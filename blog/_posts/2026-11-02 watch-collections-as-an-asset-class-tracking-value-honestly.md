# Watch Collections as an Asset Class: Tracking Value Honestly

Serious **watch collection value tracking** has a credibility problem. Ask ten collectors what their watches are worth and you will get ten numbers, most of them anchored to the best day the market ever had. A Nautilus bought at retail, a Daytona inherited from a father, a handful of independents picked up at auction — together they might be a meaningful slice of your net worth. Or they might be worth half of what you tell yourself. The difference matters, because a number you cannot trust is worse than no number at all.

This article is about doing it honestly. Where watches actually sit as an asset class, how to value a collection without fooling yourself, why liquidity is the part most collectors ignore, and how to put watches into your total net worth picture alongside your company, your brokerage accounts, and your real estate — without letting sentiment inflate the balance sheet.

## Key Takeaways

* Watches are a real but illiquid asset class: prices are observable, but selling costs are high, timing is slow, and dealer spreads routinely take 15–30% off the number you see on listing sites.
* Honest valuation means marking to what you could actually sell for — net of fees, in a reasonable timeframe — not to the highest ask you can find online.
* A watch collection belongs in your net worth as part of a physical assets and collectibles category, clearly separated from liquid holdings, so it never distorts decisions that depend on accessible capital.
* The market cycle of 2021–2023 is the permanent lesson: secondary prices for steel sports models roughly doubled and then gave most of it back. Marking to a peak is how collectors end up lying to themselves.
* Tracking value honestly is not about pessimism. It is about knowing which part of your wealth is passion, which part is investment, and never confusing the two when you make a capital decision.

## Are Watches Actually an Asset Class?

The honest answer: yes, with caveats that would make a portfolio manager wince.

Watches meet the basic tests. There is a deep secondary market with observable prices. Certain references — steel sports models from Rolex, Patek Philippe, and Audemars Piguet above all — have held or grown purchasing power over long periods. Supply of discontinued references is fixed forever, and supply of current hot references is deliberately constrained. There are dealers, auction houses, price databases, insurance products, and even funds built around the category.

But the caveats are structural, not temporary. Watches produce no cash flow, so all return comes from price appreciation, which makes the category behave more like art than like equities. Transaction costs are enormous compared to financial assets: an auction house may take a seller's commission, a dealer buys at a discount to retail-out pricing, and private sales carry authentication and escrow friction. Condition, box, papers, and service history move individual prices by double-digit percentages, which means two "identical" watches are not fungible the way two shares of the same stock are.

And the market is narrow. A handful of references from a handful of brands represent most of the liquidity. The long tail — most dress watches, most fashion brands, most complications from second-tier makers — depreciates like a used car. If your collection is built on love rather than on the ten most liquid references, that is a perfectly good reason to collect. It is not an investment portfolio.

So the working frame for a founder: treat watches as a legitimate line in your net worth, inside physical assets and collectibles, valued conservatively, and never counted on for liquidity you might actually need.

## The Three Numbers Every Watch Has

Most valuation confusion collapses once you realize every watch has at least three different prices, and collectors habitually quote the wrong one.

### Retail price

What the brand charges at an authorized dealer. For hot references, retail is a fiction — you cannot walk in and buy a steel Daytona at list — and for everything else, retail is the ceiling you will never see again after leaving the boutique. Retail is useful for insurance conversations and almost nothing else.

### Market ask

What comparable watches are listed for on secondary platforms. This is the number collectors love, because it is public, high, and easy to find. It is also an ask, not a sale. Listings sit for months. Sellers negotiate. Platforms take fees. A market ask tells you the optimistic edge of the range, not what your watch is worth.

### Net realizable value

What lands in your account if you decide to sell within a reasonable window — say, 60 to 90 days. This means either a dealer buy-in price (fast, certain, typically the lowest), a platform sale minus fees and shipping and the negotiation haircut, or an auction result minus seller's commission after months of waiting. For most watches, net realizable value sits meaningfully below market ask — spreads of 15–30% are normal, wider for illiquid references.

Honest **watch collection value tracking** uses the third number. Your balance sheet should reflect what the collection converts to, not what a listing site says on its best day. If that feels painful, that pain is information: it is the gap between the story and the asset.

## The 2021–2023 Lesson: Marking to Peak Is Self-Deception

The recent cycle deserves its own section because it permanently changed how thoughtful collectors think about value.

Between 2020 and early 2022, secondary prices for the most-hyped references went vertical. Steel sports models traded at multiples of retail. Collections doubled on paper without a single purchase. Then the market turned in mid-2022, and over the following two years the hottest references gave back most of the run-up. Watches were still above pre-boom levels in many cases — but anyone who had marked their collection to the 2022 peak, and made decisions based on that number, was working with a balance sheet that was substantially wrong.

The failure was not owning watches through a downturn. Cycles happen. The failure was letting a paper number harden into a belief. Collectors borrowed against peak valuations, counted the collection as an emergency reserve, or delayed diversifying elsewhere because "the watches are doing the work." When the correction came, none of those decisions looked good.

The discipline that survives cycles is simple: revalue periodically against realized sales rather than asks, apply the liquidity haircut every time, and watch the trend rather than celebrating a single mark. A collection tracked this way can fall in value without breaking anything else in your financial life — because nothing else was leaning on an inflated number.

## Where Watches Belong in Your Net Worth

The right place for a watch collection is inside your net worth, clearly labeled, and structurally separated from liquid assets. Both halves of that sentence matter.

Inside your net worth, because excluding real value is as distorting as inflating it. If your collection would net $150k in a sale, that is real wealth, and ignoring it understates your position — which affects concentration math, goal tracking, and the honest answer to [what is my net worth](/blog/what-is-my-net-worth-the-founders-guide-to-total-wealth-intelligence).

Clearly separated, because the worst modeling error you can make is letting illiquid value blur into money you can spend. Your liquid runway — the months you can cover if income stops — is a cash and brokerage calculation. Watches do not belong in it. A founder who counts a watch collection in emergency reserves has an emergency plan that requires finding a buyer for a Lange during the exact kind of downturn that softens Lange prices.

## A Practical Valuation Routine

You do not need an appraiser on retainer. You need a repeatable routine that removes wishful thinking. Here is one that takes under an hour per quarter for a typical collection.

**Inventory precisely.** Reference number, serial, year, condition grade, box and papers status, service history. Two watches with the same reference can differ 20%+ in value on these details alone, so a valuation that ignores them is a guess.

**Pull sold prices, not asks.** Use platforms and auction results that show completed sales for your specific reference and condition tier. Where only asks are available, apply a haircut — a starting rule of thumb is 10–15% off realistic asks for liquid references, more for slow movers, before fees.

**Subtract selling costs.** Platform fees, shipping and insurance, authentication, or auction commissions. This is the difference between market ask and net realizable value, and skipping it is the single most common inflation in collection valuations.

**Record the number and date, and move on.** The goal is a defensible conservative mark, refreshed quarterly, with a note on method. In IOMI, that mark lives in the collectibles compartment and flows into total net worth automatically — you update the valuation, and every downstream number, from concentration to [your net worth trend](/blog/the-modern-net-worth-tracker-a-founders-guide-to-wealth-intelligence), reflects it without spreadsheet surgery.

**Resist mid-quarter marking.** If prices spike, you will know at the next scheduled revaluation. Chasing marks upward in hot markets and forgetting to mark down in cold ones is exactly the asymmetry this routine exists to prevent.

## Passion Position vs. Investment Position

One more distinction that keeps collectors honest: decide, piece by piece, which watches you hold as investments and which you hold because you love them and would not sell at any sensible price.

The investment pieces get the full discipline — conservative marks, liquidity awareness, a real willingness to sell when the thesis changes. The passion pieces get valued honestly too, but you stop pretending they are portfolio. A watch you will never sell has a net realizable value on paper and a practical liquidity of zero. Knowing which is which changes nothing about the joy of ownership and everything about the quality of your financial decisions.

For most collectors, this split is clarifying rather than painful. It usually reveals that the "investment" story was carrying more weight than it deserved — and that is fine. Collecting does not need an investment justification. It needs an honest line on the balance sheet, at an honest number, in the right category.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are watches a good investment?

A small set of references from a few brands have historically held or grown value; the vast majority of watches depreciate. Even the winners carry no cash flow, high transaction costs, and real cyclicality — the 2022–2024 correction erased much of the preceding boom. Treat watches as a passion-led collectible category where a disciplined buyer might do well, not as a substitute for a portfolio.

### How do I track the value of my watch collection?

Keep a precise inventory (reference, condition, box and papers, service history), revalue quarterly against completed sales rather than listing prices, subtract realistic selling costs, and record the conservative result. Then hold that number inside your total net worth under physical assets and collectibles, separated from liquid assets, so it never inflates the capital you can actually deploy.

### What is my watch actually worth: the listing price or the dealer offer?

Neither extreme, but closer to the dealer offer than most collectors want to hear. Listing prices are asks that sellers rarely achieve in full, and fees plus negotiation typically take 15–30% off. Net realizable value — what lands in your account within 60–90 days — is the honest mark for a balance sheet. Dealer buy-in offers are the fast, certain floor of that range.

### Should watches count in my net worth?

Yes, at conservative net realizable value, in a collectibles category. Excluding them understates your wealth; counting them at inflated marks distorts it. The critical rule is separation: watches belong in total net worth but never in liquidity calculations like emergency runway, because converting them to cash is slow, costly, and worst in downturns.

### How often should I revalue my collection?

Quarterly is the sweet spot for most collections: frequent enough that the trend is current, infrequent enough that you are not chasing market noise or marking up in euphoria. Revalue against completed sales, apply the same method every time, and note the date and basis so future-you can trust past-you's numbers.

### Does insurance value equal market value?

No. Insurance appraisals are typically set at replacement cost — closer to retail or full market ask — because their job is to make you whole at a boutique, not to predict a sale. Using insurance values for net worth systematically overstates the collection. Keep both numbers: insurance value for coverage, net realizable value for your balance sheet.
