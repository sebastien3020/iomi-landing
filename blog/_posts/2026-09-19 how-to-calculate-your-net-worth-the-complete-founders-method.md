# How to Calculate Your Net Worth: The Complete Founder's Method

The formula everyone knows — assets minus liabilities — is the easy part. The hard part is what most guides on **how to calculate net worth** skip entirely: what counts as an asset when your wealth includes a private company, two LLCs, crypto in four places, and a property with a mortgage? What number do you write down for equity that has never traded? Which entity's cash is "yours"? Get these questions wrong and the formula produces a precise answer about a fictional person.

This is the founder's method: a step-by-step process for calculating a net worth figure you can actually trust and maintain — complete inventory, honest valuation rules for the hard assets, entity handling that doesn't lie, and the system that keeps the number current without a monthly spreadsheet ritual.

## Key Takeaways

* Net worth is assets minus liabilities — and for founders, the entire difficulty lives in the inventory and valuation steps, not the arithmetic.
* Completeness first: the most common error isn't mis-valuing an asset, it's omitting whole categories — company equity, entity-held assets, forgotten SAFEs.
* Private assets need a valuation *rule*, not a guess: one method per asset, written down, updated on real events and on a calendar.
* Entities require the double-view: each business compartmentalized, personal separate, and a consolidated total — never one blended number.
* One number isn't enough: track total net worth alongside its liquid portion, because the gap between them governs your real flexibility.

## The Formula Is Trivial. The Inventory Isn't.

Net worth = total assets − total liabilities. Every explanation of how to calculate net worth starts with that formula, and nobody needs help with the subtraction. The reason two founders with identical wealth can compute numbers that differ by millions is everything upstream of it: what got counted, what got valued how, and what got silently skipped.

So the method starts with a complete inventory, and "complete" is the operative word. Walk through every category deliberately — the goal of this pass is *existence*, not valuation. Just list what's there:

* **Cash and equivalents:** every checking, savings, and money-market account — personal and inside each entity. Wallet balances on payment apps count.
* **Brokerage and trading:** taxable investment accounts, wherever held.
* **Retirement:** 401(k)s from every old employer, IRAs, and their international equivalents.
* **Crypto:** exchange accounts and wallets — all of them.
* **Real estate:** every property, personal or entity-held.
* **Private and company equity:** your company stake, angel investments, SAFEs, fund positions.
* **Physical assets and collectibles:** vehicles, watches, art — anything with genuine resale value.
* **Liabilities:** mortgages, business loans, credit lines, personal loans, margin balances, tax obligations already incurred.

Two findings recur in almost every first pass. First, forgotten items — the old 401(k), the SAFE from 2022, the exchange account with a nontrivial balance. These are usually worth more than any valuation refinement you'll ever make. Second, the realization that the biggest line items are the ones with no login: your company, your property, your entities. That's where the method earns its name — and it's why generic calculators built on "link your accounts" produce founder net worth figures that are [structurally incomplete](/blog/the-modern-net-worth-tracker-a-founders-guide-to-wealth-intelligence).

## Valuing the Easy 80%

Most of your line items have observable values, and the only real principles here are freshness and honesty about what "value" means.

**Connected accounts: automate and forget.** Cash, brokerage, retirement, and exchange balances have exact, current values — the only question is whether your calculation sees today's numbers or January's. Manual transcription is where net worth calculations go to die: the first pass is fine, the twelfth never happens. **Real estate: market value, consistently sourced.** Use live market valuations or a recent appraisal, pick one source per property, and stick with it — switching sources between updates injects noise that looks like change. The mortgage goes on the liability side at its current payoff balance; resist the urge to think in "equity" terms during inventory, because netting things early is how double-counting and omissions creep in. Gross assets on one side, all debts on the other, subtract once at the end.

**Vehicles and collectibles: realizable value, honestly.** What it would actually fetch, not what you paid or hope. For collectibles with real markets — watches, art — recent comparable sales; update once or twice a year. If an item's value is emotional rather than market, either exclude it or carry it at a token value. Inflating the fun line items is a victimless crime until you make a liquidity decision based on the total.

**One classification rule throughout:** record what each asset *is*, not where it sits. Crypto held at a broker is crypto. This doesn't change the total, but it determines whether every later analysis — allocation, concentration, risk — reads true. IOMI applies this rule automatically.

## Valuing the Hard 20%: Private Equity

Now the line item that makes or breaks the whole calculation: your company stake, plus any angel checks and SAFEs. There's no ticker, so you need a *rule* — a defensible method, written down, applied consistently.

**For your own company, anchor to real events.** The cleanest marks, in rough order of strength: an actual secondary transaction in your shares; your latest priced round; a 409A valuation; a defensible multiple on revenue or profit benchmarked against comparable companies. Use the strongest anchor available, record the number *and the method and date*, and update when a new event occurs or a year passes — whichever comes first.

**Consider a deliberate haircut.** Many founders carry their stake below the last round's implied value — recognizing illiquidity, future dilution, and the distance between paper and proceeds. A common pattern is tracking two views: the full mark for total net worth, and a discounted or even zeroed version for personal planning, so that no life decision quietly depends on an exit that hasn't happened. Either is defensible; choose one deliberately.

**The discipline that matters more than the method:** update on a calendar, not a mood. Marking your company up in optimistic months and "not getting around to it" in rough ones turns your net worth into a morale dashboard. One method, one schedule, no exceptions.

**For angel checks and SAFEs:** carry at cost until a priced round marks them up or down; a converted SAFE takes the conversion round's value; a company that's quietly dying gets written down, not forgotten at cost. Individually small, these positions are collectively meaningful — and they're the most commonly omitted asset class in founder calculations.

## Handling Entities: The Double-View

If you operate through an LLC or holding company, here's where generic methods fall apart — and where one structural decision fixes everything: compute *two views, never one blend*.

**View one: per-compartment.** Each entity gets its own mini balance sheet: its cash, its assets (property, equipment, investment accounts), its liabilities. Your personal ledger is its own compartment. Within each compartment, the same assets-minus-liabilities arithmetic applies.

**View two: consolidated.** Your economic net worth is your personal net worth plus your ownership share of each entity's net value. This is the headline number — the honest total of what you're worth.

Both views are necessary because they answer different questions. The consolidated view answers "what am I worth?" The compartment view answers everything operational: how much cash the business actually holds, what your *personal* runway looks like without touching entity funds, what a distribution would move from which pocket to which. A single blended number answers neither question well — it inflates your sense of personal liquidity with working capital that has obligations, while burying entity-level signals entirely.

Two technical notes. Avoid double-counting: if you list your company equity as a personal asset (based on a valuation of the whole business), don't *also* add that entity's internal assets to your total — the equity value already contains them. Entities you track internally (your LLC holding property) get consolidated line by line; entities you value externally (your startup at its round price) enter once, as equity. And ownership percentages matter: 60% of an entity means 60% of its net value in your consolidated view.

For most founders the gap is dramatic (60–80% illiquid is common), and the gap is the number that governs real decisions: buffer sizing, risk capacity, "can I afford this?" ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the formula for calculating net worth?

Total assets minus total liabilities. Assets include cash, brokerage and retirement accounts, crypto, real estate, private and company equity, and physical assets with resale value; liabilities include mortgages, loans, credit lines, and incurred tax obligations. The arithmetic is trivial — the quality of the result depends entirely on completeness of the inventory and honesty of the valuations. Learning how to calculate net worth properly is mostly learning that inventory and valuation discipline.

### How do I include my company in my net worth?

Anchor to the strongest real event available: a secondary transaction in your shares, your latest priced round, a 409A valuation, or a defensible multiple benchmarked against comparables. Record the number, method, and date; update on new events or annually. Many founders also carry a deliberate discount — or track a second, conservative view — to reflect illiquidity and future dilution, so that personal planning never silently depends on an unrealized exit.

### Should business assets count in personal net worth?

Economically yes — your ownership share of each entity's net value belongs in your consolidated net worth. Practically, keep two views: compartments (each entity's own balance sheet, personal separate) and consolidation (the honest total). The blended single number is the mistake: it inflates apparent personal liquidity with working capital that has obligations. And avoid double-counting — if you list company equity at a whole-business valuation, don't also add that entity's internal assets.

### How often should I recalculate my net worth?

With automated connections, the liquid 80% recalculates itself — IOMI refreshes balances on every sync. What needs a schedule is the manual 20%: private equity on funding events or annually, real estate when the market or an appraisal moves it, collectibles once or twice a year. The practical review rhythm is monthly: check the trend, identify the driver (markets, savings, valuations, debt), and move on. Total time: minutes.

### What's the biggest mistake people make calculating net worth?

Omission, not mis-valuation. Forgotten 401(k)s, unlisted SAFEs, entity-held assets, and — for founders — entire company stakes left out because "it's not real until the exit." An incomplete inventory produces a precisely computed answer about a person who doesn't exist. The second-biggest: blending business and personal into one number, which corrupts both liquidity planning and entity-level decisions.

### Why track liquid net worth separately from total net worth?

Because the gap between them governs your real flexibility. A founder can be worth $5M with $300k reachable — and every practical decision (cash buffers, risk capacity, big purchases) depends on the $300k, not the $5M. Tracking both numbers, and watching how the liquid share trends, catches a specific failure mode: a rising total masking shrinking flexibility. Most founders find their illiquid share sits at 60–80%, which usually argues for more conservative buffers than generic rules suggest.
