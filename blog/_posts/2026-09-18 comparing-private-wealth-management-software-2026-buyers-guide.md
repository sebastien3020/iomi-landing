# Comparing Private Wealth Management Software: 2026 Buyer's Guide

The market for **private wealth management software** has quietly split in two. One branch serves institutions — advisor platforms, family-office suites, reporting engines priced like enterprise contracts. The other serves individuals directly: founders, operators, and self-directed investors who want institutional-grade oversight of their own wealth without hiring an institution to get it. This guide is about the second branch, because that's where most of the confusion — and most of the buying mistakes — live.

The confusion is understandable: tools with very different jobs share the same shelf. Budgeting apps, portfolio trackers, net-worth aggregators, and wealth-intelligence platforms all describe themselves in similar language. This guide gives you a decision framework: what to evaluate, how the main options honestly compare, what pricing models reveal about incentives, and how to run a trial that produces a confident answer in two weeks.

## Key Takeaways

* Start from your balance-sheet complexity, not from feature lists: entity structures and private assets disqualify most consumer tools immediately.
* The market has four distinct categories — budgeting apps, pure trackers, AI chat tools, and wealth-intelligence platforms — and most bad purchases come from confusing them.
* Non-negotiables: read-only connectivity, full asset-class coverage, honest classification, and entity separation. Everything else is preference.
* Pricing reveals incentives: flat subscriptions align the tool with your clarity; free tools and percentage fees align it with something else.
* A structured two-week trial — connect everything, test your three hardest questions — beats any review, including this one.

## Start With Your Balance Sheet, Not Their Features

Software comparisons usually begin with feature matrices. Buying decisions should begin with a mirror. Answer four questions about your own situation first, because they do most of the filtering:

**Do you operate through entities?** If you have an LLC, a holding company, or several of each, entity compartmentalization is your first-order requirement — and it eliminates most of the market outright. Tools built on the linked-personal-accounts model can't represent your finances, however good their charts.

**Do private assets matter to you?** Company equity, angel checks, SAFEs, real estate, collectibles. If yes, you need a platform that treats manually valued assets as first-class citizens next to synced accounts — not a "miscellaneous" bucket.

**What's your actual job-to-be-done?** Be precise. "Control monthly spending" is a budgeting job. "Know what I own and how it's performing" is a tracking job. "Know what I own, how well it's deployed, and what to do next" is an intelligence job. Different jobs, different tools, and no tool does all three well.

**Who else needs the picture?** Just you? You and a spouse? You and a CPA at year-end? The answer shapes how much structure you need.

Founders and operators typically land on: entities yes, private assets yes, intelligence job, small circle. That profile is who this guide serves — and if your answers came out simpler (no entities, mostly liquid, budgeting job), you'll be better served and charged less by a simpler tool. Knowing that *before* comparing features is the guide's first practical payoff.

## The Non-Negotiables Checklist

Whatever corner of the private wealth management software market you buy from, five requirements shouldn't bend. Use them as your disqualification round:

**1. Read-only by architecture.** The platform sees balances and transactions and structurally cannot move money. Credentials never stored by or visible to the tool. For software aggregating your entire financial life, this is the security conversation that matters — be wary of vague reassurance language as a substitute for the read-only design fact.

**2. Full asset-class coverage.** Bank and wallet accounts, brokerage, retirement, crypto across exchanges and wallets, real estate with live valuations, private and company equity, physical assets and collectibles, liabilities. Missing any class you own means a permanently wrong total.

**3. Honest classification.** Assets classified by what they are, not the institution holding them. Crypto at a broker is crypto. This single design choice determines whether concentration and risk figures reflect reality.

**4. Entity separation with consolidation.** Compartments per business entity, a personal ledger, a consolidated toggle. Blended totals are fiction for anyone operating through companies, which is why this requirement dominates for founders.

**5. Grounded intelligence, if intelligence is claimed.** Any analysis, score, or AI layer must compute from your actual data and admit gaps rather than estimate. Deterministic simulation you can interrogate beats probabilistic output you must trust.

Then there's the differentiator round — features that separate finalists once the non-negotiables pass: efficiency scoring with a factor breakdown, deterministic simulation, projections from real holdings, goals with status-quo trajectories, a daily brief, and a grounded assistant. This layer is where the [software-vs-advisor question](/blog/wealth-management-software-vs-advisor-the-2026-founders-framework) also resolves — the more of the analytical work software does, the more precisely you can scope (and price) any human advice you add.

## What Pricing Models Tell You

Price is information about incentives, not just cost. Three models dominate:

**Free.** A financial tool with no subscription monetizes another way: ads, data, or cross-sold financial products. That last one matters most — a "free" dashboard that earns referral fees on loans and investment products is a distribution channel wearing a dashboard costume, and its recommendations inherit the conflict.

**Percentage of assets.** Standard for advisory services, occasionally attached to software. The math deserves attention: 1% of a $2M balance sheet is $20,000 every year, growing with your assets, largely for reporting and monitoring that flat-fee software now does continuously. Paying percentage fees specifically for *judgment* can be rational; paying them for *software functions* is a rounding error away from a mistake.

**Flat subscription.** You pay for the tool; clarity is the product; incentives stay clean. No commissions, nothing cross-sold, no fee that scales with your success. IOMI's model: [Personal at $29/month, Business at $39/month](https://iomifinance.com/pricing) — founding rates, locked while subscribed (regular $79) — annual billing worth two months free, and a 1-month free trial with no credit card required.

A last cost consideration that outweighs price: switching costs are real (private-asset data entry, valuation history, learned workflows), so the platform's *scope ceiling* matters. Buying a personal-scale tool while your entity structure grows means migrating in eighteen months. Buy for the balance sheet you're building, not just the one you have.

## Run a Two-Week Trial That Actually Decides

Reviews — this one included — can only narrow the field. The decision should come from a structured trial on your real data. The protocol:

**Days 1–2: Connect everything.** All institutions, personal and business. You're testing connection coverage and sync reliability on *your* accounts — the only coverage that matters. Note anything that fails or needs workarounds.

**Days 3–4: Build the private side.** Enter company equity at a defensible mark, entities with their holdings, property, liabilities. This tests the tool's real capacity for your complexity: if private assets feel bolted-on now, they'll feel worse in year two.

**Week 2: Ask your three hardest questions.** Write down, before trialing, the three questions you most want answered — for example: What's my true net worth including everything? How concentrated am I across accounts and entities? What happens to personal runway if I stop distributions for a year? Then test whether the tool answers them from your data, without a spreadsheet on the side. If the tool can't answer them cleanly, no feature list compensates.

**Throughout: Watch your own behavior.** The meta-signal that predicts long-term value: are you checking the tool because it's informative, or avoiding it because it's work? A platform that fits disappears into your routine within a week — a daily brief you actually read, a dashboard that answers before you finish asking.

Score the finalists against the non-negotiables, your three questions, and the behavior signal — then commit and build. The compounding value of any of these platforms comes from history and habit, both of which start when the evaluation stops. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is private wealth management software?

It's software that gives an individual the consolidated oversight an institution would build for a client: every asset and liability in one live view — bank, brokerage, retirement, crypto, real estate, private equity, collectibles — with analysis on top. The individual-facing branch splits into budgeting apps, pure trackers, and wealth-intelligence platforms like IOMI, which add scoring, projection, and simulation to the consolidated balance sheet.

### How is wealth management software different from a budgeting app?

Budgeting apps manage flows — income, spending categories, monthly discipline — and are the right tool for that job; Monarch is a strong example. Wealth management software manages the balance sheet: assets, liabilities, entities, allocation, efficiency, and trajectory. The categories overlap in marketing language but not in architecture, and the most common buying mistake is purchasing one to do the other's job.

### Which tool is best for founders with LLCs and private equity?

The requirements that filter the market: entity compartments (each LLC or holding company tracked separately, consolidated on demand), private equity and manually valued assets as first-class entries, read-only connectivity, and classification by asset nature. IOMI is built specifically for this profile with up to 3 entities on the Business plan. Pure trackers like Kubera handle broad assets but differ on entity structure and the intelligence layer — worth testing both against your real data.

### Is it safe to connect all my accounts to one platform?

The architecture to insist on is read-only aggregation: IOMI connects through Plaid to 12,000+ institutions, can see balances and transactions, and structurally cannot move money — bank credentials are never stored by or visible to the platform, and there's no custody or execution capability. That design fact matters more than any marketing reassurance: full visibility, zero access is the correct posture for a tool of this scope.

### How much should wealth management software cost?

Flat-subscription tools for individuals generally run $10–$50 per month depending on scope. The comparison to weigh isn't tool-vs-tool pennies but model-vs-model: a flat $39/month is roughly $470 a year, while a 1% assets-under-management fee on a $2M balance sheet is $20,000 a year, growing with your wealth. Pay percentage fees for human judgment if you need it — not for reporting and monitoring that software does continuously.

### Should I use free wealth tracking tools?

Understand the model first. Free tools monetize through ads, data, or referral fees on financial products cross-sold to you — the last of which quietly shapes what the tool shows and recommends. For a simple balance sheet and casual tracking, that trade may be acceptable. For serious oversight of a complex balance sheet, a flat subscription keeps the product and the incentive identical: your clarity.

### How long does it take to evaluate and set up a platform properly?

Two weeks of trial answers the buying question: two days connecting accounts, two days building the private side (entities, equity marks, property, liabilities), then a week testing your three hardest questions against your real data. Setup itself is front-loaded — an afternoon of focused work — after which maintenance drops to minutes per month, since connected accounts refresh on every sync and private assets update on real events.
