# Finding a Financial Advisor for Entrepreneurs: When You Need One (and When Software Is Enough)

Searching for a **financial advisor for entrepreneurs** usually starts with a specific pain. Your company just raised a round. You sold a property. You have cash piling up in a business account and no plan for it. The instinct is to find a professional. That instinct is sometimes right. But for many founders, the real problem is not a missing advisor. It is missing visibility.

This guide gives you a practical framework: what an advisor actually does, which founder situations genuinely require one, which situations are better served by wealth software, and how to combine both without paying twice for the same work. No sales pitch for either side. Advisors and software solve different problems, and knowing which problem you have is most of the decision.

## Key Takeaways

* An advisor's core value for founders is judgment on irreversible, high-stakes decisions: exits, estate structure, complex tax situations, and concentrated stock. Software cannot replace that.
* Most day-to-day founder questions — what do I own, where is my cash idle, what happens if I move $50k — are visibility and modeling questions, not advice questions.
* Traditional advisors often struggle with entrepreneur balance sheets: private company equity, multiple LLCs, crypto, and real estate rarely fit the standard AUM model.
* A common failure mode is paying ~1% of assets per year mostly for reporting — work that flat-fee software now does continuously.
* The strongest setup for many founders is a hybrid: software for continuous oversight and simulation, an advisor hired for specific decisions, paid flat or hourly.

## What a Financial Advisor Actually Does (and Doesn't)

Strip away the branding and an advisor delivers four things: planning, investment management, coordination, and judgment.

Planning is the roadmap — retirement targets, insurance coverage, education funding, estate documents. Investment management is portfolio construction and rebalancing, typically for a fee around 1% of assets under management. Coordination means quarterbacking your CPA, attorney, and insurance agent so their work fits together. Judgment is the intangible: a person who has seen a hundred exits telling you what founders regret.

Here is what most advisors do not do well: they do not see your whole balance sheet. The standard advisory relationship covers the assets the advisor manages. Your company equity, your rental property, your LLC operating accounts, your crypto — these usually sit outside the managed portfolio, acknowledged in an annual review and ignored the rest of the year. For a salaried executive whose wealth is a brokerage account and a 401(k), that gap is small. For a founder whose largest asset is a private company, the gap is most of the picture.

That is why the search for a **financial advisor for entrepreneurs** is harder than it looks. You are not looking for portfolio management. You are looking for someone who understands a balance sheet where the biggest line item has no ticker symbol.

## The Founder's Balance Sheet Problem

Entrepreneur wealth is structurally different from employee wealth, and the differences are exactly where standard advice models break.

### Concentration is the default, not a mistake

Textbook advice says never hold more than 10% in one asset. Founders routinely hold 60–90% of their net worth in one private company. That is not a portfolio error to be corrected; it is the strategy. What you need is not a lecture on diversification but a clear view of what the concentration means: how much of your net worth is illiquid, what your liquid runway looks like if the company stalls, and what a partial secondary sale would change.

### Income is lumpy and entity-shaped

A founder's cash flow arrives through entities: distributions from an LLC, a salary you set yourself, occasional liquidity events. Advice built on "save 20% of your paycheck" does not map. The useful questions are entity questions — how much cash should the business hold, when to distribute, how personal runway interacts with business runway. Most advisors are not set up to track [personal and business assets side by side](/blog/tracking-personal-vs-business-assets-the-founders-guide-to-total-oversight), so those questions get answered from memory, once a year.

### The asset mix doesn't fit the custodian

Private equity, real estate, crypto held on exchanges and in wallets, collectibles — a typical founder portfolio is full of assets an advisor cannot custody and often will not model. When your advisor's software can't hold the asset, the asset disappears from the plan.

## When You Genuinely Need a Human Advisor

Software has real limits. There are founder situations where hiring a professional is clearly the right call.

**A liquidity event is on the horizon.** If you expect to sell your company or a meaningful stake in the next 24 months, the planning that happens *before* the transaction — entity structure, timing, charitable vehicles, estate moves — can matter enormously, and much of it is irreversible after the sale. This is specialist work involving your attorney and CPA, and an experienced advisor who has run the play before earns their fee.

**Your estate situation has teeth.** Multiple entities, children, assets in more than one country, or a net worth approaching estate-tax territory: these need documents, trustees, and legal judgment. No dashboard drafts a trust.

**Complex tax coordination.** IOMI, for the record, is not a tax engine, and no tracking tool should pretend to be one. Multi-state or multi-country exposure, QSBS questions, equity compensation planning — that is CPA and advisor territory.

**You will not act without accountability.** Some people know exactly what to do and don't do it. If having a person who calls you is what makes you execute, that service has real value. Be honest with yourself about whether you're paying for expertise or for discipline.

**A pattern worth noticing:** every one of these is an *episode* — a decision, a transaction, a document. None of them requires a permanent percentage of your assets. Which leads to the fee conversation.

## The Hybrid Model: Software First, Advice on Demand

The setup that fits most founders is not either/or. It is a layered system.

**Layer one: continuous oversight through software.** Everything connected, personal and business compartmentalized, net worth and concentration visible daily. This is your default state, and it costs a flat subscription. A good wealth management foundation means you are never assembling spreadsheets to figure out where you stand.

**Layer two: episodic professional advice.** When one of the genuine advisor triggers fires — exit planning, estate work, complex tax coordination — hire the right specialist for that episode. Prefer flat-fee or hourly fee-only advisors (fiduciaries who sell no products) over AUM arrangements, because your need is episodic and their expertise is what you're buying, not custody.

## How to Vet an Advisor If You Hire One

If you've matched yourself to the "genuinely need one" list, vet candidates like you'd vet a key hire.

**Demand fiduciary status in writing.** A fiduciary is legally required to act in your interest. Many people with "advisor" on their card are held only to a suitability standard and earn commissions on what they sell you. Ask directly: "Are you a fiduciary at all times, for all accounts?"

**Interrogate the fee model.** Fee-only (flat, hourly, or retainer) removes the deepest conflicts. If the answer is AUM-based, ask what you get beyond portfolio management, and price the reporting component honestly against software before you sign.

**Test them on entrepreneur problems.** Ask how many clients hold most of their net worth in a private business. Ask how they model entity structures, how they'd think about a partial secondary, what they do with crypto and real estate. Vague answers mean their practice is built for a different client.

**Check the record.** Look up the firm and the individual on the SEC's Investment Adviser Public Disclosure site and FINRA BrokerCheck. Disclosures and disputes are public.

**Clarify what "seeing your finances" means.** If the advisor's view of your wealth will be limited to the accounts they manage, understand that going in — and keep your own consolidated view regardless. The advisor works for you; the system of record should be yours.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do entrepreneurs need a financial advisor?

For specific situations — exit planning, estate structuring, complex multi-entity tax coordination, or a major liquidity event — yes, an experienced professional is worth hiring. For ongoing questions like net worth tracking, idle cash detection, concentration monitoring, and scenario modeling, software now handles the work continuously at a flat cost. Many founders run a hybrid: software for oversight, specialists hired per decision.

### How much does a financial advisor cost for a founder?

The most common model is a percentage of assets under management, typically around 1% per year — $20,000 annually on a $2M portfolio, growing as your assets grow. Fee-only alternatives charge flat annual retainers (often $5,000–$15,000) or hourly rates. Before paying AUM fees, separate what you're buying: judgment on hard decisions is worth paying for; reporting and monitoring is what software does for a flat monthly subscription.

### What should I look for in a financial advisor as an entrepreneur?

Four things: fiduciary status in writing at all times; a fee structure without product commissions; demonstrated experience with clients whose wealth is concentrated in private businesses and spread across entities; and clear answers on how they handle assets they don't manage, like company equity, real estate, and crypto. If their process can't see most of your balance sheet, their advice starts from an incomplete picture.

### Can software replace a financial advisor?

Software replaces the reporting, aggregation, and modeling portions of advisory work — often the majority of what the fee pays for. It does not replace legal judgment, estate documents, tax strategy, or the experience of someone who has guided many exits. IOMI is explicit about this line: it consolidates your full financial picture, scores capital efficiency, and simulates moves deterministically, but it does not provide personalized investment advice.

### What is a fiduciary, and why does it matter?

A fiduciary is legally obligated to act in your best interest rather than merely recommending "suitable" products. Non-fiduciary advisors can earn commissions on what they sell you, which quietly shapes recommendations. Asking "are you a fiduciary at all times, for all accounts, in writing?" is the fastest filter in the vetting process — and any hesitation is your answer.
