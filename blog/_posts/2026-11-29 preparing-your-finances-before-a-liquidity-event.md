# Preparing Your Finances Before a Liquidity Event

Founders spend years preparing their company for an exit and about three weeks preparing themselves. The data room gets polished; the personal balance sheet stays a guess. Then the wire lands, and a person who has operated on lumpy income and illiquid equity for a decade suddenly holds more liquid capital than they have ever managed — with no plan, no structure, and a dozen people offering to help manage it. Learning how to **prepare finances before exit** is not a luxury for the already-rich; it is the difference between a liquidity event that compounds and one that dissipates.

This guide is the personal-side checklist: what to do in the 24 months, 12 months, and 90 days before a sale, secondary, or major distribution. It covers visibility, entity hygiene, scenario modeling, team assembly, and the discipline of deciding things before the money arrives. It is educational — the tax and legal specifics of your situation belong with your CPA and attorney, and this article will say so more than once, because the irreversible mistakes live there.

## Key Takeaways

* Start personal exit prep 12–24 months out: the highest-value moves — entity structure, planning with your CPA and attorney — are time-locked and often irreversible after signing.
* Get complete visibility first: you cannot plan a liquidity event on top of a balance sheet you cannot see; consolidation is step zero, not a post-exit chore.
* Separate personal and business finances completely before diligence starts — blurred boundaries cost time, trust, and sometimes deal value.
* Model your post-exit picture deterministically before the money arrives: net proceeds scenarios, runway, and allocation, so the wire lands into a plan instead of a vacuum.
* Decide your first-12-months allocation rules in advance, including a deliberate do-nothing period for most of the capital.

## Why Preparation Beats Reaction

Exits reward preparation asymmetrically. Almost everything valuable you can do happens before the transaction: structuring, planning conversations with professionals, cleaning the personal balance sheet, modeling scenarios. Almost nothing valuable happens in the week after the wire except executing decisions you already made — or improvising, which is where post-exit regret is manufactured.

There is a reason the improvisation fails. A liquidity event changes three things at once: the size of your liquid wealth, the visibility of it (people will know), and your own psychology (sudden capital produces either paralysis or a spending-and-investing spree; rarely calm). Every one of those is easier to handle with rules you set while the money was still theoretical.

The window matters, too. Several of the most consequential pre-exit moves — how equity is held, what planning structures exist, timing questions your CPA will want to discuss — have deadlines measured in months or years before the transaction, not days. If a sale is plausible within two years, the preparation clock has already started.

## 24 Months Out: Build Total Visibility

You cannot prepare what you cannot see. The first block of work is unglamorous: assemble one complete, current picture of everything you own and owe — personal and business, liquid and illiquid.

That means every account across every institution, your equity position as it actually is (share classes, vesting, any secondary sales already done), real estate with live valuations, crypto across exchange accounts and wallets, collectibles, and every liability including personal guarantees tied to the business. If assembling this takes you more than a day, that is the point: it should take minutes, continuously, and today it doesn't.

Two numbers fall out of the exercise that shape everything downstream:

**Your liquid net worth today.** What you could access in a month, excluding the company. **Your true concentration.** For most founders approaching exit, the company is 70–90% of net worth. The exit is, among other things, the single largest de-risking event of your life — which is exactly why the receiving side of it deserves a plan.

## 18 Months Out: Clean the Personal–Business Boundary

Diligence will examine the company's finances. It goes faster, and reads better, when the boundary between company and founder is surgically clean: no personal expenses drifting through the business, no business assets parked in personal accounts, no undocumented loans in either direction, distributions on a defensible rhythm.

Beyond diligence optics, the boundary matters for you. Post-exit, your relationship to the entity changes or ends; anything tangled across the boundary becomes friction at exactly the moment you want clean closure. Track the two sides as separate compartments with a consolidated view on top — the structural discipline described in [tracking personal vs. business assets](/blog/tracking-personal-vs-business-assets-the-founders-guide-to-total-oversight) — and the cleanup becomes a checklist instead of an archaeology project.

This is also the window to inventory personal guarantees. Loans, leases, and credit lines you have personally guaranteed connect entity risk to your personal balance sheet; some will need to be released or renegotiated as part of the transaction. Surfacing them 18 months out means they are handled in the deal; discovering them in closing week means delay.

## 12 Months Out: Assemble the Team and Have the Structuring Conversations

This is the block where "educational article" hands off to "your professionals," and the handoff is the advice.

**CPA first.** Exit taxation is deeply situation-specific — entity type, holding periods, state and country exposure, how the deal is structured. Some of the most valuable planning is only available well before a transaction. IOMI is not a tax engine and this article is not tax advice: get a CPA experienced in transactions of your size and type, and get them early enough to matter.

**Attorney for the structural questions.** How proceeds are received, estate implications, any trust or entity planning appropriate to your situation. Like tax planning, much of this is irreversible after signing — which is why it appears at 12 months and not 12 days.

**Decide what role, if any, an advisor plays.** Post-exit, you will be pitched constantly. Deciding your advice model in advance — what you handle with software and your own judgment, what you hire for by the decision, whether you want ongoing management at all — inoculates you against deciding it under pitch pressure. The framework for that decision is laid out in [wealth management software vs. advisor](/blog/wealth-management-software-vs-advisor-the-2026-founders-framework).

The pattern across all three: your leverage is time. Founders who prepare finances before exit conversations get serious have professionals working from a complete picture with room to act; every one of these conversations is cheap and productive with a year of runway and expensive or moot without it.

## 6 Months Out: Model the After

Now the modeling block: turn the theoretical exit into concrete scenarios while changes are still free.

**Model net proceeds, not headline price.** Deal price minus debt, transaction costs, any escrows and holdbacks, and taxes (per your CPA's estimates) — across a conservative, expected, and optimistic case. Founders anchor on the headline and are reliably surprised by the distance to net. Put the three cases in writing.

**Simulate the post-exit balance sheet.** Take each net-proceeds case and model what your total picture looks like: liquidity, allocation, income produced, runway at your intended lifestyle. **Set your goals in numbers.** What is the capital for? Runway for the next venture, income floor for the family, a target net worth with a date? ## 90 Days Out: Write the Rules for the First Year

The last block is behavioral. Decide now, in writing, what happens in the twelve months after the wire.

**The do-nothing period.** Commit to leaving the bulk of proceeds unallocated for a defined period — commonly six to twelve months — in safe, liquid form while you adjust. Nearly every experienced post-exit founder endorses some version of this rule; nearly every regret story starts with deploying fast. Note the nuance: parked-by-plan is not the same as idle-by-neglect. A deliberate reserve with a review date is strategy; the same cash still sitting there unexamined two years later is the problem described in [idle cash: how much is too much](/blog/idle-cash-how-much-is-too-much).

**Allocation rules, pre-written.** After the do-nothing period, what gets deployed, where, on what schedule? Rules written before the money arrives are reasoned; rules written after are rationalized.

**A pitch policy.** Decide in advance how you respond to the inbound: the deals, the funds, the friends' rounds. A standard answer ("everything goes through my quarterly review") protects both your capital and your relationships.

**Your monitoring cadence.** Post-exit, your finances change shape — from one dominant illiquid asset to a portfolio that needs actual oversight. Decide the rhythm now: a daily thirty-second brief, a weekly efficiency check, a monthly review against goals. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How early should I prepare my finances before an exit?

Start 12–24 months before a plausible transaction. The highest-value items — tax and structuring conversations with your CPA and attorney, entity cleanup, equity housekeeping — are time-locked and often irreversible after signing. Visibility and modeling work (consolidating your balance sheet, simulating scenarios) can start today regardless of timeline, and gets more valuable the earlier it exists.

### What should I do first to prepare finances before an exit?

Build one complete, current picture of everything you own and owe — every account, your equity as it actually is, real estate, crypto, collectibles, and all liabilities including personal guarantees. Everything else in exit prep (structuring, modeling, allocation rules) builds on that picture. If assembling it takes more than a day today, put consolidation infrastructure in place first.

### How do I estimate what I'll actually receive from a sale?

Model net proceeds, not headline price: deal price minus debt, transaction costs, escrows and holdbacks, and taxes per your CPA's estimates — in a conservative, expected, and optimistic case. The distance between headline and net reliably surprises founders. The tax component is deeply situation-specific, which is why a transaction-experienced CPA belongs on the team a year out, not a month out.

### What should I do with the money right after the exit?

Less than you think, deliberately. Most experienced founders endorse a defined do-nothing period — often six to twelve months with the bulk of proceeds in safe, liquid form — while you adjust and execute rules you wrote before closing. Pre-written allocation rules, a standard response to investment pitches, and a monitoring cadence do more for outcomes than any fast deployment.

### Why does concentration matter so much before a liquidity event?

Because for most founders, the company is 70–90% of net worth going in — meaning the exit is the largest single de-risking event of their lives, and the receiving side of it (how the suddenly liquid capital gets deployed) resets their entire risk profile. Knowing your true concentration and liquidity before the event is what lets you design the after, instead of discovering it.
