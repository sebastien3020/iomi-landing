# What Counts as an Asset? The Founder's Classification Guide

Ask ten founders **what counts as an asset** and you will get ten different balance sheets. One counts his company equity at the last round's valuation; another leaves it out entirely because "it's not liquid." One includes her watch collection; another says collectibles are hobbies, not holdings. Someone counts the car, someone doesn't, and almost nobody agrees on how to treat the money sitting in the LLC's operating account.

This matters more than it sounds. If your definition of an asset is inconsistent, your net worth is a mood, not a number — and every decision built on it inherits the fuzziness. This guide gives you a working definition, a classification system that handles the messy founder cases, and the principle that keeps the whole thing honest: classify holdings by what they are, not where they sit.

## Key Takeaways

* An asset, for net worth purposes, is anything you own that has measurable monetary value and could, in principle, be converted to cash — regardless of how long that conversion would take.
* Founders should classify holdings by what they are, not where they sit: crypto held at a brokerage is still crypto; cash in an LLC account is still cash, held by an entity you own.
* Private company equity is an asset and belongs on your balance sheet — with a defensible, conservative valuation and an explicit liquidity label.
* Personal-use items follow a simple rule: count it if it has a real resale market and meaningful value; give it a conservative value or skip it if it doesn't.
* The classification only pays off when it is tracked continuously — a one-time inventory decays; a live system compounds.

## The Working Definition

Strip away accounting formalities and the definition is short: an asset is something you own that has measurable monetary value. Three words in that sentence do the work.

**Own.** You must hold the economic interest. Money you manage for someone else is not your asset. Vested equity is yours; unvested equity is a claim that becomes yours on a schedule, which is why it deserves its own line rather than silent inclusion.

**Measurable.** There must be a defensible way to put a number on it. Public stocks are trivially measurable. A private company requires a method — last round, a revenue multiple, a conservative haircut — but a method exists. Your reputation, network, and future earning power are real but not measurable, so they stay off the balance sheet.

**Monetary value.** It could be exchanged for money, even if slowly. A rental property takes months to sell; it is still an asset. Your unused domain names have a thin market; they are still assets, just small ones.

Liabilities are the mirror image: debts you owe. Net worth is the difference. The mechanics of putting the two together are covered in [how to calculate your net worth](/blog/what-is-my-net-worth-the-founders-guide-to-total-wealth-intelligence); this guide is about getting the asset side classified correctly first.

## The Principle That Fixes Most Arguments: Classify by Nature, Not Location

Here is the single most useful rule in asset classification, and the one most tools get wrong: a holding is what it is, regardless of where it sits.

Crypto held in a brokerage account is crypto, not "brokerage." A money-market fund inside your trading account is cash-like liquidity, not equities. Company stock inside a retirement account is still concentrated equity exposure. Cash in your LLC's checking account is cash — held by an entity, with implications for how you access it, but its nature is cash.

Why does this matter? Because your real questions are nature questions. "How exposed am I to crypto?" requires summing crypto everywhere it lives. "How much liquidity do I have?" requires finding every cash-like position regardless of which institution holds it. If your classification follows account labels instead of asset nature, both answers come out wrong.

## The Eight Classes of a Founder's Balance Sheet

A classification system needs categories that are few enough to stay usable and precise enough to be meaningful. For founder wealth, eight classes cover essentially everything.

### 1. Bank and wallet accounts

Checking, savings, money market accounts, and cash-equivalent wallets. This is your liquidity layer — the capital available this week. Include personal accounts and, separately compartmentalized, each business entity's operating accounts.

### 2. Brokerage and trading

Public equities, ETFs, bonds, and funds in taxable accounts. Liquid within days. Note the nature rule: if the account also holds crypto or large cash sweeps, those positions belong to their own classes.

### 3. Retirement accounts

401(k)s, IRAs, and their international equivalents. Real assets with a time lock. Count them fully, label the access constraint, and keep the specific rules conversation for your CPA — the point here is classification, not tax strategy.

### 4. Crypto

Exchange accounts and wallets. Sum both — a picture that includes your exchange balance but forgets wallet holdings, or vice versa, systematically misstates your exposure. How to keep that consolidated view honest is its own topic, covered in [one honest crypto picture](/blog/cold-wallets-and-exchange-balances-one-honest-crypto-picture).

### 5. Real estate

Primary residence, rentals, land, and fractional interests. Use live market-based valuations where available rather than purchase price — a property bought in 2016 and carried at cost misstates today's balance sheet, in either direction.

### 6. Private equity and company equity

Your own company, angel investments, fund positions. For most founders this is the largest asset and the most commonly mishandled — either left off entirely ("too uncertain") or carried at a hope-based number. Both errors are worse than a conservative estimate with a stated method. More on valuation below.

### 7. Physical assets and collectibles

Watches, cars, art, instruments — items with genuine resale markets. The inclusion test: could you realistically sell it, and is the value meaningful relative to your balance sheet? A $40k watch collection, yes. A $900 bicycle, skip it or accept the noise.

### 8. Liabilities

Mortgages, credit lines, business loans you have personally guaranteed. Not an asset class, but the balance sheet is incomplete without the other column, and personal guarantees in particular deserve explicit tracking because they connect entity risk to personal wealth.

## The Hard Cases, Settled

The eight classes handle the easy 80%. Founders live in the hard 20%, so let's settle the recurring disputes.

**Your company equity.** It counts. The objection — "it's illiquid and the valuation is soft" — argues for careful labeling, not exclusion. Excluding your largest asset does not make your balance sheet conservative; it makes it wrong. Use a defensible method: the last priced round with a haircut for preference stacks and illiquidity, or a conservative revenue multiple if there is no recent round. Update it when reality changes, not when optimism does. And label it illiquid, so your liquid runway calculation never leans on it. The reasoning is developed further in [liquid vs. illiquid net worth](/blog/liquid-vs-illiquid-net-worth-why-the-split-matters).

**Unvested equity and options.** Track them, but on their own line, outside your core net worth. They are a scheduled claim, not a current holding. When they vest, they migrate to the equity class.

**Business assets inside your entities.** The company's cash, equipment, and receivables belong to the entity, and your asset is the equity in that entity. This is exactly why entity compartments matter: mixing the LLC's operating cash into your personal cash overstates your personal liquidity and understates the business's. Keep each entity as its own unit with a consolidated view on top — the approach detailed in [tracking personal vs. business assets](/blog/tracking-personal-vs-business-assets-the-founders-guide-to-total-oversight).

**The primary residence.** It counts — it is owned, measurable, and convertible. The practical caveat is that you live in it, so treat its value as illiquid and never count it toward runway. If including it feels like it flatters the number, the fix is the liquid/illiquid split, not exclusion.

**Cars and depreciating equipment.** Count vehicles at conservative market value if the value is meaningful; accept that the line trends down. Most everyday equipment — laptops, furniture, tools — fails the meaningful-value test and adds noise. Skip it.

**Money owed to you.** A documented loan to a friend or a founder note is an asset at face value discounted by your honest estimate of collection. A vague "he owes me" is not.

**Domain names, IP, and side-project assets.** If it produces income or has a demonstrated market, count it conservatively. If it is potential, it is not yet an asset.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What counts as an asset when calculating net worth?

Anything you own with measurable monetary value that could be converted to cash: bank balances, brokerage and retirement accounts, crypto in exchange accounts and wallets, real estate, private company equity, and physical assets with real resale markets like watches, cars, and art. Subtract liabilities — mortgages, loans, credit balances — to get net worth. The practical test is threefold: do you own it, can you defensibly value it, and does a market for it exist.

### Does my company count as an asset?

Yes. Your equity in a private company is typically a founder's largest asset, and leaving it off the balance sheet makes the picture wrong, not conservative. Value it with a defensible method — last priced round with a haircut, or a conservative multiple — label it illiquid, and update the number when reality changes. Never count it toward liquid runway.

### Is a car an asset or a liability?

The car itself is an asset — it is owned, measurable, and sellable — while the loan against it is a liability. List both. Practically, count vehicles at conservative resale value when the value is meaningful relative to your balance sheet, and accept the line trends downward. A leased car is not your asset; it is someone else's asset you are renting.

### Do personal items like watches or art count as assets?

Count them when two things are true: a genuine resale market exists, and the value is meaningful relative to your total picture. A collection of watches or art with auction comparables belongs on the balance sheet at conservative values. Ordinary possessions — furniture, electronics, clothes — technically have value but add noise without insight, and most people rightly exclude them.

### Are retirement accounts assets if I can't touch them for decades?

Yes, fully. Access restrictions affect liquidity, not ownership — the money is yours. The correct treatment is to count retirement balances at full value and label them as time-locked, so your liquid-versus-illiquid split stays honest. Questions about withdrawal rules and penalties are for your CPA; classification does not depend on them.

### Is money in my business bank account my asset?

Not directly — it belongs to the entity, and your asset is your equity in that entity. The distinction matters: mixing business cash into personal liquidity overstates what you can personally spend and blurs how the business is actually doing. Track each entity as its own compartment with a consolidated view on top, which is how IOMI structures personal versus business wealth.

### How should I value assets that don't have a market price?

Use a stated, conservative method and apply it consistently. Private equity: last round minus an illiquidity haircut, or a conservative revenue multiple. Real estate: live market-based estimates rather than purchase price. Collectibles: recent comparable sales at the low end. The goal is not precision — it is a number you can defend, updated on evidence rather than optimism.
