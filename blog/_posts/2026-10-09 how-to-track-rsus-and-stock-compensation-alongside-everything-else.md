# How to Track RSUs and Stock Compensation Alongside Everything Else

For a growing class of operators, stock compensation is not a bonus — it is the majority of total pay and, within a few years, the largest line on the balance sheet. Yet **RSU tracking** is where most people's financial picture quietly falls apart. The brokerage app shows vested shares. The equity portal shows the grant schedule. Neither shows what you actually want to know: what is this worth, how much of my net worth does it represent, and what happens to my position at the next vest?

The failure is not laziness; it is that RSUs sit awkwardly between categories. They are income you have not received, equity you do not fully own, and concentration risk you did not choose. This guide covers how to track stock compensation honestly — vested and unvested, public and private — as one part of a complete net worth picture rather than a mystery number in a separate tab.

## Key Takeaways

- RSUs belong in your net worth picture as equity holdings — tracked alongside your accounts, property, and crypto, not in a separate spreadsheet that never agrees with anything else.
- The vested/unvested distinction is the core of honest RSU tracking: vested shares are yours; unvested grants are a schedule of future events, not present wealth.
- Stock compensation is concentration by default: your income and a growing share of your assets depend on one employer. Measuring that share is step one of managing it.
- In IOMI, RSUs and stock compensation are tracked as part of the private equity / company equity asset class, side by side with every other holding, and counted in the Capital Efficiency Score's concentration and risk balance factors.
- Vesting events are plannable cash-flow events: simulate them in advance and decide your hold/diversify policy before the shares land, not after.

## Why RSUs Disappear From the Financial Picture

Ask someone with four years of RSU grants what their net worth is and you will usually get a number that excludes most of their equity — or includes all of it, unvested and all, at a price from last quarter. Both answers are wrong in opposite directions, and both come from the same root: RSUs live in systems that do not talk to the rest of your finances.

The typical setup is three disconnected views. The equity administration portal knows your grants and vesting schedule but nothing about your life. Your brokerage knows the vested shares that landed but nothing about what is still coming. Your own mental model knows roughly what the total "should" be worth, updated whenever you happen to think about it. Nobody consolidates, so the single largest driver of your financial trajectory floats outside every decision you make.

The consequences are practical, not cosmetic. People negotiate offers without knowing what they are walking away from. They set savings goals that ignore their largest income stream. They discover their true exposure to their employer's stock only when the stock drops. [Knowing your real net worth](/blog/what-is-my-net-worth-the-founders-guide-to-total-wealth-intelligence) requires the equity to be in the picture — all of it, correctly labeled.

## Vested vs Unvested: The Distinction That Does the Work

Honest **RSU tracking** rests on one discipline: never let vested and unvested blur together.

**Vested shares are assets.** Once RSUs vest and shares are delivered, you own equity in a company. It belongs in your net worth at current value, exactly like shares you had bought — because economically, holding vested RSUs is identical to buying the stock today. That last point deserves a beat: every day you hold vested shares, you are making the same decision as someone spending cash to buy them. If you would not buy, that is information.

**Unvested grants are a schedule, not wealth.** Unvested RSUs are a contingent promise — contingent on your continued employment, and priced at whatever the shares are worth when they vest, not when they were granted. Counting unvested grants at today's price as present net worth overstates your position and, worse, anchors your lifestyle to money that can evaporate with a job change or a drawdown.

The right treatment is to track them separately but visibly: vested equity in your net worth, unvested grants as a forward schedule of planned events. That schedule is genuinely valuable — it is your income forecast — it just is not an asset yet.

**Private-company RSUs and options** add a layer: no liquid market, valuation set at funding events, and often a liquidity condition on top of the time condition. The same discipline applies with more humility about the number. Track the position at the latest credible valuation, label it clearly as illiquid, and never let a paper number drive a cash decision.

## Stock Compensation Is Concentration You Didn't Choose

A founder chooses concentration. An employee paid in RSUs accumulates it passively: every vest adds employer stock, and unless you sell deliberately, your exposure ratchets up quarter after quarter. Meanwhile your paycheck depends on the same company. Income and assets, correlated on one name — the classic risk cluster, assembled by default.

This is where consolidated tracking pays for itself. IOMI's Capital Efficiency Score — a 0–100 health check of how well capital is deployed — scores five factors: liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration. Employer equity feeds directly into the last two. A position that grew from 15% to 45% of net worth over two years of vests moves the score, and a moving score is much harder to ignore than a feeling. How the factors work is covered in [the Capital Efficiency Score guide](/blog/what-is-a-capital-efficiency-score-the-founders-guide-to-wealth-intelligence).

What you do about concentration is a personal policy question, and the useful move is to set the policy before each vest rather than re-litigating it after. Common patterns people adopt: sell a fixed share of each vest on arrival; hold everything until a target liquidity cushion exists, then sell overflow; or hold deliberately with a pre-committed exposure ceiling. IOMI does not recommend one — it makes the current number visible so your policy has something to bind to.

### Should unvested RSUs count in my net worth?

The disciplined answer is no — unvested grants are contingent on continued employment and future prices, so counting them as present wealth overstates your position. Track them as a forward schedule instead: visible, plannable, but clearly separated from assets you own today.

### How do I value private-company RSUs or options?

At the latest credible valuation — typically the most recent funding round — clearly labeled as illiquid. The number is an estimate by nature; the discipline is to keep it visible without letting a paper valuation drive cash decisions. IOMI tracks private equity as its own asset class for exactly this reason.

### Does IOMI handle the taxes on my stock compensation?

No. IOMI is not a tax engine — it does not track cost basis or tax lots and does not compute or optimize tax outcomes. Vesting and sales have real tax consequences; take your consolidated picture to a CPA. What IOMI provides is the accurate position and scenario arithmetic that make that conversation productive.

### How much employer stock is too much?

There is no universal number, but the two useful questions are: what share of your net worth is exposed to your employer (income included), and could your liquid assets carry you through losing both the job and a chunk of the stock value at once? The Capital Efficiency Score's concentration and risk balance factors put a moving number on the first question.

Yes — that is what the Simulator is for. It computes reallocations and planned cash-flow events deterministically from your actual holdings, so you can compare hold-everything against sell-a-fixed-share policies and see the resulting position, projection, and score before the vest date arrives.

Three things, all preparation rather than reaction: check what the vest will do to your concentration number, confirm your hold-or-sell policy still matches your situation, and make sure the event sits in your cash-flow forecast as a planned event.
