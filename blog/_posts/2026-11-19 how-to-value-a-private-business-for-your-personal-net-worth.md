# How to Value a Private Business for Your Personal Net Worth

Learning **how to value a private business** is unavoidable for any founder who wants an honest net worth number. For most business owners, the company is the single largest line on the balance sheet — and the only one without a price feed. Your brokerage account updates itself. Your house has comparable sales. Your company has whatever number you decide to write down, which is exactly why most founders either skip it entirely or carry a fantasy figure they would never defend out loud.

Both failure modes have the same cost: every downstream decision starts from a wrong picture. This guide covers the practical valuation methods that work for net worth tracking, when each one applies, the discounts founders forget, and — importantly — where self-serve valuation ends and professional work begins. The goal is not a number you could defend in a courtroom. It is a number you can defend to yourself, updated the same way every time.

## Key Takeaways

* For net worth tracking, consistency beats precision: a conservative method applied the same way every quarter is worth more than a sophisticated one applied once.
* The five workhorse methods are the last priced round, revenue multiples, earnings multiples, book value, and discounted cash flow — each fits a different kind of business.
* Apply discounts honestly: illiquidity, minority stakes, and key-person dependence all mean your share is worth less than the headline math suggests.
* Write down the method, the inputs, and the date every time you update — the documentation is what makes year-over-year changes meaningful.
* Formal valuations belong to professionals; they are the right tool for sales, buyouts, estate planning, and other material events, not for monthly tracking.

## Why Bother Valuing It at All?

A tempting position: the company is worth what someone pays for it, so carry it at zero until an exit. Conservative, defensible, and wrong in practice — because a net worth picture missing its largest asset produces bad decisions everywhere else.

Concentration is the clearest example. If your company is worth roughly four times your liquid assets, you are heavily concentrated, and choices about your public portfolio, cash buffer, and next investment should reflect that. Carrying the company at zero hides the concentration; carrying it at an inflated dream number exaggerates your wealth and licenses spending the balance sheet cannot support. Either distortion propagates into every scenario you model.

There is also a discipline benefit. Valuing the business on a schedule, with a written method, forces a founder to look at revenue quality, earnings, and dependence on themselves — the same lens a buyer will eventually apply. The number is a byproduct; the periodic honest look is the point. A complete picture of [what your net worth actually is](/blog/what-is-my-net-worth-the-founders-guide-to-total-wealth-intelligence) starts with putting a defensible number on the thing you spend most of your life building.

## Method 1: The Last Priced Round

If your company has raised equity from outside investors, the most recent priced round is the natural anchor: sophisticated parties negotiated a price with their own money at stake.

The mechanics are simple — your fully diluted ownership percentage times the post-money valuation — but three corrections matter before the output goes on your balance sheet:

**Preferences sit above you.** Investors typically hold preferred stock with liquidation preferences. In modest exit scenarios, preferences absorb value before common shareholders see anything, so common stock is worth less per share than preferred. The larger the preference stack relative to realistic exit values, the bigger the haircut on your common.

**Rounds go stale.** A valuation from three years ago describes a different company and possibly a different market. If the business has grown materially, the stale number understates; if the market has repriced the sector, it overstates. Either way, note the age of the anchor and adjust with judgment — or fall back to a fundamentals method below.

**Headline valuations embed terms.** A round with heavy structure — multiple preferences, ratchets, warrants — can carry a headline number well above what a clean price would have been. When the terms are exotic, treat the headline skeptically.

A reasonable convention for net worth purposes: ownership × last post-money, then a discount of 20–40% for the combination of preference stack and staleness, sized by honesty rather than optimism.

## Method 2: Revenue Multiples

For businesses without a priced round — most owner-operated companies — market multiples are the workhorse. The revenue multiple applies when the business is growing and revenue quality, not current profit, is the story.

The formula is annual revenue times a multiple typical for your sector and model. The entire game is choosing the multiple honestly, and revenue quality is what moves it:

* **Recurring versus one-off.** Contracted, recurring revenue commands multiples several times higher than project-based revenue that resets to zero each January.
* **Growth rate.** A business growing 50% a year and one shrinking 5% can have identical revenue and wildly different values.
* **Margin structure.** Software revenue and reselling revenue are different animals at the same top line.
* **Customer concentration.** If one client is 60% of revenue, a buyer prices the fragility, and so should you.

Where do multiples come from? Public comparables (adjusted heavily downward for size and liquidity), industry reports, and — most usefully for small businesses — marketplaces and broker data for actual sales of companies like yours. For net worth tracking, pick a multiple at the conservative end of the plausible range, write down why, and keep it stable until the facts change.

## Method 3: Earnings Multiples

For profitable, stable businesses — agencies, services firms, e-commerce, most main-street companies — earnings multiples fit better than revenue, because what a buyer is purchasing is a profit stream.

The standard base is SDE (seller's discretionary earnings) for owner-operated businesses — profit plus the owner's salary and discretionary expenses — or EBITDA for larger companies with management in place. Small owner-operated businesses commonly trade in the low single digits of SDE; larger, more transferable businesses command more. Three adjustments keep the number honest:

**Normalize the owner out.** If the business only produces its profit because you work sixty hours a week at below-market pay, subtract a market salary for your replacement before applying any multiple. What remains is the transferable profit stream.

**Average the cycle.** Use two or three years of earnings, not the best year in company history.

**Price the transferability.** A business that runs without you is worth several turns more than one that is really a well-paid job with your name on the door. Key-person dependence is the single largest and least-admitted discount in owner valuations.

## Methods 4 and 5: Book Value and DCF

Two more methods complete the toolkit, each with a narrow but real role.

**Book value** — assets minus liabilities on the company's balance sheet — is the floor. For asset-heavy businesses (equipment, inventory, property) it can be a meaningful number; for services and software companies it mostly ignores what makes the business valuable. Its best use in net worth tracking is as a sanity check: if your multiple-based estimate is below book, something is off in one of the two.

**Discounted cash flow** projects future cash flows and discounts them to present value. It is the theoretically correct method and the practically fragile one — small changes in growth or discount assumptions swing the output enormously, which makes it an excellent way for a founder to manufacture whatever number they wanted. For self-serve net worth purposes, treat DCF as a cross-check on a multiples-based estimate, not as the primary method, unless your business has unusually predictable contracted cash flows.

## The Discounts Founders Forget

Whatever method produces the headline number, three discounts stand between it and what your stake is actually worth to you.

**Illiquidity.** You cannot sell private shares this afternoon. A sale takes months to years, costs fees, and may not succeed at all. Professional valuations routinely apply discounts for lack of marketability in the 20–35% range; your net worth math should too. This connects to the broader planning point that [business equity is not cash](/blog/business-equity-is-not-cash-planning-around-illiquidity) — the number on the balance sheet and money you can spend are different things separated by a transaction that has not happened yet.

**Minority position.** If you hold 30% and cannot control distributions, a sale, or strategy, your 30% is worth less than 30% of the whole — buyers of minority stakes in private companies demand discounts for exactly this reason.

**Key-person dependence.** Already priced into a good earnings multiple, but worth naming twice because founders systematically underweight it. The honest test: if you disappeared for six months, what happens to revenue?

Stacked, these discounts commonly reduce a naive estimate by a third to a half. That feels punishing. It is also roughly what the market will say when you eventually test it, and a net worth number built on market-honest logic is the one worth tracking.

## Putting the Number on Your Balance Sheet

Method chosen, discounts applied — now make it operational. Four practices turn a one-off estimate into a tracked asset.

**Document the method.** Write down the formula, the inputs, and the date: "60% ownership × 4× trailing-twelve-month SDE of $400k, less 30% marketability discount = $672k, June 2026." Next quarter, update the inputs, not the philosophy.

**Update on a schedule, not on mood.** Quarterly or semi-annually is plenty. The failure mode is updating the valuation whenever you feel good about the business — which converts your net worth into a mood ring.

**Change the method only for a reason.** A priced round, a major shift in the business, an actual offer — these justify re-anchoring. Otherwise, consistency is what makes the year-over-year trend meaningful, and the trend is the useful signal.

## When to Hire a Professional

Everything above is for tracking — your own eyes, your own decisions. Some situations require a formal valuation by a credentialed professional, and self-serve math is the wrong tool:

* **Selling the business or a stake** — you need defensible numbers and, more importantly, negotiating support.
* **Buying out a partner or being bought out** — both sides need an independent number.
* **Estate and gift planning** — transfers of private equity have legal and tax dimensions where a formal appraisal is the required instrument; this is CPA and attorney territory.
* **Divorce or disputes** — courts want credentialed appraisals, not spreadsheets.
* **Issuing equity or options** — companies granting stock compensation need independent valuations performed to the applicable standards.

The two uses complement each other. Your tracked, documented estimate makes you a better consumer of professional valuations — you will understand the methods, question the assumptions, and notice when a number serves someone else's interests. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I value a private business for net worth purposes?

Pick the method that fits the business: last priced round (adjusted for preferences and staleness) if you have outside investors; a revenue multiple for growing companies; an earnings multiple on SDE or EBITDA for profitable stable ones. Apply honest discounts for illiquidity, minority position, and key-person dependence, document the formula and date, and update on a fixed schedule using the same method.

### What is the simplest formula to value a small business?

For an owner-operated profitable business: seller's discretionary earnings (profit plus your salary and discretionary expenses, minus the market cost of replacing yourself) times a sector-typical multiple — commonly low single digits for small businesses. Then subtract 20–35% for illiquidity. It is rough, but applied consistently it produces a trackable, defensible number.

### Should I include my business in my net worth at all?

Yes — at a conservative, documented value. Excluding it hides your true concentration and distorts every allocation decision; inflating it licenses spending your balance sheet cannot support. The useful discipline is carrying it at a number you could justify to a skeptical buyer, and separately tracking your liquid net worth so you never confuse paper wealth with spendable money.

### How often should I update my business valuation?

Quarterly or semi-annually for the inputs (revenue, earnings), and only re-anchor the method when something real happens — a priced round, an offer, a structural change in the business. More frequent updates add noise, not information, and updating on sentiment turns your net worth into a mood indicator.

### What discount should I apply for illiquidity?

Professional practice for lack-of-marketability discounts on private stakes commonly runs 20–35%, higher when a sale is especially unlikely or the ownership structure complicates it. Add further discounts for minority positions without control. If the stacked discounts feel harsh, that is usually a sign the undiscounted number was optimistic.

### When do I need a professional business valuation?

For any event with legal or transactional weight: selling the company or a stake, partner buyouts, estate and gift transfers, disputes, or issuing equity compensation. These require credentialed, defensible appraisals and coordination with your CPA and attorney. Self-serve valuation is for tracking and decision awareness, not for documents other parties will rely on.
