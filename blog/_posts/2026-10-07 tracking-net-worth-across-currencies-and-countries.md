# Tracking Net Worth Across Currencies and Countries

If your money lives in one country, tracking net worth is arithmetic. If it lives in three, it becomes a research project. A founder with a US brokerage account, a European bank account, a rental apartment priced in a third currency, and company equity in a Delaware entity does not have a net worth number — they have four partial numbers that were never true on the same day. A **multi-currency net worth tracker** exists to solve exactly this: one consolidated figure, in one base currency, built from accounts and assets that refuse to sit in the same place.

This is not a niche problem anymore. Remote founders incorporate where the investors are, live where the quality of life is, and bank wherever they happened to be when they opened the account. This guide covers what cross-border tracking actually requires, where spreadsheets fail at it, and how to build a picture you can trust enough to make decisions with.

## Key Takeaways

- Cross-border wealth fails silently in spreadsheets: stale exchange rates, forgotten accounts, and assets valued on different dates produce a net worth number that is confidently wrong.
- A real multi-currency net worth tracker needs three things: automated account syncing, a single base currency for consolidation, and coverage for assets that have no login — property abroad, private equity, collectibles.
- Currency is a risk dimension, not just a display preference. Knowing what share of your wealth is denominated in each currency is as important as knowing your asset allocation.
- Personal and business must stay separated across borders too: a foreign operating entity is its own compartment, not a line mixed into your personal accounts.

## Why Cross-Border Wealth Is So Hard to See

Three things break the moment your finances cross a border.

**Every number arrives in a different unit.** Your brokerage reports in dollars, your bank in euros, your property is priced in the local market's currency. Adding them requires conversion, conversion requires a rate, and the rate you used last quarter is not the rate today. A 5% currency move on a third of your net worth is a bigger swing than most people's monthly savings — and a manually maintained spreadsheet simply does not register it.

**Every asset updates on a different clock.** Bank balances change daily. Foreign property values change slowly and are only observable when you go looking. Private company equity changes at funding events. When you consolidate by hand, you are averaging together a Tuesday, a last-month, and a last-year, and calling the result "current."

**Accounts multiply and memory doesn't.** Anyone who has moved countries has at least one account they check twice a year. Small balances, old pensions, a broker you stopped using — individually trivial, collectively a real gap. Fragmentation is the default state of cross-border wealth, and the cost is not just inaccuracy; it is the low-grade anxiety of never being sure you have counted everything. Strip away the marketing and there are four requirements.

You need every asset and liability expressed in one currency of your choosing — usually the one you think and spend in. The moment you have one honest base-currency total, questions that used to take an afternoon ("what is my actual liquidity?") take a glance.

## Currency Exposure Is a Risk, Not a Formatting Question

Most people treat currency as a display setting. It is actually an allocation. If 60% of your net worth is denominated in one currency and you spend in another, you are running a large, unhedged position — whether you chose it or not.

A consolidated view makes this exposure visible for the first time. Once you can see it, three questions become answerable:

- **Matching:** Do my assets roughly match the currency of my future spending? Someone planning to buy property in Europe while holding only dollars is short their own future.
- **Concentration:** Is any single currency an outsized share of the total? Concentration is one of the five factors in IOMI's Capital Efficiency Score — a 0–100 health check of how well capital is deployed — because too much of anything, currency included, is a fragility.
- **Drag:** Is idle cash sitting in a currency and account where it earns nothing? Idle capital hiding in a secondary-country account is one of the most common findings when people consolidate for the first time.

None of this requires forecasting exchange rates, which nobody can do reliably. It requires knowing your exposure, which almost nobody actually does before they consolidate.

There is also a second-order benefit worth naming: liabilities denominated in another currency partially hedge assets in that same currency. A founder with a euro mortgage against a euro-priced apartment is far less exposed to the euro than the asset side alone suggests. This kind of natural offset is invisible when assets and debts live in different spreadsheets — and obvious the moment both sides of the balance sheet sit in one consolidated view. Seeing the netted exposure, rather than the gross one, is frequently the difference between an expensive overreaction and a shrug.

## The Cross-Border Founder's Setup, Step by Step

Here is a practical sequence for getting from scattered to consolidated.

**1. Inventory before you connect.** List every account, in every country, including the embarrassing dormant ones. The list itself usually surprises people.

**2. Choose your base currency deliberately.** Pick the currency you plan and spend in — not necessarily the one you earn in. Every consolidated number will be denominated in it, so choose once and stay consistent.

**3. Connect the connectable.** Link banks, brokerages, retirement accounts, and crypto exchange accounts and wallets through read-only connections. This is the bulk of the automation win.

**4. Add the unconnectable by what it is.** Foreign real estate goes in as real estate with a live valuation. Your stake in a foreign entity goes in as private equity / company equity. Physical assets and collectibles get their own entries. Liabilities too — a mortgage in another currency is one of the most overlooked exposures.

**5. Separate your entities.** Give each operating company its own compartment. Your consolidated view then means something: personal wealth, business wealth, and the total, each honest on its own.

**6. Let the system hold the picture.** From here, the tracker's job is to stay current on every sync while you check in briefly. ## What to Watch Once You're Consolidated

A **multi-currency net worth tracker** is not a set-and-forget purchase; it changes what you pay attention to. Three habits are worth building:

- **Read moves in base-currency terms.** When your net worth shifts, ask whether assets moved or the exchange rate did. The distinction changes the response: a falling asset might warrant action, a currency wobble usually warrants nothing.
- **Rebalance exposure on life events, not headlines.** Moving countries, changing where clients pay you, buying property — these justify revisiting currency allocation. A news cycle does not.
- **Re-check the perimeter yearly.** New accounts appear; old ones should be closed or connected. The perimeter of "everything I own" is the one thing no tracker can maintain for you.

The end state is simple to describe and rare to achieve: one number, current as of the latest sync, that includes every country, every currency, every entity, and every asset class you actually hold.
