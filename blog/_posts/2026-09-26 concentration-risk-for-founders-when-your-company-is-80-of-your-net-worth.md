# Concentration Risk for Founders: When Your Company Is 80% of Your Net Worth

Diversification is the one free lunch in finance, and founders skip it almost by definition. Building a company means pouring years of labor and most of your capital into a single private asset — and if it works even moderately well, that asset outgrows everything else you own. Waking up with 80% of your net worth in one illiquid position is not a planning failure. It is the natural end state of a successful founding journey. **Concentration risk for founders** is therefore not a question of whether, but of how much, how measured, and how managed.

This article treats concentration as the structural condition it is. Not "diversify immediately" — advice that is often impossible and sometimes wrong for a founder — but a clear-eyed framework: what concentration actually exposes you to, why founders systematically underestimate it, how to measure it continuously, and what deliberate management looks like when your options are constrained.

## Key Takeaways

- Founder concentration is triple-layered: the company is simultaneously your largest asset, your income source, and your daily occupation — one event can hit all three at once.
- The 80% number is common and often rational for a season; the risk is in not knowing the number, not necessarily in the number itself.
- Founders systematically understate concentration through stale valuations, invisible correlated holdings, and counting the company at its optimistic mark.
- Measurement is the manageable half: concentration should be a continuously tracked metric across your full consolidated balance sheet, not a guess at funding rounds.
- Managing concentration is about the trajectory of everything else — where new dollars go, how liquidity events are used — more than about selling what you often cannot sell.

## What Concentration Risk Actually Means for a Founder

Portfolio theory defines concentration risk simply: too much exposure to a single asset means a single adverse event can do outsized damage. For a public-market investor holding 40% in one stock, the fix is mechanical — sell some. For a founder, both the risk and the fix are different in kind, because founder concentration comes in three layers stacked on the same underlying asset.

Layer one: the asset. Company equity is the largest line on the balance sheet — often 70–90% for founders past product-market fit. It is illiquid, hard to hedge, and repriced in steps.

Layer two: the income. The same company most likely pays your salary or distributions. An event that impairs the equity usually impairs the income simultaneously — the two are one exposure wearing two coats.

Layer three: the occupation. Your time, skills, network, and identity are invested in the same place. A severe company event costs you your asset, your income, and your role in the same week.

This triple correlation is what makes founder concentration categorically different from a concentrated stock position. When a public-market investor's big holding drops 50%, their salary continues. When a founder's company hits a wall, everything moves together. The honest risk picture is not "one large position" but "one large position plus a correlated income stream plus a correlated career" — and the portion of your net worth that sits *outside* that correlation cluster is the part doing the actual diversifying. That outside portion is your [liquid net worth](/blog/liquid-vs-illiquid-net-worth-why-the-split-matters), and its size relative to burn is the single most protective number you control.

## Why Founders Underestimate Their Concentration

Almost every founder who measures concentration properly for the first time finds it higher than they assumed. The underestimation has consistent causes.

Stale and optimistic marks. Company equity is usually valued at the last funding round — a price set at a moment of maximum enthusiasm, possibly years ago, for preferred shares with rights common shareholders lack. Counting your common at the preferred mark overstates the position's realizable value while — paradoxically — the position's *share* of net worth is often understated in the other direction, because founders mentally shrink the big scary number.

Invisible correlated holdings. Concentration is exposure to a factor, not just to a ticker. The founder whose company serves one industry, whose angel checks went to companies in the same space, and whose vested options from a prior employer sit in an adjacent public company holds one correlated bet spread across four line items. Measured naively, it looks diversified. It is not.

Fragmented visibility. Most founders simply cannot see their full balance sheet in one place — accounts scattered across institutions, entities, exchanges, and spreadsheets. Without a consolidated denominator, the concentration numerator is a guess. This is the mundane, fixable cause, and fixing it is exactly the job of [asset consolidation](/blog/asset-consolidation-software-the-strategic-cockpit-for-founders-in-2026): every holding, every entity, one denominator.

Emotional accounting. The company does not feel like a "position." It feels like the mission. Positions get risk-managed; missions get believed in. Both attitudes have their place — but the balance sheet should be built by the risk manager, not the believer.

## Measuring Concentration Honestly

You cannot manage a number you do not have. The measurement discipline has three parts.

### Get the Denominator Right

Concentration is company value divided by total net worth — and the denominator must be complete. That means everything, consolidated: bank and wallet accounts, brokerage, retirement, crypto exchange accounts and wallets, real estate with live valuations, physical assets, liabilities, and every business entity. ### Get the Numerator Honest

Value the company position with two lenses, the way [the liquid/illiquid analysis](/blog/liquid-vs-illiquid-net-worth-why-the-split-matters) suggests for all illiquid assets: the official mark for total-net-worth purposes, and a conservative view for planning purposes. Some founders run the planning lens with the equity at half its mark, or even at zero, to see whether the rest of the balance sheet could survive alone. If the zero-case is unlivable, that fact is worth knowing while there is still time to change it.

## Managing Concentration When You Can't Just Sell

Standard concentration advice — trim the position — mostly does not apply. Founder equity often cannot be sold, should not be sold beyond a point (control, signaling, upside), or can be sold only at rare windows. Deliberate management therefore works the edges. None of what follows is a recommendation; it is the option space founders typically weigh, each option with trade-offs to evaluate with your own advisors.

Direct everything else away from the bet. The most available lever is the trajectory of new dollars. If the company already dominates the balance sheet, savings and distributions arguably work hardest when they build the uncorrelated side — liquid reserves, diversified public holdings, assets that do not rhyme with your company's fate. This is where the [next-dollar question](/blog/how-ai-answers-what-should-i-do-with-my-next-dollar) and concentration management become the same question: given the balance sheet's dominant fact, where does the marginal dollar do the most structural good?

Treat liquidity events as rebalancing moments. Distributions, profit shares, and — where they exist — secondary sales are the rare occasions when value crosses from the concentrated side to the free side. The founders who manage concentration well tend to run each event through an explicit allocation decision rather than letting proceeds pool as residue or, the opposite failure, reflexively recycling everything back into the bet.

Mind the correlation cluster. Angel investments in your own sector, industry-adjacent public stocks, even real estate in a company town — each adds to the factor bet. Seeing them tagged and totaled in one view is half the cure.

Size the survival floor. Decide the minimum uncorrelated cushion that makes the concentrated bet tolerable — months of burn the family can live on if the company had a terrible year — and build to it before optimizing anything else. What deliberate management does *not* require is resolving the concentration. Eighty percent may remain eighty percent for years, rationally. The difference between the managed and unmanaged version is that the managed founder knows the number, has stress-tested the floor, and steers every controllable dollar with the concentration in view.

## The Psychology: Betting on Yourself Without Lying to Yourself

Concentration risk management fails for founders less from ignorance than from identity. The bet on the company is a bet on yourself, and hedging it can feel like disloyalty to your own conviction.

The reframe that works: risk management is not doubt. Taking chips off the table when possible, building the uncorrelated floor, and tracking the number honestly are not statements about the company's prospects. They are acknowledgments that variance exists — that even excellent companies hit droughts, and that the version of you with twelve months of liquid runway makes better decisions inside the company than the version with two. A founder who cannot survive a bad year makes fear-driven calls; a founder with a floor negotiates, waits, and holds conviction cheaply. Paradoxically, the uncorrelated cushion is what lets you stay aggressive on the bet that matters.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is concentration risk for founders?

Concentration risk is outsized exposure to a single asset — for founders, their own company equity, which commonly represents 70–90% of net worth. Founder concentration is more severe than a concentrated stock position because it is triple-correlated: the company is usually the founder's largest asset, primary income source, and full-time occupation simultaneously, so one adverse event can hit all three at once.

### Is having 80% of net worth in my company always bad?

No — it is often the rational shape of a successful founding journey, especially early. The problem is not the number itself but not knowing it, not stress-testing it, and not steering the controllable remainder deliberately. A known 80% with a sized survival floor and new dollars building the uncorrelated side is a managed position. An unknown "most of it, probably" with residue cash and correlated side bets is the risky version.

### How do I measure my concentration accurately?

Divide your company position's value by your complete net worth — and the word "complete" is where most measurements fail. You need every asset and liability across personal accounts and all business entities in one consolidated view, valued currently. IOMI automates the denominator through read-only connections across 12,000+ institutions with all asset classes covered, and tracks concentration continuously as one of the five Capital Efficiency Score factors.

### At what value should I count my company equity?

Use two lenses. For total net worth, the recognized mark (typically the last round) is standard, though common shares may realistically be worth less than the preferred price. For planning, run a conservative case — equity at a significant haircut, or even zero — and check whether the rest of the balance sheet could carry your life. If the conservative case fails, that finding matters more than any precise valuation.

### What can I actually do about concentration if I can't sell shares?

Work the edges you control: direct new savings and distributions toward uncorrelated liquid assets rather than reinforcing the bet, treat every liquidity event as an explicit allocation decision, avoid stacking correlated side bets (same-sector angel checks, adjacent public stocks), and build a defined survival floor of liquid reserves. These are options to weigh with your own advisors — the common thread is steering the trajectory of everything that is not the company.

Yes.
