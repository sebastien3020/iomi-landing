# Wealth Management for Solopreneurs: A Solo Founder's Playbook

A solopreneur is a complete finance department with a headcount of one. You are the CEO who earns the money, the CFO who allocates it, the bookkeeper who reconciles it, and the household that spends it — with no colleague to catch what you miss. **Wealth management for solopreneurs** is therefore a different discipline than either employee personal finance or funded-startup finance: nobody is withholding your taxes, nobody is marking your equity, and nobody will ever schedule the review meeting for you.

The traditional answer — hire a wealth manager — fits solo founders worst of all. Your biggest asset is a business no advisor can manage, your income is too lumpy for their models, and a percentage-of-assets fee buys you quarterly attention you could give yourself weekly with better data. This playbook lays out the alternative: a lightweight, systematic setup a single person can actually run — clean separation, one scoreboard, runway math, and a review cadence measured in minutes.

## Key Takeaways

- Solopreneurs carry three jobs at once — earner, allocator, bookkeeper — and the failure mode is not bad decisions but decisions never consciously made: idle cash, accidental concentration, unexamined burn.
- Rule one is separation: the business is an entity with its own accounts and its own compartment, even at a headcount of one. Blurred boundaries make every downstream number a lie.
- One scoreboard beats many: a consolidated net worth across personal and business, with the Capital Efficiency Score as the single health read across liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration.
- The solo wealth stack is software plus per-project professionals — a flat-subscription platform for continuous oversight, a CPA for tax, an attorney when structure changes — not a percentage-fee relationship.
- The playbook runs on cadence: five minutes daily for awareness, thirty weekly for decisions, an hour monthly for direction. Small, scheduled, and sustainable by one person.

## The Solopreneur's Financial Position, Honestly Described

Start with what makes the solo founder's balance sheet structurally odd.

**The business is the portfolio.** For most solopreneurs, the business — its cash, its equipment, its value as a going concern — dwarfs every other asset. Yet it appears in no brokerage statement and no banking app. The single largest holding is invisible to every conventional tool, which is how solo founders end up "managing" the 30% of their wealth that happens to have a login page.

**Income and assets share one engine.** When an employee's employer struggles, their savings survive. When your business struggles, your income and your main asset fall together — and there is no second income in the household P&L to bridge the gap. This correlation is the central risk of solo entrepreneurship, and it is exactly the kind of risk that stays invisible until measured.

**Everything is lumpy.** Revenue arrives in bursts, taxes leave in quarterly chunks, and the smooth monthly rhythm every consumer finance product assumes simply does not exist. The salary-shaped tools fail quietly, and the solopreneur concludes — wrongly — that they are bad with money. They are not. They have been using instruments calibrated for someone else's life.

None of this is a reason for anxiety. It is a design brief. The system that fits it has four parts.

## Part One: Separate the Entity, Even at Headcount One

The most common solo-founder mistake is treating the business account as a slightly formal personal account — paying personal expenses from it, letting client payments pile up in it, moving money across the boundary by vibe. It feels harmless because you are both parties. It is not harmless: it makes your burn unknowable, your business's health unreadable, and your legal separation (if you have an LLC, the thing you formed it for) questionable. Your accountant will also bill you for the archaeology.

The fix is structural, then habitual. Structurally: business accounts for business, personal accounts for personal, and transfers between them as explicit, deliberate events — an owner's draw is a decision, not a leak. Habitually: the draw happens on a schedule, ideally as a fixed monthly amount from a buffer that absorbs the lumpy revenue, so your personal finances experience something like a salary even though the business never does.

## Part Three: Run Your Cash Like an Operator

Solopreneur cash management is runway management. The operator's toolkit, applied personally:

- **Know true burn.** Fixed personal costs plus fixed business costs plus the annualized share of irregular expenses. This number is the denominator of every safety calculation, and it is nearly always higher than the gut estimate.
- **Hold a deliberate buffer.** Enough that a bad quarter is an annoyance, not a forced sale — and not so much that idle capital drags on the score. The right size is discoverable, not guessable: model a lean six months and see where reserves trough.
- **Mechanize the tax set-aside.** Nobody withholds for you. A fixed share of every arrival moves to a reserve on arrival, and that reserve never counts as spendable. The percentages and estimates themselves are your CPA's domain — IOMI is not a tax engine — but the dates belong in your forecast as planned events so they never ambush you.
- **Forecast forward, conservatively.** IOMI's cash-flow forecast computes profile income minus expenses plus planned events over your actual balances. Model the recurring floor as dependable, variable revenue conservatively, and windfalls at zero until signed. The full method is in [cash flow forecasting for founders with irregular income](/blog/cash-flow-forecasting-for-founders-with-irregular-income).

When a real decision arrives — take the sabbatical, buy the apartment, raise your own pay — rehearse it first. The Simulator computes reallocations and planned events deterministically over your real holdings, the Projection shows the year-by-year path, and Goals track milestones against a status-quo trajectory. And when you just have a question — "how many months can I run if nothing lands until June?" — IOMI AI answers from your actual data, never from estimates when data is missing.

## Part Four: The Right Help, Bought the Right Way

Solo does not mean unadvised. It means buying expertise per problem instead of per year.

- **A CPA, absolutely.** Quarterly estimates, entity elections, deductions — this is specialist work with real stakes, and it is bought by the engagement, with far better inputs when you arrive holding a clean consolidated picture.
- **An attorney, occasionally.** When the structure changes: forming the LLC, signing the big contract, bringing on a partner.
- **A financial advisor, optionally and knowingly.** For a genuine liquidity event or estate complexity, a fee-for-service planner can earn their fee. What rarely makes sense for a solopreneur is the classic percentage-of-assets relationship — paying an annual slice of your wealth for reporting you now generate yourself and advice you consume twice a year. ### What is wealth management for solopreneurs?

The practice of running a complete financial system — business and personal — as a single person: separating the entity, consolidating everything into one net worth view, managing cash as runway, planning around irregular income, and buying professional expertise per problem rather than as a percentage-fee relationship. Software does the continuous oversight; you make the decisions.

### Do solopreneurs need a wealth manager?

Usually not in the traditional percentage-of-assets form. A solo founder's largest asset is a business no advisor can manage, and the reporting half of an advisor's value is now available as software for a flat fee. What solopreneurs genuinely need is a CPA, occasional legal counsel, and — around major liquidity or estate events — possibly a fee-for-service planner, hired knowingly for judgment rather than by default for reporting.

### How should I separate business and personal finances as a one-person company?

Separate accounts, always — and transfers across the boundary as scheduled, explicit events, ideally a fixed monthly owner's draw from a buffer that absorbs lumpy revenue. In IOMI the separation is structural: each business entity is its own compartment (up to 3 on the Business plan), personal is its own view, and the consolidated picture sits on top without mixing the two.
