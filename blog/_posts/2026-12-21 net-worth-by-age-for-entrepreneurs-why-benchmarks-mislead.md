# Net Worth by Age for Entrepreneurs: Why Benchmarks Mislead

Every founder has looked up a **net worth by age** table at least once, usually late at night, usually after a hard month. The tables are everywhere: median net worth at 30, recommended multiples of salary at 40, where the top decile sits at 50. And for entrepreneurs, nearly every number in them is a trap — not because the data is wrong, but because it describes a financial life you deliberately chose not to live.

Benchmark tables are built from populations of mostly salaried people, compounding steadily along smooth curves. Founder wealth does not move in curves. It moves in cliffs: years of flat or negative personal accumulation while a company absorbs everything, then step-changes when equity gets priced or sold. Comparing your cliff-shaped life against a curve-shaped table produces bad feelings and worse decisions in both directions. This article unpacks exactly why the comparison fails, what the tables can still legitimately tell you, and the five measures that actually track whether an entrepreneur is winning.

## Key Takeaways

* Age benchmarks are drawn from predominantly salaried populations; founders' wealth follows a step function that the averages structurally cannot describe.
* Median and average tell wildly different stories in skewed distributions — and entrepreneur outcomes are among the most skewed distributions in personal finance.
* A founder's benchmark number is also mostly unmeasurable by survey: private equity at self-reported values, spread across entities, minus what the tables assume.
* Comparing against benchmarks drives the two classic founder errors: despair-quitting during the flat years and overconfidence after a paper markup.
* The useful replacements are internal: your own trajectory, liquid runway, concentration, capital efficiency, and progress against goals you chose.

## Where the Tables Come From — and Who They Describe

The net worth by age numbers circulating online trace back to large household surveys — in the US, primarily the Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances — filtered through countless blog retellings. Understanding the source explains the mismatch.

These surveys measure households across an entire economy: every income level, every career type, every region. The resulting medians describe the center of a population in which business owners with meaningful equity are a small minority, and founders of growth companies a rounding error. The median 35-year-old household's balance sheet is a savings account, a retirement plan, some home equity, and debt. Yours is an operating company, entity cash, maybe property, and a large illiquid position nobody can price precisely — [including you](/blog/how-to-value-a-private-business-for-your-personal-net-worth).

Three statistical properties make the tables especially treacherous for entrepreneurs:

**Skew.** Wealth distributions have a long right tail, so averages run far above medians — and entrepreneur outcomes are more skewed still: many modest results, a few enormous ones. In a distribution like that, the "average" describes almost no actual person. Any table that doesn't say whether it reports mean or median is already misleading you.

**Survivorship in the comparisons you actually make.** The founders you measure yourself against are the visible ones — the exits, the raises, the announcements. The denominators — the quiet windings-down, the acqui-hires, the businesses that plateaued — do not post. Your informal benchmark is the tail of a skewed distribution wearing the costume of a peer group.

**Measurement failure at the exact spot founders live.** Surveys capture private business value as owner self-reports — the least reliable line on any balance sheet. The one asset class that dominates your net worth is the one the benchmark data measures worst.

## The Step Function Against the Curve

The deeper problem is not statistical — it is structural. Benchmarks assume wealth accumulates; founder wealth *converts*.

A salaried professional's net worth is a compounding curve: income arrives monthly, a fraction is saved, markets do their work. Age-based benchmarks are literally models of this curve — that is why they scale with age and salary multiples.

A founder's trajectory looks nothing like it. The typical shape: a long flat-or-declining phase, where salary is suppressed, savings go into the company, and measured net worth may be lower at 34 than it was at 29. Then discontinuities — a priced round marks up equity overnight; a secondary converts a slice to cash; an exit moves the number by more in one day than the previous decade combined. Between cliffs, the paper number drifts on estimates.

Judged against the curve at any single point, the founder is either failing (mid-flat-phase, "behind" the table by years) or absurdly ahead (post-markup, "ahead" by decades — on paper). Both readings are noise. The founder at 36 with modest liquid savings and a growing company is not behind the median 36-year-old; they are on a different trajectory whose value is concentrated in an unpriced position. The comparison is not unflattering — it is *undefined*, like ranking a marathoner mid-race against sprinters by current speed.

And this is where benchmark-chasing turns from useless to harmful: founders who feel "behind" make desperation moves — premature exits, bad terms, abandoning compounding positions for cosmetic liquidity. Founders who feel "ahead" after a markup spend paper wealth, under-reserve, and confuse valuation with money. The table produces exactly the two errors, in exactly the two phases.

## What the Tables Are Still Good For

Honesty requires the other side: population benchmarks are not worthless, and a founder who dismisses all external reference points has replaced one distortion with another.

**They calibrate the safety-net question.** Whatever your equity story, there is a version of the question worth asking at every age: if the company went to zero tomorrow, what would remain — and how does *that* number compare to a median life? Not because median is the goal, but because the answer measures the true downside of your current bet. A 45-year-old whose zero-scenario is an empty 401(k) and no home equity is carrying more risk than the same paper net worth suggests.

**They price the alternative path.** The tables approximate what the salaried counterfactual of you would have accumulated. That is genuinely useful once a decade or so: the founder path should be chosen, and periodically re-chosen, with a realistic view of what it costs while it is not paying.

**They anchor family conversations.** Partners and households reason from population norms — reasonably so. The tables give a shared external reference for conversations about risk and runway that otherwise run on vibes.

Used this way, benchmarks answer narrow questions about *downside and opportunity cost*. What they cannot answer is the question founders actually type into the search bar: *am I doing well?* That needs different instruments entirely.

## The Five Measures That Actually Track Founder Progress

Replace the external table with internal instruments — numbers that measure your position against your own trajectory and structure rather than a stranger's curve.

**1. Your own trend, honestly measured.** The only net worth comparison with signal is you-versus-you: the complete number — [everything you own, across personal and business](/blog/what-is-my-net-worth-the-founders-guide-to-total-wealth-intelligence), with equity at a documented conservative value — tracked with a consistent method over quarters and years. Direction and slope matter; the level, mostly, does not.

**2. Liquid runway.** Months of personal expenses covered by genuinely liquid assets. This is the founder's real safety metric — the number that determines whether you can survive the flat phase and negotiate from strength rather than need. It is also the number the benchmark tables silently assume and founders most often neglect.

**3. Concentration.** What percentage of net worth is the single largest position? Not because concentration is wrong — it is usually the strategy — but because the number should be *chosen and watched*, not discovered. Its trend tells you whether your risk is compounding faster than your diversification.

**4. Capital efficiency.** Of the capital you *can* control — the liquid and semi-liquid remainder — how well is it working? Idle cash, fee drag, risk balance: these are fully in your control during exactly the years when the equity number is not. ## How to Actually Answer "Am I on Track?"

Pull it together into a usable protocol — the one to run instead of the midnight table lookup.

**Quarterly, check the instruments:** complete net worth trend (same method as last quarter), liquid runway, concentration, efficiency score, distance to next milestone. Fifteen minutes with a live dashboard; an afternoon with spreadsheets, which is why the spreadsheet version stops happening.

**Annually, run the counterfactuals:** the zero scenario (company worthless — what remains, and can you live with it?), and the salaried counterfactual (is the founder path still worth its opportunity cost?). These are the two legitimate uses of population data — deliberately rare, deliberately scheduled.

**At every discontinuity, re-model rather than celebrate or panic.** A markup, a secondary offer, a downturn: each changes the shape of your balance sheet, and the right response is to simulate the paths forward from the new position — deterministically, from real holdings — not to re-rank yourself against a table that never described you.

Age is the least informative variable on a founder's balance sheet. Trajectory, runway, concentration, efficiency, and chosen milestones carry all the signal. Measure those, and the benchmark tables become what they always should have been: a mildly interesting description of a life you decided not to lead.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a good net worth by age?

For entrepreneurs, the question is close to unanswerable as posed — published tables describe predominantly salaried populations whose wealth compounds smoothly, while founder wealth moves in steps driven by equity events. The productive reframe: is your complete, honestly-valued net worth trending up under a consistent method; is your liquid runway adequate; and are you hitting milestones you chose? Those questions have answers; "good for 35" does not.

### Why do net worth benchmarks use median instead of average?

Because wealth distributions are heavily right-skewed, a small number of very large fortunes pull averages far above what a typical household holds; the median — the middle household — describes the population's center honestly. Any table that does not state which it reports is suspect. Entrepreneur outcomes are more skewed still, which is one reason even the median misleads as a founder reference point.

### Should founders even track net worth if the number is mostly paper?

Yes — the discipline is not the level but the method. Track the complete number with business equity at a documented, conservative value, and track liquid net worth beside it, so paper and spendable wealth never blur. The pair, trended over time under one consistent method, is the founder's honest progress measure; a single unexamined number, in either direction, is where the self-deception lives.

### How do I compare myself to other founders instead?

Mostly, don't — visible founder outcomes are the surviving tail of a skewed distribution, so the peer comparison inherits every distortion of the tables plus survivorship bias. Where founder norms genuinely help is on structure rather than outcomes: sensible runway, deliberate concentration, efficient deployment of controllable capital. You can be top-decile on those tonight, regardless of where your equity eventually prices.

### What should net worth look like before versus after a liquidity event?

Before: dominated by illiquid equity, thin liquid share — the correct posture is defensive on cash, deliberate on concentration, efficient with the remainder. After: the step-change converts risk into allocatable capital, and the job flips from protecting runway to deploying well. The wrong move is treating either state as a rank; both are phases, and each has its own instruments to watch.

It is the defining bet of the founder path — often correct, never neutral.
