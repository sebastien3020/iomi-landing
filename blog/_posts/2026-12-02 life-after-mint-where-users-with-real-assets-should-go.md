# Life After Mint: Where Users with Real Assets Should Go

When Intuit shut Mint down and pointed everyone at Credit Karma, millions of people went shopping for a **mint replacement** — and most of the advice they found answered the wrong question. The standard listicle assumes every former Mint user wants the same thing: a free-ish app that categorizes transactions and nags about the dining-out budget. For a large group of users, that is exactly right. But Mint also quietly served a different population: people who never cared much about the budgets and used it as a crude net worth dashboard — linking brokerage accounts, adding the house as a manual asset, watching the total. Those users have real assets, and a budgeting app is not their answer.

This guide is for the second group. If your finances now include a business or LLC, investment property, crypto on exchanges or in wallets, private equity, or just a brokerage portfolio that dwarfs your checking account, the replacement question is not "which budgeting app is prettiest." It is "what tool is actually built for a balance sheet like mine?" We will sort the landscape honestly — including where competitors genuinely beat IOMI — and give you a decision framework based on what your finances have become since you first signed up for Mint.

## Key Takeaways

* Mint served two populations: budgeters, and people using it as a makeshift net worth dashboard. The right replacement depends entirely on which one you were.
* If your core need is budgeting and spending control, Monarch is the strongest mainstream successor — better at budgets than IOMI, and we say so plainly.
* If your wealth now spans business entities, property, crypto, or private equity, budgeting apps structurally cannot represent it; you need a wealth platform, not a spending tracker.
* The dividing question: do you manage what you spend, or what you own? Spending tools optimize cash flow; wealth tools consolidate, score, and simulate the balance sheet.
* Whatever you pick, prefer read-only aggregation, flat pricing over percentage-of-assets models, and coverage for every asset class you actually hold.

## Why Mint's Death Created So Much Confusion

Mint's genius — and its curse — was being everything to everyone for free. It was primarily a budgeting product, and its categorization, alerts, and credit-score features served that identity. But because it aggregated accounts broadly, a substantial slice of users ignored the budgets entirely and treated it as a poor man's net worth tracker: link everything linkable, add manual assets for the rest, watch the number.

When it died, the replacement market split along that hidden fault line — but the coverage did not. "Best Mint alternatives" articles compared budgeting features, because Mint was filed under budgeting. Users whose actual usage was wealth tracking followed those recommendations into apps that do budgeting brilliantly and represent a business entity or a property portfolio poorly or not at all.

The years since have compounded the mismatch. The person who linked a checking account and a small 401(k) to Mint in 2012 may now own an LLC, a rental, some crypto, and RSUs from a tech job — or a company of their own. Finances grow up; the tool category has to grow up with them.

## First, the Honest Rundown of the Usual Suspects

Each of the popular Mint successors is genuinely good at something. The question is whether that something is your problem.

**Monarch** is the most complete mainstream successor: excellent budgeting, shared household finances, clean design, solid aggregation, and a team that ships. If your primary use of Mint was budgets, categories, and couple-level money coordination, Monarch is probably your answer and you can stop reading — genuinely. Where it thins out is exactly where this article's audience lives: business entities as separate compartments, private equity, asset-level classification, and analysis of how capital is deployed rather than spent. **YNAB** is the strongest tool ever built for zero-based budgeting discipline — every dollar gets a job, and its methodology genuinely changes spender behavior. It is also unapologetically not a net worth or investment platform.

**Empower Personal Dashboard** (the former Personal Capital) offers free aggregation with respectable investment views. The free tool exists to feed an advisory business — expect calls about wealth management services, which is a fine trade if you want an advisor and a distraction if you do not.

**Kubera** is the closest philosophical cousin to what this article recommends: a serious net worth tracker with broad asset coverage, including manual and alternative assets, with a clean design. **Spreadsheets** deserve their mention: infinitely flexible, free, and the default for many analytical people. Their cost is manual upkeep and staleness — the spreadsheet is accurate the weekend you rebuild it and decays thereafter.

## The Real Question: Do You Manage Spending, or Wealth?

Here is the dividing question that sorts every former Mint user in about ten seconds: **when you opened Mint, did you look at the budget screen or the net worth number?**

If you looked at budgets: your problem is cash flow. You want categorization, targets, alerts, and behavioral nudges. Monarch, Copilot, or YNAB will serve you better than IOMI will — IOMI is not a budgeting app and does not pretend to be.

If you looked at the net worth number: your problem is oversight. And the requirements list for oversight is completely different:

* **Every asset class, natively.** Bank and wallet accounts, brokerage, retirement, crypto exchange accounts and wallets, real estate with live valuations, private equity and company equity, collectibles, liabilities. Not "everything else goes in a manual line called Other."
* **Classification by what things are.** Crypto held at a broker is crypto; company stock in a brokerage account is concentrated equity. If the tool classifies by custodian, your risk picture is wrong.
* **Entity separation.** A business is not a checking account with a weird name. Personal and business must be tracked side by side without mixing — each entity its own compartment, consolidated view on top.
* **Analysis, not just totals.** A number going up is nice; knowing your idle cash, concentration, and drag is useful. This is the difference between tracking and intelligence, unpacked in [the modern net worth tracker guide](/blog/the-modern-net-worth-tracker-a-founders-guide-to-wealth-intelligence).
* **Forward motion.** What happens if I move $50k? When do I hit my number? Deterministic projection and simulation turn the dashboard from a mirror into an instrument.

Budgeting apps fail this list not because they are bad products but because they are built for the other problem. No amount of feature updates makes a spending tracker into a balance sheet system; the data model underneath is different.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best Mint replacement?

It depends on which Mint you used. For budgeting and household spending control, Monarch is the strongest mainstream successor, with Copilot and YNAB as excellent alternatives with different philosophies. For net worth oversight across real assets — businesses, property, crypto, private equity — a wealth platform like IOMI or a broad tracker like Kubera fits better, because budgeting apps cannot structurally represent that kind of balance sheet.

### Is IOMI a budgeting app like Mint was?

No. IOMI is a wealth intelligence platform: it consolidates everything you own read-only, separates personal and business entities, scores capital efficiency, and simulates financial moves. It does not do envelope budgets or spending targets — for that, Monarch is the better pick, and running both side by side is a reasonable stack since they solve different problems.

### What should former Mint users with a business use?

A tool with true entity separation. Mixing an LLC's operating cash into personal net worth corrupts both pictures — runway, distributions, and concentration all become unanswerable. IOMI tracks each business entity as its own compartment (up to 3 on the Business plan) beside personal wealth, with a consolidated view on top, which is a structural feature budgeting apps do not have.

### Can I track crypto in a Mint replacement?

Coverage varies widely. IOMI treats crypto as a native asset class — exchange accounts and wallets — and classifies holdings by what they are, so crypto held at a broker still counts as crypto in your allocation. If you hold meaningful crypto, test any candidate tool against your actual exchanges and wallets during a trial before committing.

### Is it safe to connect my accounts to a Mint alternative?

Check the access model. IOMI connects through Plaid in read-only mode: it can see balances and transactions, it cannot move money, and it never sees or stores your bank credentials. Read-only is the standard you should demand from any aggregator — a tool that can observe but not transact removes the most serious risk category.

### How much do Mint replacements cost?

Mint's free model was subsidized by ads and data; nearly all successors charge subscriptions, and pricing changes often enough that current numbers belong on each product's site. The structural question matters more than the monthly figure: prefer flat subscriptions over percentage-of-assets pricing. IOMI is flat — $29/month Personal, $79/month Business, with a free month to start and no credit card required.

### Why not just use a spreadsheet?

Spreadsheets are flexible and free, and for a static balance sheet they work. Their weakness is decay: every balance is stale the day after you update it, and the manual refresh ritual is exactly the chore that made people love Mint in the first place. Live aggregation plus automatic classification means the picture is current every time you look — and the analysis layer (scoring, projections, simulation) never fits in a spreadsheet anyway.
