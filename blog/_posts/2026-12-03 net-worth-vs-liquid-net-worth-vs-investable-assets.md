# Net Worth vs Liquid Net Worth vs Investable Assets

The **net worth vs liquid net worth** distinction sounds like accounting pedantry until the day it isn't. A founder with $6M of net worth discovers, mid-crisis, that only $180k of it can become cash this month. A family celebrating crossing "two million" realizes the number is mostly a house they live in and a company they can't sell. Same balance sheets, three very different numbers — and each number answers a different question.

This article defines the three measures precisely — total net worth, liquid net worth, and investable assets — shows how to compute each from a founder-shaped balance sheet, and, most importantly, maps each number to the decisions it should govern. Because the real failure mode isn't ignorance of the definitions; it's using the wrong number for the decision at hand. Runway decisions made on total net worth end badly. Ambition calibrated on liquid net worth alone undersells everything you've built. The fix is to know all three, live, and reach for the right one on purpose.

## Key Takeaways

* Total net worth = everything you own minus everything you owe. It measures wealth. It does not measure what you can spend, deploy, or survive on.
* Liquid net worth counts only what converts to cash quickly at fair value — after haircuts for taxes, penalties, and forced-sale discounts. It measures resilience.
* Investable assets sit between the two: capital that is or could readily be deployed into investments, excluding your home, operating businesses, and personal property. It measures deployable firepower.
* For founders, the three numbers diverge dramatically — 70–90% of total net worth is often illiquid — which makes the distinction more consequential than for almost anyone else.
* Each decision has a correct denominator: runway and emergencies use liquid; allocation and advisor conversations use investable; long-term progress and estate thinking use total.

## Total Net Worth: The Scoreboard

Total net worth is the complete inventory: every asset at fair value, minus every liability. Bank and wallet accounts, brokerage, retirement, crypto on exchanges and in wallets, real estate at live valuations, private and company equity, collectibles — minus mortgages, business debt where you're personally exposed, and every other obligation. The [full method for founders](/blog/how-to-calculate-your-net-worth-the-complete-founders-method) has its own guide; the short version is: everything counts, valued honestly, netted properly.

What the number is for: measuring long-term progress. Total net worth is the scoreboard that tells you whether the whole enterprise of your financial life is compounding. Tracked over years, it captures things no other number sees — the company's growing value, the property appreciation, the debt melting away.

What the number is *not* for: any decision involving time pressure. Total net worth includes assets that take months or years to convert to cash, and some (your operating business) whose conversion would end the very thing generating your income. Treating the scoreboard as a checkbook is the classic founder error, and it gets expensive precisely when things go wrong — which is when you can least afford it.

One honesty requirement: total net worth is only as good as its worst valuation. A private company marked at the last round's headline number, a house marked at a hopeful listing price, or [collectibles marked with wishful thinking](/blog/classic-cars-in-your-net-worth-valuation-without-wishful-thinking) all inflate the scoreboard without changing reality.

## Liquid Net Worth: The Survival Number

Liquid net worth answers a colder question: if you needed cash soon — weeks, not quarters — what could you actually raise at fair value?

Start from total net worth and remove everything that can't convert quickly: primary residence (you live there; selling takes months), private company equity (no market, or a market measured in quarters and permissions), direct real estate, collectibles, and typically most retirement accounts — early withdrawal usually triggers taxes and penalties that make them last-resort money, which is worth discussing with your CPA before ever counting on.

Then apply haircuts to what remains. Taxable brokerage converts fast, but selling appreciated positions triggers capital-gains consequences — the details are CPA territory; the principle is that $500k of positions is not $500k of cash. Crypto converts fast on liquid majors, less so on thin positions where your own sale moves the price. Cash converts at 100%.

A realistic founder example: $6.2M total net worth — company equity $4.2M, home equity $700k, retirement $350k, rental property $400k, brokerage $380k, cash $170k. Liquid net worth: cash plus haircut brokerage, roughly $480–500k. **Eight percent of the scoreboard.** That ratio — not either number alone — is the real finding. It says: resilient wealth on paper, four to five years of modest runway in practice, and any plan assuming otherwise is fiction.

What liquid net worth is for: emergency planning, personal runway, "how long could I fund the company/myself if revenue stopped," and honest [concentration-risk conversations](/blog/concentration-risk-for-founders-when-your-company-is-80-of-your-net-worth). It is the number that decides how much cash buffer you actually need — and it's the one number in the trio you should never round up.

## Investable Assets: The Deployment Number

Investable assets is the measure sitting between the two, and the one most people define sloppiest. It answers: how much capital do I have that is, or could readily be, deployed into investments?

The standard construction: cash and equivalents beyond your operating buffer, taxable brokerage, retirement accounts (invested, even if not touchable), crypto held as investment, and investment real estate by some definitions — minus your primary residence, operating businesses, and personal property. It differs from liquid net worth by *ignoring the time constraint*: retirement accounts count because they're deployed capital, even though they're not quickly spendable. It differs from total net worth by *excluding lifestyle and operating assets*: the house and the company aren't portfolio.

Why the number matters practically: it's the denominator for allocation. When you ask "what's my equity/fixed-income/crypto split?" the split only makes sense over investable assets — including the house would distort everything toward "real estate," and including the company would make every founder look insanely concentrated in "private equity" without saying anything actionable about the deployable portfolio. It's also the number the wealth-management industry quotes at you, because it approximates what they can manage; know it before any advisor conversation so the AUM fee math is transparent.

For allocation decisions — where should the next dollar go, is the deployable portfolio diversified, what would moving $100k change — investable assets is the working denominator. ## Why Founders Confuse the Three (and Pay for It)

For a salaried professional, the three numbers travel together — maybe 60/40/50 ratios of total. The gap is modest, so sloppiness is cheap. For founders, the numbers diverge violently: the company dominates total net worth, contributes zero to liquid, and sits outside investable. Sloppiness is expensive.

The classic errors, each mapped to a wrong denominator:

**Runway planned on total.** "I'm worth $6M, I can afford this burn rate." The burn is paid from liquid; the $6M mostly can't help. This error surfaces at the worst possible moment.

**Lifestyle scaled to paper.** Spending calibrated to the scoreboard rather than to liquid income and reserves — the mechanism behind wealthy-on-paper, fragile-in-practice.

**Diversification judged on investable alone.** The deployable portfolio looks beautifully balanced — while the company makes total net worth 75% one asset. Both frames are needed: investable for portfolio construction, total for the honest concentration picture.

**Buffers sized on the wrong base.** Cash reserves should key off liquid net worth and burn, not off total. A founder with high total and thin liquid needs a *larger* cash buffer than the scoreboard suggests, not a smaller one.

The pattern in every error: right number, wrong question. And the practical cause is almost always the same — the founder only ever *sees* one number, because their tracking produces one number.

## Which Number for Which Decision

The working map, in one place:

* **Emergency fund sizing, personal runway, "can I survive X months?"** → Liquid net worth, conservatively haircut.
* **Portfolio allocation, rebalancing, next-dollar decisions** → Investable assets.
* **Long-term progress, annual review, estate conversations** → Total net worth.
* **Concentration honesty** → Total (the company counts) *and* liquid (what's actually reachable) together.
* **Advisor conversations and fee math** → Investable assets — know it before they quote it.
* **Lifestyle and spending calibration** → Liquid income and reserves; never the scoreboard.

Ratios between the numbers deserve as much attention as the numbers themselves. Liquid-to-total below ~10% is common for founders and worth managing deliberately — through buffers sized honestly, and through planned steps (distributions, secondaries where available) rather than denial. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between net worth and liquid net worth?

Total net worth is everything you own minus everything you owe, including illiquid assets like your home, private company equity, and collectibles. Liquid net worth counts only what could convert to cash quickly at fair value — typically cash and taxable brokerage after haircuts — and excludes real estate, private equity, and usually retirement accounts. For founders, liquid net worth is often under 10–20% of total.

### What counts as investable assets?

Capital that is or could readily be deployed into investments: cash beyond operating buffers, taxable brokerage, retirement accounts, investment-held crypto, and by some definitions investment real estate. It excludes your primary residence, operating businesses, and personal property. It's the right denominator for allocation decisions and the number advisors typically mean when they ask about "assets."

### Should retirement accounts count as liquid net worth?

Generally no. Early withdrawal typically triggers taxes and penalties that make retirement funds last-resort money rather than accessible cash — the specifics vary by account type and situation, so talk to your CPA before counting on them. Retirement accounts do count in investable assets, since they're deployed capital, and in total net worth. That three-way treatment is a good illustration of why the measures differ.

### Why is liquid net worth so low for founders?

Because the dominant asset — the company — has no quick market, and much of the rest is often real estate and retirement. A founder at $6M total with $500k liquid is normal, not broken. The point isn't to force the ratio higher immediately; it's to know it, size cash buffers off the liquid number, and plan deliberate steps over time rather than discovering the gap during a crisis.

### What haircuts should I apply when calculating liquid net worth?

Cash: none. Taxable brokerage: reduce for the tax consequences of selling appreciated positions (your CPA can size this) and for market risk if you'd be selling into weakness. Crypto: liquid majors convert at close to market; thin positions deserve bigger discounts. Anything requiring a buyer to be found — property, private stakes, collectibles — belongs outside liquid entirely, not merely haircut.
