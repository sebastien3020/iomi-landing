# The Q4 Financial Review for Founders: 2026 Edition

A **Q4 financial review** is the one financial habit that separates founders who steer their wealth from founders who discover it once a year. By early November you have ten months of real data: what the business actually distributed, what your investments actually did, where cash actually accumulated. The last eight weeks of the year are your window to look at all of it while you can still act on some of it.

Most founders skip this review for a simple reason: assembling the picture takes longer than analyzing it. Personal accounts in one place, business accounts in another, the brokerage statement somewhere else, the property value a guess, the company equity a shrug. This guide gives you a structured review you can run in an afternoon — what to check, in what order, and which questions matter for someone whose balance sheet spans personal and business.

## Key Takeaways

* A Q4 review is an audit of position, not performance: what you own, where it sits, how liquid it is, and whether the allocation still matches your intentions.
* Founders need to review two balance sheets — personal and business — and the boundary between them, which is where most drift accumulates.
* The highest-yield checks are usually the boring ones: idle cash across entities, forgotten accounts, and concentration that grew silently all year.
* Deterministic simulation beats resolution-making: model the two or three moves you are considering before January, with real numbers.
* Software that consolidates everything continuously turns the Q4 review from a two-week spreadsheet project into an afternoon of actual decisions.

## Why Q4, and Why Founders Specifically

Salaried professionals can run a year-end review in an hour: one employer, one 401(k), maybe a brokerage account. A founder's review is structurally different. Your income arrived through entities and events, not paychecks. Your largest asset probably has no ticker symbol. Your cash is split across operating accounts, personal accounts, and places you have honestly stopped watching.

Q4 matters because it is the last window where the current year is still open. Distributions can still be timed. Retirement contributions for the year can still be made. Planned purchases can be accelerated or deferred. And several of these decisions have deadlines that involve your CPA — which means you want your numbers assembled in November, not during the December scramble when every professional you rely on is buried.

There is a second reason Q4 reviews earn their keep: drift. Over a year, allocations move without any decision being made. The business account accumulated more cash than the business needs. Your public portfolio grew or shrank relative to your company equity. A crypto position doubled and now represents more risk than you ever chose. None of these are mistakes — they are what happens when time passes. The review is where you notice.

## Step 1: Rebuild the Complete Balance Sheet

Start with the full inventory, and be strict about completeness. The review is only as good as the picture it starts from. That means:

* Bank and wallet accounts — personal and every entity
* Brokerage and trading accounts
* Retirement accounts
* Crypto — exchange accounts and wallets
* Real estate, at current market value rather than purchase price
* Private equity and company equity, at your documented estimate
* Physical assets and collectibles worth tracking
* Every liability: mortgages, business debt, credit lines

Two rules make this inventory useful rather than decorative. First, classify holdings by what they are, not where they sit — crypto held at a broker is still crypto, and a money-market fund inside a brokerage account is still cash-like. Second, keep personal and business compartmentalized. A consolidated number matters, but so does knowing which side of the wall each dollar lives on.

## Step 2: Audit Your Cash — All of It

Cash is where founders leak the most value with the least awareness. The audit has three questions.

**How much cash do you actually hold, everywhere?** Sum it across personal accounts, every entity's operating account, and cash-like positions inside brokerage accounts. Most founders who do this for the first time find meaningfully more cash than they expected — often spread across five or more accounts, each individually unremarkable.

**How much of it is earning nothing?** Idle capital is the classic silent cost. Money sitting in a 0% operating account for a year, while yield was available with equivalent safety, is a real loss that never shows up as a line item. Flag every account where the balance exceeds its working purpose.

**How much should each compartment hold?** The business needs an operating buffer sized to its expenses and volatility. You need a personal runway sized to your life. Everything above those two numbers is capital awaiting deployment — which is a decision, not a default. ## Step 3: Check Concentration Honestly

For founders, concentration is usually the strategy, not an accident — most of your net worth may sit in one private company, and that can be exactly right. The Q4 question is not "are you concentrated" but "do you know precisely how concentrated, and did it change this year?"

Compute three splits:

* **By asset:** what percentage of net worth is your single largest holding?
* **By liquidity:** what percentage could become cash within 30 days without a forced discount?
* **By entity:** how much of your wealth is inside structures you control versus personal accounts?

The liquidity split deserves particular attention in Q4. A founder can be a paper millionaire and unable to write a six-figure check. If your liquid share shrank this year — because the company grew, or because you deployed cash into illiquid positions — your risk profile changed even though you decided nothing. The [liquid versus illiquid net worth split](/blog/liquid-vs-illiquid-net-worth-why-the-split-matters) is the single most decision-relevant cut of a founder's balance sheet, and the year-end review is when to re-measure it.

## Step 4: Review the Boundary Between Personal and Business

The personal–business boundary is where drift concentrates, because money crosses it irregularly and both sides are usually tracked in different systems — or not at all.

Questions worth an honest pass:

* Did the business distribute what you planned this year, more, or less — and was the difference a decision or an accident?
* Are there personal expenses running through the business, or business expenses on personal cards, that blur the picture?
* Is the business holding cash that has no operating purpose, effectively functioning as your untracked savings account?
* If you run multiple entities, does each one's balance sheet make sense on its own?

## Step 5: Score the Year Against Your Goals

Now the comparison: where you are versus where you intended to be.

If you set a net worth target, a savings rate, or a milestone at the start of the year, measure against it plainly. If you did not, set the baseline now — the most valuable output of a first Q4 review is often just the number that next year's review will be compared against.

Three framings keep this honest:

**Measure the trajectory, not the snapshot.** One year's net worth change is noisy, especially when a private company valuation moves. What matters is whether the trajectory — contributions, deployment, drag — points where you want.

**Separate market effects from decisions.** If your net worth grew because public markets rose, that is weather. If it grew because you deployed idle cash, cut drag, or raised distributions and invested them, that is you. Score yourself on the second category.

**Turn vague goals into milestones.** "Grow net worth" is not reviewable. "Liquid net worth of $X by December 2027" is. IOMI's Goals feature tracks milestones against a status-quo trajectory, so next Q4 you are checking progress against a line, not a feeling.

## Step 6: Simulate Next Year's Big Moves Before January

The classic year-end failure is ending the review with resolutions instead of models. "I should diversify" and "we should distribute more" are moods. A useful Q4 closes with the two or three moves you are actually considering, run with real numbers.

Typical founder scenarios worth modeling in November:

* Distribute $150k from the business and deploy it into public markets — what does the five-year picture look like versus leaving it in the company?
* Pay down the property loan versus investing the same cash — what does each path do to net worth and liquidity?
* Take a partial secondary next year at an assumed valuation — what does the liquid/illiquid split become?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a Q4 financial review?

It is a structured year-end audit of your complete financial position: net worth across all assets and liabilities, cash levels across every account and entity, concentration and liquidity, progress against goals, and the moves worth modeling for next year. For founders it spans both personal and business balance sheets, because the boundary between them is where most unnoticed drift accumulates.

### When should founders do their year-end financial review?

Early-to-mid November is the sweet spot. You have ten months of real data, and enough of the year remains to act — timing distributions, making contributions, and getting on your CPA's calendar before the December rush. A review run in late December can observe the year but rarely change it.

### What should a founder check first in a Q4 financial review?

Cash. Sum it across every personal account, every entity, and cash-like positions inside brokerage accounts, then flag what is idle. It is the fastest check, it routinely surfaces the largest immediately-fixable inefficiency, and it requires no judgment calls about valuations — just visibility.

### How do I include my company equity in the review?

Use a documented, conservative estimate — the most recent priced round, a revenue or earnings multiple for a profitable business, or book value as a floor — and record the method and date so next year updates the same way. The review cares less about precision than consistency: a stable method makes year-over-year changes meaningful. For material decisions like a sale or estate planning, a professional valuation is the right tool.

### Do I need an advisor to run a year-end review?

Not for the review itself — it is a visibility and measurement exercise, and software now does the assembly continuously. Where professionals earn their place is in what the review surfaces: year-end tax coordination belongs with your CPA, and decisions like exit structuring or estate work belong with specialists. Arriving at those conversations with complete numbers makes them shorter and better.
