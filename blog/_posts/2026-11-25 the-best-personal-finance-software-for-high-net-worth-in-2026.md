## What Changes About Software Needs Above Seven Figures

Most finance apps are organized around a question — "where did my money go this month?" — that quietly stops being the important question as wealth grows. Three shifts drive the change.

**The balance sheet overtakes the income statement.** At $80k of net worth, spending discipline determines your trajectory. At $5M, allocation does: whether $800k is idle, whether 70% of your wealth sits in one private company, whether the real estate is marked to anything resembling reality. Software organized around transactions and categories is optimizing the small lever.

**Assets stop fitting the connectors.** High-net-worth portfolios are full of things that don't sync from a bank feed: private equity, LLC interests, direct real estate, collectibles, wallet-held crypto. A tool is only as good as its coverage of what you *actually own* — and for many wealthy users, the unsyncable assets are the majority of the picture.

**Structure appears.** Entities, holding companies, jointly-held property. The moment wealth is held through structures, "one big list of accounts" stops being an accurate model of your life. You need [personal and business tracked side by side without mixing](/blog/tracking-personal-vs-business-assets-the-founders-guide-to-total-oversight) — separation that most consumer apps simply have no concept of.

With those shifts in mind, here is the honest tour.

## Monarch: The Right Answer to a Different Question

Monarch is, for household budgeting and cash-flow management, genuinely excellent — arguably the best mainstream option since Mint's sunset. Clean design, solid account aggregation, flexible budgets and categories, good shared-finances support for couples, thoughtful recurring-transaction handling. If your core need is understanding and steering household spending, Monarch is the better pick, full stop — and no wealth platform should pretend otherwise.

The limits appear exactly where high-net-worth needs begin. Monarch's model of the world is accounts, transactions, and budgets — not entities, illiquid positions, and allocation analysis. Private company equity, multi-LLC structure, and concentration analysis are outside its design intent. It can hold manual assets, but it doesn't *reason* about them: there's no capital-efficiency lens, no deterministic scenario modeling of a reallocation, no business/personal compartments.

The practical takeaway: Monarch and a wealth platform aren't substitutes; they're different layers. Some founders run both — Monarch for household flow, a wealth layer for the balance sheet. The full comparison is at [IOMI vs. Monarch](https://iomifinance.com/compare/monarch).
