# Consolidating Alternative Assets in Your Net Worth

Ask most wealth apps what you are worth and they will answer confidently — about the third of your wealth they can see. Bank balances, brokerage accounts, maybe a retirement plan. Meanwhile the rental property, the stake in your own company, the crypto across two exchanges and a wallet, and the watch collection sit outside the frame entirely. For founders and operators, folding **alternative assets into net worth** is not a nice-to-have; it is the difference between knowing your financial position and knowing a rump of it.

Consolidation sounds like data entry, but it is really a set of judgment calls: what counts as an alternative asset, how each one gets valued honestly, how it gets classified, and how the whole picture stays current without becoming a part-time job. This article works through each call in order, ending with the structure that makes consolidated net worth genuinely useful rather than merely complete.

## Key Takeaways

* For most founders, alternative assets — real estate, private equity, crypto, collectibles — are the majority of net worth and the least tracked part of it.
* Every alternative asset needs three things recorded: a value, the basis for that value, and the date the basis was set.
* Classify assets by what they are, not where they sit — crypto at a brokerage is crypto, a REIT is real estate exposure.
* Net liabilities against their assets: a property is worth its value minus its mortgage, and leverage should be visible, not hidden.
* A consolidated net worth is only useful if it stays current — connect what can be connected, and put honest update rhythms on the rest.

## What Counts as an Alternative Asset

"Alternative" simply means anything outside the easily-tracked trio of bank accounts, public brokerage holdings, and retirement plans. For the IOMI-style balance sheet, the categories that matter:

* **Real estate** — primary home, rentals, land, fractional interests.
* **Private and company equity** — your own company's stock, angel investments, SAFEs, fund LP positions, options and RSUs in private companies.
* **Crypto** — exchange accounts and wallets.
* **Physical assets and collectibles** — watches, cars, art, wine, instruments.
* **Everything owed against the above** — mortgages, margin, loans secured by any of it.

Two observations change how seriously you take this list. First, proportions: for a typical founder, company equity plus real estate alone often exceeds everything sitting in bank and brokerage accounts combined. The "alternatives" are the estate; the tracked accounts are the petty cash. Second, behavior: these are precisely the assets that do not send statements, do not appear in apps, and therefore drift out of every picture you keep. The result is that most high-net-worth individuals systematically understate — or simply do not know — their own net worth.

The consolidation project is the correction. Done once, honestly, it usually produces the first true net worth number the person has ever seen.

## The Three-Field Rule: Value, Basis, Date

Everything in honest alternative-asset tracking follows from one rule: **no naked numbers.** Every asset carries three fields — a value, the basis for that value, and the date that basis was set.

A brokerage account needs only the value; the market supplies basis and date continuously. Alternative assets have no such feed, so the discipline must be explicit:

* *"Rental property: $840k — comparable-sales estimate — March 2026."*
* *"Company equity: $2.1M — Series B price less 30% discount — round closed Nov 2025."*
* *"Speedmaster: $6,500 — recent auction comps — June 2026."*

Why this matters: the basis tells you how much to trust the number, and the date tells you how stale that trust is. A net worth built from naked numbers converges on fiction — every figure was defensible when entered, none has aged visibly, and the total wears a precision it does not have. With basis and date attached, staleness announces itself, and your total becomes what it should be: a current, honest estimate with known soft spots.

This rule is also the antidote to the two classic self-deceptions: marking your company at the last round's headline price forever, and carrying collectibles at what you paid rather than what they would fetch.

## Valuing Each Class Honestly

The three-field rule sets the format; each asset class has its own honest way to fill it.

### Real estate

Use live market-based valuations where available — IOMI carries real estate with live valuations — and sanity-check against local comparables periodically. The number that goes into net worth is *equity*: value minus the mortgage against it. Carrying the gross value while the mortgage hides elsewhere in a liability list overstates the asset's contribution and disguises your leverage.

### Private and company equity

The hardest class, because there is no market and the temptation runs one direction. Defensible bases: the last priced round (with a self-imposed discount for illiquidity and preference-stack position), a 409A, or a conservative revenue multiple you can justify out loud. Update the mark only on real events — a new round, a secondary transaction, a material change in the business. And resist marking up between events; a stake that "feels" bigger has no place on a balance sheet.

### Crypto

Prices are live, so valuation is easy — the honest-tracking problem is completeness and classification. Exchange accounts connect like accounts; wallets need to be included too, not remembered occasionally. And crypto counts as crypto wherever it is held: coins sitting at a mainstream brokerage are crypto exposure, not "brokerage assets". Classification by nature, not location, is what keeps your risk numbers true.

### Physical assets and collectibles

Value at what the market would pay, evidenced by recent comparable sales — not at acquisition cost, and not at insured value (which measures replacement, not sale). Update on a stated rhythm — semiannually is plenty for most collections — and record acquisition cost separately so you can see appreciation without confusing it with value. We went deeper on collection-specific structure in [Collectibles portfolio tracker: 5 dashboard examples for founders](/blog/collectibles-portfolio-tracker-5-dashboard-examples-for-founders-in-2026).

### Liabilities against alternatives

Every borrow secured by an alternative asset belongs adjacent to that asset. Net equity per asset, gross leverage in total. Both numbers matter: the first for what you are worth, the second for how fragile the worth is.

## Classification: The Step Everyone Skips

Listing assets is inventory. Classifying them is where consolidation starts paying rent — because classification is what makes exposure computable.

The rule, again: **assets are what they are, not where they sit.** Applied consistently:

* Bitcoin at a broker → crypto.
* A REIT in your brokerage account → real estate exposure.
* A gold ETF → physical-asset exposure.
* Your angel check via a fund → private equity.

Without this discipline, a portfolio spread across four institutions can look diversified while being one concentrated bet in different wrappers. With it, you can finally answer the questions that consolidated net worth exists to answer: What is my true real estate exposure, direct and indirect? What fraction of my wealth is in assets I could not sell within a month? How much rides on one company?

That last set of questions is exposure analysis, and it is what separates a wealth platform from a longer list. Once every asset is classified and current, measures like the Capital Efficiency Score become possible — a 0–100 reading across liquidity, idle capital, drag, risk balance, and concentration that treats your alternatives as full citizens of the balance sheet rather than footnotes. Concentration in particular is meaningless without consolidated alternatives, since the concentrated position usually *is* an alternative: the company, the building, the token.

## What a Consolidated Net Worth Changes

The payoff of all this discipline is not the number. It is what the number makes possible:

**Decisions from the whole board.** Whether to buy the building, join the round, or sell the collection are portfolio questions, and they were previously being answered with a third of the portfolio invisible. Consolidation upgrades every one of these decisions at once.

**Trend over snapshot.** A single true reading is useful; a *history* of true readings is where insight lives. Which third of your wealth actually grew last year? Is the collection appreciating or just beloved? Did leverage creep?

**Exposure with nowhere to hide.** Concentration, illiquidity, and leverage become numbers you track rather than facts you rediscover during crises.

**Better conversations.** Advisors, lenders, and partners all work from your balance sheet. Handing over a current, basis-annotated picture instead of a shrug changes the quality of everything they do for you.

None of it requires precision beyond honesty. An estimate with a stated basis and a visible date is a fully respectable citizen of a balance sheet. What has no place there is the unmarked guess — and after consolidation, there are none left.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are alternative assets in a net worth context?

Everything outside easily-tracked bank, brokerage, and retirement accounts: real estate, private and company equity, crypto held on exchanges and in wallets, and physical assets like watches, cars, and art — plus the liabilities secured against them. For founders these are routinely the majority of net worth, which is why excluding them makes the number meaningless.

### How do I value private company equity in my net worth?

Use a defensible basis — the last priced round with a self-imposed illiquidity discount, a 409A valuation, or a conservative multiple you could justify aloud — and record the basis and its date alongside the value. Update only on real events like a new round or secondary sale. Never mark up between events on feel.

### Should I include my house and other property in net worth?

Yes, at market-based value minus the mortgage against it — net equity is what belongs in the total. Keep the liability visibly paired with the asset so your leverage stays legible. Whether you treat primary-home equity as spendable wealth is a planning judgment; whether it belongs in the picture is not.

### How often should alternative asset values be updated?

Match the rhythm to the asset: connected accounts and crypto update automatically on every sync; real estate rides on live valuations with periodic sanity checks; private equity updates on events only; collectibles semiannually against auction comparables. Recording the date with every mark makes overdue updates flag themselves.

### How do I track crypto that's spread across exchanges and wallets?

Connect exchange accounts like any other account, and include wallets explicitly rather than from memory. Then classify all of it as crypto regardless of where it sits — coins held at a mainstream brokerage are crypto exposure, not brokerage assets. Completeness and honest classification matter more than the live pricing, which is the easy part.
